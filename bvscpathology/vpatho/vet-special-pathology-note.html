Chapter 01

Classification of Viral Infection & Vesicular Diseases

Viral disease is first understood through how the virus injures the host cell, then applied to the classic vesicular disease group — FMD, Vesicular Stomatitis and Vesicular Exanthema — which are clinically identical but must be separated by host range and lab tests.

Types of viral-induced cell injury

  • Cytocidal effect — causes cell death/necrosis
    • Capsid proteins are toxic, block host protein/DNA/RNA synthesis
    • Examples: Herpes virus, picornavirus, parvovirus, adenovirus, flavivirus
  • Non-cytocidal effect — produces cytopathic effect (CPE) in two forms
    • Persistent infection — replication/release by budding without killing the cell (Arena virus, retrovirus)
    • Chronic infection — recovery from acute infection, virus remains intracellularly at low levels → infectious carriers (FMD), chronic disease (African swine fever), delayed disease (canine distemper)
  • Cell transformation (non-cytocidal) — oncogenic viruses transform normal cells to cancer cells
    • DNA viruses: Polyoma, papilloma, hepatitis B, herpes viruses
    • RNA viruses: Retrovirus/oncorna viruses

Slow viral infection — long incubation, progressive fatal disease (Lentivirus). Latent infection — viral genome persists without replication, reactivates under stress (Herpes virus).

Cytopathic effect (CPE) EXAM HOT

  • Characterised by inclusion bodies — aggregates of virions and viral protein, intranuclear or intracytoplasmic or both
    • Intranuclear (i/n): Herpes virus, adenovirus, parvovirus
    • Intracytoplasmic (i/c): Pox virus, paramyxovirus, reovirus, rhabdovirus
    • Both i/n and i/c: Canine distemper, measles
  • Syncytia formation — multinucleated cells with single cytoplasm (Herpes virus, paramyxovirus)

Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) EXAM HOT

Synonyms: Aphthous fever, Aftosa, Enzootic aphthiae. Acute, highly contagious febrile disease of cloven-footed animals with vesicles in mouth and feet.

  • Etiology — Picornavirus, genus Aphthovirus. Serotypes O, A, C, SAT1, SAT2, SAT3, Asia 1 (O, A, C, Asia 1 prevalent in India)
  • Transmission — ingestion of contaminated material; virus is epitheliotropic
  • Pathogenesis — invades epithelial cells → vesicle formation → viraemia via lymph/blood → epithelium of mucous membranes, interdigital space, tongue, gums; myocardial degeneration in calves
  • Clinical signs — high temperature, vesicles in mouth rupturing to ulcers, vesicles on feet causing lameness, lesions on udder/teat, smacking of tongue, drooling
  • Gross lesions — vesicles on lips, tongue, palate, coronet, udder, teat; also rumen, reticulum, omasum; haemorrhage/oedema of abomasum and small intestine; "Tigroid heart" in calves from myocardial degeneration
  • Microscopic lesions — vacuoles in epithelium with eosinophilic cytoplasm and pyknotic nuclei coalescing into vesicles; hyaline degeneration/necrosis of myocardial and skeletal fibres ("tigroid" pattern); secondary bacterial osteomyelitis/arthritis
  • Sequelae — mastitis, anaemia, sexual dysfunction, delayed growth; infertility from thyroid/endocrine damage
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs, complement fixation test, virus isolation, PCR

Vesicular Stomatitis

Synonym: Sore mouth of cattle and horse. Caused by Rhabdovirus; limited to the western hemisphere; three antigenic strains — New Jersey, Indiana, Trinidad.

  • Susceptibility — horse, cattle, pig (goat and sheep resistant)
  • Transmission — vectors (mosquitoes, black flies, gnats, midges); seasonal
  • Clinical/gross lesions — cattle: vesicles on tongue, lips, dental pad, buccal mucosa causing salivation/anorexia; horses: dorsum of tongue/lips only; swine: snout or feet with lameness. No myocardial lesions, very low mortality
  • Microscopic lesions — intercellular oedema of stratum spinosum, cell dissociation/necrosis, neutrophilic infiltration, erosions; heals in 7–10 days
  • Diagnosis — cannot be produced in cattle by intramuscular inoculation (lingual route only) — this differentiates it from FMD, which CAN be produced intramuscularly

Vesicular Exanthema

Acute, febrile, non-fatal disease of pigs only caused by Calicivirus. First reported 1935, California. Transmitted by ingestion. Pathogenesis resembles FMD (viraemia 72–84 hrs, localises in buccal mucosa and skin above hooves). Lesions: vesicles on snout, nose, tongue, lips, gums, interdigital space, coronary band, teat, udder; abortion in sows. Diagnosis: CFT, virus neutralization, AGPT.

Chapter recap
  • Cell injury — cytocidal (kills), non-cytocidal (persistent/chronic), or transformation (oncogenic).
  • Inclusion bodies — i/n: herpes, adeno, parvo; i/c: pox, paramyxo, reo, rhabdo; both: CD, measles.
  • FMD — Picornavirus (Aphthovirus), cloven-footed animals, "Tigroid heart" in calves.
  • Vesicular stomatitis — Rhabdovirus; cannot be reproduced in cattle by IM route (differs from FMD).
  • Vesicular exanthema — Calicivirus, pigs only.
  • Key differentiator — host range table separates the three "look-alike" vesicular diseases.
Chapter 02

Morbillivirus Diseases — Rinderpest, PPR & Canine Distemper

The Morbillivirus genus (Paramyxoviridae) produces three antigenically-related, lymphotropic and epitheliotropic diseases across very different hosts — cattle (Rinderpest), small ruminants (PPR) and dogs (Canine Distemper) — all sharing leucopenia and lymphoid necrosis as a signature.

Rinderpest (Cattle Plague) EXAM HOT

  • Etiology — Morbillivirus, antigenically related to canine distemper, PPR and measles viruses
  • Incidence — historically the foremost cause of cattle death in Africa/Asia; not reported in the Indian subcontinent since June 1995; led to the founding of the first Veterinary College (Lyons, France, 1762)
  • Susceptibility — cattle, buffaloes (also sheep, goat, pig, deer, wild bovids); 100% mortality in exotic breeds, 20–50% in indigenous breeds
  • Pathogenesis — inhaled droplets → epithelium of upper respiratory tract → tonsils/regional lymph nodes → viraemia in mononuclear cells → high affinity for lymphoid tissue and alimentary mucosa → destruction of lymphocytes → marked leucopenia; death from dehydration or secondary infection due to immunosuppression
  • Clinical signs — fever 104–105°F, dry muzzle, constipation then "shooting diarrhoea" with foetid odour, photophobia, excessive salivation, marked leucopenia
  • Gross lesions — necrotic foci/haemorrhagic streaks in abomasum (pyloric region); swollen ulcerated Peyer's patches; "Zebra marking" — linear haemorrhages on rectal mucosal folds (pathognomonic); liver shows chronic passive congestion; purulent conjunctivitis with corneal ulceration
  • Microscopic lesions — ulcers, haemorrhage, necrosis with multinucleated cells; eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions in mucosal epithelium (intranuclear rare)
  • Diagnosis — CFT, AGPT, virus isolation, cDNA probes (differentiate from PPR), PCR

Peste-des-Petits-Ruminants (PPR, Goat Plague)

Synonyms: Plague of small ruminants, erosive stomatitis, stomatitis–pneumoenteritis complex.

  • Etiology — Morbillivirus. First reported 1942 (Africa); India — first in sheep, Villupuram district, Tamil Nadu, 1989
  • Susceptibility — more severe in goats than sheep; fatal in young animals
  • Pathogenesis — retro-pharyngeal mucosa → viraemia → alimentary, respiratory, lymphoid systems; marked lymphoid necrosis
  • Clinical signs — Acute form (resembles Rinderpest in cattle): high fever, sneezing, serous→mucopurulent ocular/nasal discharge, leucopenia, diphtheritic oral plaques, halitosis, diarrhoea, dyspnoea, death within a week, abortion
  • Subacute form — more common in sheep, milder, recovery in 10–14 days with lasting immunity
  • Gross lesions — oral/pharyngeal/oesophageal erosions; "Zebra stripes" at ileo-caecal junction, colon, rectum; enlarged haemorrhagic retropharyngeal/mesenteric lymph nodes; fibrinous bronchopneumonia with secondary bacterial infection
  • Microscopic lesions — syncytia in stratified squamous epithelium, intracytoplasmic and intranuclear eosinophilic inclusions
  • Diagnosis — pneumonia distinguishes PPR from Rinderpest (absent in Rinderpest); AGID/CIE, immunocapture sandwich ELISA, RT-PCR

Canine Distemper (CD) EXAM HOT

Synonym: Carré's disease, hard pad disease. Caused by Paramyxovirus.

  • Susceptibility — dogs, foxes, jackals, ferrets, wild Canidae; Baltic seals (phocid virus, identical to CD)
  • Transmission — ingestion/inhalation; nearly always complicated by secondary bacteria (Bordetella bronchiseptica — pneumonia; Salmonella spp. — GI lesions)
  • Pathogenesis — pantropic virus: entry → local lymph node proliferation → viraemia → skin, GI, urinary, biliary tract, brain vascular endothelium → old-dog encephalitis via demyelination
  • Clinical signs — diphasic fever (initial viral fever 3–4 days, normal to day 11–12, second fever from secondary bacterial infection); nasal discharge, conjunctivitis, bronchitis, pneumonia, diarrhoea; pustules on ventral abdomen; hyperkeratosis of digital pads ("hard pad"); keratitis/blindness; nervous signs — epilepsy, chewing fits, myoclonus, torticollis, nystagmus, convulsions
  • Gross lesions — catarrhal/purulent nasal-pharyngeal exudate; interstitial pneumonia (pure CD) or purulent bronchopneumonia (secondary infection); "nutmeg" liver; hydropericardium; meningeal hyperaemia
  • Microscopic lesions — demyelination (affinity for myelinated CNS); perivascular cuffing, gliosis with gemistocytes, neuronophagia; round/oval acidophilic intracytoplasmic or intranuclear inclusion bodies in respiratory/GI/urogenital epithelium and CNS neurons/microglia; cytoplasmic inclusions in circulating neutrophils/erythrocytes are diagnostically important
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs, inclusion bodies, IFT on corneal smear, animal inoculation
Chapter recap
  • Morbillivirus trio — Rinderpest (cattle), PPR (goat/sheep), Canine Distemper (dogs) — antigenically related.
  • Rinderpest — "Zebra marking" on rectum; India rinderpest-free since June 1995.
  • PPR vs Rinderpest — pneumonia is the key differentiator, present only in PPR.
  • Canine distemper — diphasic fever, "hard pad" hyperkeratosis, demyelinating "old dog encephalitis."
  • Shared theme — all three cause marked lymphoid necrosis / leucopenia.
Chapter 03

Herpesvirus & Pestivirus Systemic Diseases — MCF, BVD-MD & IBR

Three important cattle systemic viral diseases are grouped here because they present overlapping catarrhal/mucosal signs and are classic differential diagnoses of each other and of Rinderpest: Malignant Catarrhal Fever (Gamma-herpesvirus), BVD-Mucosal Disease (Pestivirus) and Infectious Bovine Rhinotracheitis (Bovine herpesvirus-1).

Malignant Catarrhal Fever (MCF)

Synonyms: Bovine malignant catarrh, Snotsiekte. Etiology: Gamma-herpesvirus (lymphotrophic).

  • Transmission — Africa: wild wildebeest carries virus to cattle; US: sheep carry virus; not transmissible cattle-to-cattle
  • Pathogenesis — virus is lymphotropic/cell-associated (no free virus detectable in tissue); main features are lymphocytic proliferation and infiltration with vasculitis
  • Clinical signs — high fever 106–107°F, catarrhal conjunctivitis/rhinitis with mucopurulent discharge, corneal opacity, emaciation, erosions of muzzle/mouth, diarrhoea, later nervous signs; swollen lymph nodes form a chain around the jugular groove
  • Gross lesions — granular swollen lymph nodes; erosions of nasal/oral/pharyngeal mucosa; erosions/ulceration in abomasum; mottled liver/kidney with infarct-like renal foci; "cooked" appearance of brain
  • Microscopic lesions — perivascular/intramural mononuclear infiltration with medial necrosis of arteries; meningeal oedema and perivascular cuffing in medulla, pons, hippocampus, cerebrum, cerebellum, spinal grey matter; no inclusion bodies or syncytia
  • Diagnosis — ELISA, serum neutralization, IFT; differentiate from Rinderpest by prominent intestinal lesions

Bovine Viral Diarrhoea (BVD) & Mucosal Disease

  • Etiology — antigenically related Pestiviruses; two biotypes — cytopathogenic and non-cytopathogenic
  • Incidence — BVD first reported New York State 1946; Mucosal disease, Canada 1946 (very high mortality)
  • Transmission — direct contact, transplacental, nasal secretions/faeces/urine/tears/milk/semen, persistently infected/viraemic animals
  • Susceptibility — 6 months to 2 years cattle
  • Clinical signs — fever 105–108°F, anorexia, diarrhoea, stringy mucus hanging from muzzle, leucopenia, oral/nasal ulcers, cold ears/muzzle/extremities; abortion, mummification; calves born alive are persistently infected with congenital cerebellar hypoplasia, cataracts, retinal atrophy, microphthalmia
  • Gross lesions — ulceration of dental pad, palate, tongue, cheeks, muzzle; irregular pharyngeal ulcers; oesophageal/abomasal erosions; ulcers on Peyer's patches; lymphocyte necrosis in lymph nodes/spleen; chronic mucosal disease → chronic oral/skin ulceration and lameness
  • Diagnosis — cross serum neutralization test differentiates from Rinderpest; MCF spreads slowly with ocular/nasal/brain signs; virus isolation, ELISA

Infectious Bovine Rhinotracheitis (IBR) EXAM HOT

Synonyms: Infectious pustular vulvovaginitis, coital exanthema, vesicular venereal disease. Etiology: Bovine herpesvirus type 1 (BHV-1), survives up to a year in frozen semen.

  • Pathogenesis — respiratory entry → rhinitis/laryngitis/tracheitis → deciliation impairs defence → spreads via lacrimal ducts to conjunctiva; via trigeminal nerve to brain (non-suppurative encephalitis); via peripheral leucocytes to placenta/foetus (abortion)
  • Respiratory form — young cattle, morbidity 100%/mortality 10%, fever, mucus discharge, dyspnoea, conjunctivitis; gross: congested oedematous tracheal mucosa with mucopurulent exudate, tracheal stenosis; microscopic: necrosis with neutrophilic/mononuclear infiltration, intranuclear inclusion bodies
  • Neonatal form — very young calves, generalised necrosis of respiratory epithelium, liver, kidney, spleen, lymph node, oesophagus, forestomach with intranuclear inclusions
  • Genital form — signs 24–72 hrs after coitus; reddened vulval mucosa with dark red punctate foci, vesicles/pustules → membrane → ulceration; bulls show similar penile/preputial lesions; heals in 2 weeks but recurs
  • Abortion — up to 60% of pregnant cows in a herd, third trimester; focal necrosis in foetal liver/spleen/kidney; marked postmortem autolysis is a striking finding
  • Diagnosis — necrotising lesions with intranuclear inclusions; ELISA, IFT, IPT, EM; virus neutralization, passive haemagglutination
Chapter recap
  • MCF — Gamma-herpesvirus, lymphotropic, vasculitis, no inclusion bodies; differentiate from Rinderpest by intestinal lesions.
  • BVD-MD — Pestivirus, two biotypes; persistently infected calves are the diagnostic hallmark.
  • IBR — BHV-1; four forms (respiratory, neonatal, genital, abortion), intranuclear inclusions throughout.
  • Differential triad — Rinderpest, MCF and BVD-MD are the classic look-alikes; the intestinal/ocular/persistent-infection pattern separates them.
Chapter 04

Orbivirus Diseases — Blue Tongue & African Horse Sickness

Both diseases in this chapter are caused by Orbiviruses (family Reoviridae) transmitted by Culicoides biting midges, and both attack blood vessels causing oedema and haemorrhage — Blue Tongue in sheep, African Horse Sickness in equines.

Blue Tongue EXAM HOT

Synonym: Catarrhal fever of sheep, Sore muzzle of sheep. First recognised South Africa 1902; endemic in India.

  • Etiology — arthropod-borne Orbivirus, family Reoviridae
  • Transmission — Culicoides biting midges; also via semen of infected bulls
  • Susceptibility — sheep most susceptible; also goat, cattle, deer
  • Pathogenesis — virus entry → replication in haematopoietic cells → viraemia → replication in endothelial cells → swelling/degeneration/necrosis → oedema, haemorrhage, thrombosis, infarction; haematopoietic effect gives anaemia and leucopenia
  • Clinical signs — high fever, reddened nasal/oral mucosa, dyspnoea, salivation, watery→crusting nasal discharge, bluish/cyanotic swollen tongue ("Blue Tongue")
  • Gross lesions — oedema of lips/nose/ears/intermandibular space; petechiae on oral/nasal mucosa; gangrene; coronet haemorrhage at skin-hoof junction causing lameness; haemorrhages in abomasum/intestine/myocardium; serosanguinous pericardial fluid; fatty liver; congested spleen
  • Pathognomonic lesion — haemorrhages at the base of the pulmonary artery and aorta
  • Microscopic lesions — loss of striation and hyalinisation of muscle; haemorrhage, oedema, necrosis
  • Diagnosis — clinical (blue tongue, coronet haemorrhage), gross lesion at pulmonary artery/aorta base, egg inoculation/lamb inoculation, virus neutralization

African Horse Sickness (AHS)

Synonym: Equine plague. Etiology: double-stranded RNA Orbivirus. Originally South African; worst Indian outbreak 1960. Transmitted by Culicoides.

  • Susceptibility — horses, mules, donkeys
  • Pathogenesis — viscerotropic virus, found in all tissues and fluids

Four clinical forms

  • Acute pulmonary form (Dunkop) — fever, dyspnoea, coughing, frothy nasal discharge (pulmonary oedema), profuse sweating, death
  • Subacute cardiac form (Dikkop) — progressive fever, oedema of lips/eyelids/neck/chest, swollen cyanotic tongue with petechiae, oesophageal paralysis (dysphagia), cardiac failure with pulmonary oedema, hydropericardium, endocarditis, death
  • Mixed form — both pulmonary and cardiac forms present
  • Mild form — no signs or mild fever, anorexia, dyspnoea, mild conjunctivitis
  • Lesions — pulmonary form: hydrothorax, frothy exudate in bronchi/trachea/pharynx/nasal passages; cardiac form: hydropericardium, ascites, myocardial haemorrhage/necrosis, congested GI mucosa, enlarged congested liver, haemorrhagic lymph nodes with lymphocyte depletion, oesophageal paralysis from pharyngeal oedema
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs/lesions; intracerebral mouse inoculation with neutralization test
Chapter recap
  • Shared vector — both spread by Culicoides midges; Orbivirus, Reoviridae.
  • Blue Tongue — cyanotic swollen tongue; pathognomonic haemorrhage at pulmonary artery/aorta base; coronet lameness.
  • AHS — four forms — Dunkop (pulmonary), Dikkop (cardiac), mixed, mild.
  • Both — vascular endothelial damage → oedema/haemorrhage is the core lesion mechanism.
Chapter 05

Equine Viral Diseases — EIA, Equine Influenza, EVA, Rhinopneumonitis & Encephalomyelitis

Horses carry a distinctive set of viral diseases: a persistent lentivirus infection (Equine Infectious Anaemia), two respiratory viruses (Influenza, Herpesvirus pneumonitis), a vasculitis-causing Togavirus (Equine Viral Arteritis) and a mosquito-borne encephalitis.

Equine Infectious Anaemia (EIA) EXAM HOT

Synonyms: Swamp fever, Equine malarial fever. Etiology: Lentivirus, shares serological reactivity with human AIDS lentivirus; persists in blood for life. First recorded in India 1987 (Bangalore stud farm).

  • Transmission — mechanical, by biting insects (mosquitoes, Tabanus, Stomoxys); iatrogenic via unsterile needles; NOT by contact; more prevalent in swampy (insect-breeding) areas
  • Pathogenesis — virus localises in spleen/liver/kidney/lymph nodes; persistent viraemia but low level except during clinical attacks (most infective then); infects macrophages → destruction → antigen-antibody complexes → fever, glomerulitis, complement depletion; antibody-coated erythrocytes → increased osmotic fragility and erythrophagocytosis → anaemia; rapid antigenic variation lets the virus evade neutralisation

  • Microscopic lesions — reticulo-endothelial hyperplasia in all organs; immunoglobulin/complement deposits in renal glomeruli (acute); myocardial hyaline degeneration and lymphocytic infiltration, haemosiderin-laden macrophages in liver (subacute); normocytic normochromic anaemia throughout
  • Diagnosis — Coggins' agar immunodiffusion test (1972) for antibodies; immunofluorescence for tissue antigen; virus isolation in leucocyte culture

Equine Influenza

Etiology: Type A influenza virus, two serotypes (A/Equi-1, 1958 Czechoslovakia; A/Equi-2, 1963 Miami). First recorded India 1987. Morbidity 80–100%, mortality 0.1%.

  • Susceptibility — all ages; horses ≤2 years most susceptible
  • Pathogenesis — inhaled virus multiplies in upper respiratory epithelium → erythema, oedema, focal erosions; brief mild viraemia; deciliation predisposes to secondary bacterial infection
  • Clinical signs — incubation 2–3 days; fever 101–106°F; dry-to-moist cough for 1–3 weeks; watery nasal discharge; deaths in foals from pneumonia
  • Lesions — erosions of nose/pharynx/larynx/trachea; lungs show peribronchitis, periarteritis, bronchopneumonia
  • Diagnosis — CFT, haemagglutination test, serum neutralization, ELISA, immunostaining

Equine Viral Pneumonitis

Etiology: Equine herpesvirus type 4. Susceptible age: 1–4 years.

  • Clinical signs — fever, congested nasal/conjunctival mucosa, dry hacking cough, recovery in 2–4 days, occasional "roaring" (recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis)
  • Gross lesions — haemorrhagic or purulent bronchopneumonia, disseminated abscesses
  • Microscopic lesions — intranuclear inclusions in respiratory epithelium, purulent rhinitis/tracheitis/pneumonia

Equine Viral Arteritis (EVA)

Synonyms: Pink eye, Rhinopneumonitis. Etiology: Toga virus (related to corona virus). First reported USA 1957.

  • Transmission — droplet inhalation, ingestion, venereal (carrier stallions shed for up to 2 years); high viral load in aborted foetal tissue/fluid
  • Pathogenesis — viraemia damages small arteries of intestine, visceral lymph nodes, adrenals → haemorrhagic enteritis, abdominal pain; pulmonary oedema/pleural effusion → dyspnoea; abortion from necrotising myometritis
  • Clinical signs — fever 102–106°F, purulent nasal discharge, conjunctivitis, cough/dyspnoea, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, jaundice, limb oedema, scrotal/preputial oedema in stallions, abortion
  • Gross lesions — congestion/oedema/petechiae of conjunctiva, nasal mucosa, pharynx, guttural pouches; subcutaneous oedema; hydrothorax; oedema at heart base and mediastinum; hydropericardium, ascites; adrenal cortical haemorrhage
  • Microscopic lesions — severe arterial change in submucosa of caecum/colon: arterial media replaced by homogenous eosinophilic material, nuclear/cytoplasmic displacement, adventitial cell infiltration
  • Diagnosis — microscopic lesions, CFT, serum neutralization, ELISA

Equine Encephalomyelitis

Synonym: Blind staggers. Etiology: Arbovirus — Eastern, Western, Venezuelan strains.

  • Transmission — Western strain via Culex (birds as reservoir); Eastern strain via Aedes; seasonal
  • Pathogenesis — insect bite → viraemia (transient in Eastern/Western, persistent in Venezuelan) → virus enters brain → neuronal damage
  • Clinical signs — loss of awareness, aimless wandering, unresponsiveness, violent collisions with objects, pyrexia, excitement/mania, blindness/circling, tremors, paralysis, stupor, recumbency, cessation of feeding/micturition, subnormal temperature, death
  • Gross lesions — hyperaemia/oedema of brain and cord; enlarged oedematous lymph nodes
  • Microscopic lesions — disseminated non-suppurative encephalitis; tigrolysis, chromatolysis, karyorrhexis, gliosis, neuronophagia; perivascular cuffing extending into white matter; vasculitis of cord
  • Diagnosis — guinea pig inoculation (paralysis in 3–5 days), chick embryo inoculation (death 18–24 hr), CFT, neutralization test
Chapter recap
  • EIA — Lentivirus, lifelong viraemia, Coggins' test; three clinical stages driven by immune-complex disease.
  • Equine influenza — Type A, two serotypes, high morbidity/very low mortality.
  • Equine viral pneumonitis — EHV-4, occasional "roaring."
  • EVA — Toga virus, arterial media replaced by eosinophilic material; venereal spread by carrier stallions.
  • Equine encephalomyelitis — Arbovirus, mosquito-borne, neuronophagia is the classic microscopic sign.
Chapter 06

Lentivirus Diseases of Small Ruminants — CAE, Visna-Maedi & Jaagsiekte

These three diseases of sheep and goats share a slow, persistent, immune-mediated or neoplastic character driven by retroviruses that replicate in macrophages or pneumocytes.

Caprine Arthritis-Encephalitis (CAE)

First reported USA 1974. Etiology: Lentivirus (related to Maedi-Visna virus).

  • Transmission — colostrum/milk, contact
  • Pathogenesis — infects monocyte-macrophage lineage cells; virus shed as monocytes mature to macrophages; lymphoproliferative lesions; antigenic variation prevents neutralisation
  • Encephalitis form — young goats 1–4 months: difficulty abducting hind limbs, ataxia, four-limb paralysis, torticollis, mild fever; lesions: white matter demyelination, perivascular cuffing
  • Arthritic form — chronic, slow, all joints affected, carpal/stifle swelling; lesions: proliferative synovitis, extensive lymphocytic infiltration, interstitial pneumonia, lymphoid hyperplasia
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs/lesions/serology, AGID, ELISA/dot-ELISA, virus isolation

Visna-Maedi

Synonyms: Ovine progressive pneumonia, lymphoid interstitial pneumonia, chronic viral encephalomyelitis of sheep. Visna (1935) and Maedi (1939) first reported in Iceland — two clinical manifestations of the same viral infection.

  • Transmission — respiratory route; infected mononuclear cells in colostrum/milk cross intestinal mucosa of lambs
  • Pathogenesis — replication in macrophages → cell-associated viraemia → dissemination to brain and other organs; concurrent Jaagsiekte (which raises pulmonary macrophage numbers) facilitates spread; antigenic variation outpaces slow antibody response
  • Clinical signs — long incubation (2–3 years), only adults, listlessness, weight loss, dyspnoea/coughing/nasal discharge over 3–10 months, firm enlarged udder in advanced disease
  • Visna lesions — demyelination with destruction of periventricular white matter of cerebellum/cerebrum, gliosis, lymphocytic meningeal infiltration, pleocytosis of CSF
  • Maedi lesions — lungs fail to collapse, dense rubbery grey, distended 2–5x normal, dry cut surface; microscopically thickened alveolar walls (mononuclear infiltration, fibroblast proliferation), bronchiolar lymphatic nodules, blue-staining intracytoplasmic macrophage inclusions (Giemsa), follicular hyperplasia, synovitis
  • Diagnosis — differentiate from Jaagsiekte

Jaagsiekte EXAM HOT

Synonyms: Ovine pulmonary adenoma/adenomatosis, pulmonary carcinoma of sheep. First reported South Africa. Etiology: Type D oncovirus (a retrovirus) — a genuinely neoplastic viral disease.

  • Transmission — droplet infection, close contact
  • Pathogenesis — replication in type II pneumocytes and Clara cells of terminal bronchioles → cell transformation → intra-alveolar/intra-bronchiolar polypoid ingrowths → copious mucus and surfactant-like protein → macrophage accumulation → death by anoxia
  • Clinical signs — incubation 1–3 years; affects 3–4-year-olds; coughing/panting after exercise, emaciation, dyspnoea, profuse watery nasal discharge, no fever, death 6 weeks–4 months; pasteurellosis is a common complication
  • Lesions — multiple foci of neoplastic alveolar type II cells in acinar/papillary patterns — adenocarcinoma of the lung; mononuclear/neutrophil infiltration of bronchial lymph nodes
  • Diagnosis — "wheelbarrow test" (hold sheep up by hind legs — watery mucus runs from nostrils); histopathology; PCR
Chapter recap
  • CAE — Lentivirus; encephalitis in kids, arthritis in adult goats.
  • Visna-Maedi — same virus, two syndromes: Visna = CNS demyelination, Maedi = interstitial pneumonia (lungs don't collapse).
  • Jaagsiekte — Type D oncovirus, true lung adenocarcinoma; wheelbarrow test is pathognomonic clinical sign.
  • Overlap — Jaagsiekte-associated macrophage increase can facilitate Maedi spread; the two must be differentiated.

Swine Viral Diseases — Swine Fever, Swine Influenza & Aujeszky's Disease

Pigs host three economically important systemic/neurotropic viral diseases: a haemorrhagic Pestivirus disease (Swine Fever), a respiratory orthomyxovirus (Swine Influenza) and a pantropic herpesvirus with a fatal pruritic/neurological course (Aujeszky's).

Swine Fever (Hog Cholera) EXAM HOT

Synonym: Hog cholera, swine plague. First recognised 1885 (USA), viral aetiology established 1903.

  • Etiology — Pestivirus, Togaviridae, RNA virus. Incubation 1–4 days (artificial), 7 days (natural)
  • Susceptibility — the pig is the only domestic animal naturally infected
  • Transmission — mainly ingestion, also inhalation, conjunctiva, nasal mucosa
  • Pathogenesis — virus multiplies in tonsillar tissue → blood → fever, erythematous skin patches, enteritis
  • Clinical signs — cerebral signs — fits, wobbling gait, howling, coma
  • Gross lesions — necrotic enteritis with spherical "button ulcers" in caecum/colon mucosa; erythematous patches on ventral abdomen/thorax, ears, thigh, perineum, snout; kidney: subcapsular haemorrhages, "turkey egg" appearance from petechiae; splenic infarcts; swollen red lymph nodes
  • Microscopic lesions — haemorrhage in Bowman's space and interstitium; button ulcer forms from infarction after arteriolar occlusion by swollen hydropic endothelium; splenic infarcts; disseminated non-purulent meningoencephalomyelitis; croupous pneumonia
  • Diagnosis — symptoms/lesions, AGPT, fluorescent antibody technique

Swine Influenza

Etiology: Type A influenza virus, acting in combination with the gram-negative bacterium Haemophilus influenzae suis; closely related to human influenza A; H1N1 strain found in pig tissue at slaughter; arose from adaptation of the 1918 human pandemic virus.

  • Susceptibility — young pigs
  • Transmission — potentially zoonotic (pig↔human); droplet inhalation, direct contact; provoked by presence of H. influenzae suis; swine lungworm can act as intermediate host
  • Pathogenesis — primarily upper respiratory tract/trachea/bronchi; secondary lung lesions from exudate drainage; enzootic pneumonia; secondary Pasteurella multocida pneumonia
  • Clinical signs — incubation 24–48 hrs; fever, rhinitis, cough, inappetence subsiding in 3–5 days (transitory recurrence possible within 3 weeks); dyspnoea, pneumonia; mortality ~1%, morbidity up to 100%
  • Gross lesions — mucopurulent tracheal/bronchial exudate; plugging causes sharply demarcated atelectasis; peribronchial consolidation
  • Microscopic lesions — necrosis of alveolar/bronchial/tracheal lining; hyaline membrane formation lining alveolar sacs; congestion, focal haemorrhage, perivascular/intralobular oedema, mononuclear infiltration
  • Diagnosis — elevated neutralising/anti-haemagglutinin antibody titres, virus isolation, immunostaining

Aujeszky's Disease

Synonyms: Pseudorabies, infectious bulbar paralysis, mad itch. First described 1902, Hungary (Aujeszky). Etiology: Herpesvirus, pantropic, affects many species (pig, cattle, dog, cat, sheep, rat).

  • Susceptibility — pigs and rodents are natural hosts; man also susceptible
  • Transmission — snout rubbing on abraded skin, intranasal infection; incubation ~7 days
  • Pathogenesis — affects all embryonic layers; travels via peripheral nerves to spinal cord causing local muscle/fascial necrosis; ganglia show neuronal degeneration and glial proliferation; produces pruritus and non-purulent encephalomyelitis
  • Clinical signs — cattle — biting/kicking with maniacal behaviour, paralysis, pyrexia, death in 6–48 hrs, sometimes sudden death; young pigs usually affected
  • Clinical signs — swine — paralysis, incoordination, convulsions, tremors, vomiting; pruritus is NOT seen in pigs (unlike other species — hence "mad itch" applies elsewhere); death within 12 hrs in young pigs
  • Gross lesions — inflammation, pulmonary oedema, pneumonia, laryngitis, gastroenteritis, myocarditis; necrosis of spleen and liver
  • Microscopic lesions — intranuclear inclusion bodies in degenerated cerebral cortical neurons of pigs; non-purulent encephalitis with perivascular cuffing; meningitis
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs/lesions; subcutaneous inoculation of oedematous fluid/spinal cord/brain into rabbits causes pruritus within 48 hrs; FAT
Chapter recap
  • Swine fever — Pestivirus, pig-only, "button ulcers," "turkey-egg" kidney.
  • Swine influenza — Type A + H. influenzae suis; zoonotic potential; high morbidity, low mortality.
  • Aujeszky's — pantropic Herpesvirus; pruritus is classic in most species but ABSENT in pigs; rabbit inoculation test for diagnosis.

Rabies

Synonym: Lyssa, Hydrophobia, Rage. Acute viral infection of man and warm-blooded animals characterised by abnormal behaviour, nervous disturbances, ascending paralysis, impaired consciousness and death. Etiology: RNA virus, family Rhabdoviridae.

Susceptibility, incidence & transmission

  • Extremely susceptible — dog, fox, wolf, jackal, cat, mongoose, bat
  • Moderately susceptible — cattle, goat, sheep, horse
  • Cattle and equine are dead-end hosts; vampire bats act as a vector
  • Incubation period depends on bite site — the nearer to the head, the shorter (2 weeks – 9 months)
  • Transmission — bite of affected animal; virus in saliva can be spread by flies; licking of wounds; haematogenous spread; transplacental spread possible in an affected bitch

Pathogenesis

  • Deposited in wound after bite → local replication in epithelial cells/myocytes → crosses neuromuscular spindles → moves centripetally through nerves → reaches CNS
  • Parasitizes ganglion cells, spreads centrifugally through nerves; great affinity for salivary glands via facial (VII) and glossopharyngeal (IX) nerves → excreted in saliva
  • In CNS: damages nerve cells and vascular endothelium → irritation → increased excitability (furious form) → medullary neuron damage (fever, polyuria, glycosuria) → neuronal degeneration → paralysis of deglutition muscles (drooling), jaw muscles (dropped jaw), respiratory muscles (asphyxia, death)

Clinical signs

  • Furious form — excitement, rage, indiscriminate biting/barking at imaginary objects, chewing inanimate objects, vacant look, champing of jaws, dribbling saliva, altered voice
  • Dumb/paralytic form — sluggish, progressive weakness, vacant look, does not obey/recognise family, hides; death 3–4 days after onset (within 10 days); horse — colic; cattle — bellowing; cat — furious form only; rabbit — dumb form only; bull/ram may show increased sexual urge

Gross & microscopic lesions

  • Gross — limited to CNS: hyperaemia, oedema with meningeal petechiae
  • Microscopic — non-suppurative encephalitis; perivascular lymphocytic cuffing; microglial proliferation forming "Babes nodules"; satellitosis around ganglion cells; neuronophagia in hippocampus/brain stem; ganglioneuritis of paravertebral ganglion; acute catarrh of respiratory/digestive tract; hyperaemia of kidney/spleen/liver/salivary gland
  • Negri bodies EXAM HOT — intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies in neurons; round/oval, acidophilic (magenta red) with basophilic granules; found in hippocampus (dog impression smear) and Purkinje cells of cerebellum (cattle impression smear)

Diagnosis

  • Impression smear of hippocampus stained by Seller's or modified Mann's or Williams' modification of van Gieson's method
  • Habel's mouse inoculation test — ammon's horn emulsion (1:20, antibiotic-treated) injected intracerebrally into 6 mice; one sacrificed on day 5, 6, 7 and brain examined for Negri bodies (usually seen day 5–6)
  • Rabbit intracranial inoculation for confirmation
  • Corneal/saliva test (FAT), Fluorescent Antibody Test, Immunoperoxidase Test, Complement Fixation Test
Chapter recap
  • Street vs fixed virus — street has long incubation + Negri bodies present; fixed is rabbit-passaged, short/fixed incubation, no Negri bodies.
  • Pathogenesis — centripetal spread to CNS, then centrifugal spread via facial/glossopharyngeal nerves to salivary glands.
  • Two forms — furious (aggression) vs dumb/paralytic (dropped jaw, quiet).
  • Negri bodies — pathognomonic intracytoplasmic inclusions; "Babes nodules" from microglial proliferation.
  • Diagnosis — Habel's mouse inoculation test is the classic named test.

Canine & Feline Viral Diseases — ICH, Parvovirus, Panleucopenia & FIP

Small-animal practice is dominated by four viral diseases: an adenovirus hepatitis (ICH), a parvovirus enteritis shared in concept across species, its feline counterpart (Panleucopenia) and a fatal immune-mediated coronavirus disease (FIP).

Infectious Canine Hepatitis (ICH) EXAM HOT

Synonym: Rubarth's disease, hepatitis contagiosa canis. Fully described by Rubarth, 1947. Etiology: Canine Adenovirus 1 (DNA virus).

  • Susceptibility — mainly young dogs; also foxes
  • Transmission — urine, faeces, saliva (naso-oral route; initial pharyngitis/tonsillitis); ingestion, inhalation
  • Pathogenesis — infects tonsils/Peyer's patches → viraemia (affinity for endothelial, mesothelial, hepatic cells) → liver, kidney, spleen, lungs as main targets; antigen-antibody immune complexes cause glomerulonephritis and corneal opacity — "Blue eye" syndrome
  • Clinical signs — peracute: death in 12–24 hrs with slight icterus, conjunctivitis, swollen tonsils; acute: anorexia, fever, subcutaneous oedema of head/neck/abdomen, vomiting/bloody diarrhoea, seizures, icteric membranes, keratoconjunctivitis with corneal opacity, swollen tonsils, albuminuria
  • Gross lesions — haemorrhages of stomach, intestine, meninges, cord, heart (endothelial damage + loss of hepatic clotting factors); enlarged oedematous spleen/lymph nodes; oedematous thickened gall bladder — pathognomonic lesion; enlarged congested liver
  • Microscopic lesions — periportal necrosis; swollen eosinophilic hepatocytes losing outline, replaced by dilated sinusoids; disrupted hepatic cords; swollen endothelial cells with pyknotic nuclei; pancreatitis, glomerulonephritis, acute splenitis, lymphadenitis, mild non-purulent encephalitis; large basophilic intranuclear inclusion bodies in endothelial cells of spleen, glomerulus, brain, hepatocytes, mesothelial cells
  • Diagnosis — difficult clinically; inclusion bodies; severe hypoglycaemia (hepatic injury); CFT, neutralization test

Canine Parvovirus Infection

Reported 1978; similar to feline panleucopenia virus but does not replicate in cats. Etiology: Parvovirus, affinity for rapidly dividing cells.

  • Intestinal form — dogs >6 weeks; vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, dehydration, fever, leucopenia; microscopically necrotising enteritis of small intestine, intranuclear inclusions in intestinal epithelium, lymphopenia/neutropenia
  • Cardiac form — puppies 2–8 weeks; enteritis, sudden death; multiple foci of myocardial necrosis with mononuclear infiltrate; intranuclear inclusions in muscle fibres; vascular endothelial damage causes haemorrhage
  • Bovine parvovirus — isolated from young calf intestine; mucoid-to-watery diarrhoea in 24–48 hrs
  • Porcine parvovirus — intrauterine foetal death, mummification, infertility
  • Diagnosis — virus isolation in bovine cell culture, immunofluorescence, EM

Feline Panleucopenia

Synonyms: Feline distemper, feline enteritis, agranulocytosis. Etiology: Parvovirus. Susceptible: cats, cheetahs, leopards, tigers.

  • Transmission — shed in urine/faeces up to 6 weeks post-recovery; direct contact, fomites
  • Pathogenesis — infects/destroys actively dividing cells of bone marrow, lymphoid tissue, intestinal epithelium, and (in very young) cerebellum/retina; transplacental spread → resorption, mummification, abortion, stillbirth; perinatal infection destroys cerebellar germinal epithelium → cerebellar hypoplasia, ataxia, tremor (now rare due to maternal antibody transfer)
  • Clinical signs — severe panleucopenia; diphasic fever 104–105°F (falls at 24 hrs, rises again ~48 hrs later); enteritis, dehydration, death after the second fever peak
  • Gross lesions — dehydration, emaciation, mucopurulent nasal/lacrimal exudate, intestinal haemorrhage, enlarged oedematous mesenteric lymph nodes, yellowish semi-fluid long-bone marrow
  • Microscopic lesions — intestinal epithelial desquamation with proliferative crypts, granular eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions in lining epithelium; necrosis of follicular/paracortical lymphocytes, splenic Malpighian corpuscles, thymic cortex, Peyer's patches; bone marrow stem-cell depletion; intranuclear inclusions also in cerebellum of hypoplastic kittens
  • Diagnosis — intranuclear inclusions best seen in tissue fixed with acidic fixatives (Zenker's, Bouin's); ELISA, serum neutralization, haemagglutination inhibition

Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP)

Slowly progressive, fatal disease over months. Etiology: Corona virus; all ages susceptible.

  • Clinical signs — "Wet form" (excess fluid) or "Dry form" (small fluid); febrile, chronic weight loss, anorexia, depression, abdominal enlargement, vomiting/diarrhoea; neurological signs in some cats
  • Gross lesions — up to 1 litre of yellow, viscid, transparent fluid with fibrin flakes in abdomen; fibrinous deposits over liver/spleen serosa; pleural/pericardial fluid
  • Microscopic lesions — fibrinous peritonitis/pleuritis; multiple necrotic foci in liver/spleen/kidney; granulomatous vasculitis, immune-mediated
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs, gross/microscopic lesions, immunofluorescence, fluid accumulation pattern
Chapter recap
  • ICH — Canine adenovirus-1; "Blue eye"; thickened gall bladder is pathognomonic; big basophilic intranuclear inclusions.
  • Canine parvo — intestinal form (older pups) vs cardiac form (2–8 week pups, sudden death).
  • Feline panleucopenia — diphasic fever, cerebellar hypoplasia in kittens, use acidic fixatives to see inclusions.
  • FIP — Coronavirus, wet/dry forms, immune-mediated granulomatous vasculitis.

Prion Diseases — Scrapie, BSE & Feline Spongiform Encephalopathy

Prion diseases are unique: caused not by a conventional virus but by a misfolded host protein (prion) with no nucleic acid, producing no inflammation or immune response, only slow spongiform degeneration of the CNS.

Scrapie EXAM HOT

Synonym: Ovine spongiform encephalopathy. Earliest record Britain, 1732. By 1878–1900 at least 17 countries had reported it. Susceptible: sheep and goat.

  • Etiology — proteinaceous infective particle (Prion); no DNA/RNA; produces no inflammation or immune reaction
  • Transmission — oral ingestion; infectious placenta; prenatal infection; coitus
  • Pathogenesis — oral ingestion → tonsils/lymph node → CNS → normal cellular prion protein (PrPc, α-helix) undergoes conformational change to β-pleated sheet isoform (PrPsc) → accumulation produces pathological lesions
  • Clinical signs — restlessness, alertness, rubbing body on hard objects, teeth grinding, muscle twitching, wobbling gait, hind-limb paralysis, fits, emaciation, death; demyelination of cord/cerebrum
  • Gross lesions — none characteristic
  • Microscopic lesions — neuronal degeneration, astrocytosis, spongiosis — prominent neuronal/neuropil vacuolation in grey matter; marked astrocyte hypertrophy/hyperplasia; perivascular demyelination
  • Diagnosis — histopathology; eyelid test (developed in USA) for live-animal screening

Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE)

Synonym: Mad cow disease. First reported 1986, British dairy herds; later Ireland, and exported cattle to Oman, Italy, Denmark, Germany, Canada; also native cattle of France, Switzerland, Portugal.

  • Etiology — prion protein (PrP); normal form harmless, abnormal form causes disease
  • Incubation period — unusually long, 4–5 years
  • Transmission — feeding of meat/bone meal supplements to cattle
  • Pathogenesis — normal PrP (α-helix) transforms to abnormal PrPsc (β-pleated sheet), resistant to protease digestion; after oral infection, low-level replication first in lymphoid tissue (tonsils, suprapharyngeal node, gut-associated lymphoid tissue) → spreads to other nodes/spleen, replicating for up to a year → finally reaches CNS, the target organ
  • Clinical signs — long incubation (4–5 yrs); apprehension/anxiety/fear, abnormal posture/gait, extreme sensitivity to sound/touch, weight loss, repeated falling, recumbency; clinical course 2 weeks–6 months; mostly females affected; behaviour gives the name "mad cow disease"
  • Microscopic lesions — cytoplasmic vacuolation of neuropil neurons (spongiosis), gliosis, ballooned neurons with narrow cytoplasmic rim, scrapie-associated fibrils on EM
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs, brain pathology, antibodies against prion protein

Feline Spongiform Encephalopathy

  • First reported England, 1990
  • Transmission linked to consumption of bovine offal from BSE-affected cattle
  • Older cats affected; neurological signs — behavioural change, tremors, ataxia
  • Microscopically — diffuse spongiform change in grey matter, cytoplasmic vacuolation of neuropil and neurons
  • Fibrils and modified prion protein identified in affected brains
Chapter recap
  • Prion basics — no DNA/RNA, no inflammation/immune response, PrPc (α-helix, normal) → PrPsc (β-pleated sheet, pathogenic).
  • Scrapie — sheep/goat, earliest disease of the group (Britain, 1732), eyelid test for live screening.
  • BSE — cattle, 4–5 year incubation, caused by contaminated meat-and-bone-meal feed, "mad cow" behaviour.
  • FSE — cats, linked to BSE-contaminated offal in feed.
  • All three — core lesion is spongiosis/vacuolation of neuropil with astrocyte/glial reaction, no inclusion bodies.

Poxviridae — Lumpy Skin Disease, Pox Group, Orf & Bovine Papular Stomatitis

The Poxviridae produce cutaneous nodular/pustular lesions across almost every species, always marked microscopically by eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusion bodies in keratinocytes.

Lumpy Skin Disease

Highly infectious disease of cattle/buffaloes caused by a Capripox virus (related to sheep/goat pox viruses). First reported Zambia, 1929; originally restricted to Africa. Spread by insect vectors, mainly flies.

  • Clinical signs — generalised cutaneous eruption of round, firm nodules; nodules also on oral/nasal/genital mucosa; enlarged superficial lymph nodes; mortality ~10%
  • Microscopic lesions — dermal inflammation; oedematous nodule with perivascular lymphocytes/macrophages/plasma cells/neutrophils/proliferating fibroblasts; acanthosis, parakeratosis, vesicle formation; eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions in keratinocytes, fibroblasts, macrophages
  • Diagnosis — eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions in skin/tissue culture, EM, fluorescent antibody, serum neutralization

The Pox Group (overview)

  • Vaccinia — used for human smallpox immunisation; dermatotropic and neurotropic strains; infects rabbit, mouse, cattle, sheep, pig, monkey, human
  • Cowpox — wide host range, closely related to but distinct from variola/vaccinia; mild, self-limiting, lesions mainly on teats/udder; vesicle formation with cytoplasmic inclusions
  • Buffalo pox — reported from India, Indonesia, Pakistan, Egypt, Italy, Russia; vaccinia-like virus; lesions on teats/udder of milking buffaloes
  • Camel pox — debilitating, often fatal, in Africa and south-west Asia including India; high mortality with generalised lesions in young camels, milder localised form in older camels; humans not susceptible
  • Monkey pox — generalised cutaneous rash on hands/feet/legs/buttocks; first identified Denmark, 1958; fatal in infant monkeys with necrotising visceral lesions; cytoplasmic inclusion bodies aid diagnosis
  • Rabbit pox — can become generalised with visceral necrotising lesions; skin lesions lack vesicular character; no inclusion bodies reported
  • Mouse pox — Europe/USA; rapidly fatal form (few/no skin lesions) or chronic form (skin ulceration of feet/tail/snout); mortality 50–100%; recovery follows splenic/hepatic viral multiplication stage if death does not occur

Sheep Pox & Goat Pox EXAM HOT

Etiology: Capripox viruses. Endemic in India, severe economic losses; mortality 50% in adults, 100% in lambs.

  • Transmission — contact, inhalation; incubation 2–14 days; viraemia deposits virus in most organs including skin
  • Clinical signs — depression, prostration, fever, ocular/nasal discharge
  • Gross lesions — malignant form common in lambs; benign form in adults (tail, perineum, inner thigh, wool-less areas); papules → nodules → vesicles → pustules → scabs; mouth ulcers on tongue, gums, cheeks
  • Microscopic lesions — localised acanthosis/hyperplasia then vesiculation; distinctive "sheep pox cells" (cellules claveleuses of Borrel) in oedematous dermis/subcutis, perivascular and between collagen bundles, with intracytoplasmic inclusions; severe necrotising vasculitis; pneumonia with sheep pox cells in alveolar septa is the key visceral lesion
  • Diagnosis — fluorescent antibody test, immunodiffusion, demonstration of sheep pox cells in skin
  • Goat pox — immunologically related to sheep pox; longer incubation, less severe; young kids may show systemic disease with skin plus respiratory/alimentary mucosal lesions

Swine Pox

Benign disease with pox lesions on ventral abdomen. Etiology: Suipox virus; transmitted by the swine louse Haematopinus suis; solid immunity follows infection.

  • Clinical signs — incubation 5–7 days; erythematous areas → papules → vesicles → red-brown scales, mainly underside of body, inner limbs, back, face; eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions in epithelial cells

Contagious Pustular Dermatitis (Orf)

Synonyms: Contagious ovine ecthyma, infectious labial dermatitis, scabby mouth, sore mouth. Etiology: Parapox virus, related to bovine papular stomatitis and pseudo-cowpox viruses. More common in goats; outbreaks peak spring/summer.

  • Pathogenesis — skin damage needed for infection; virus replicates in replacement epidermal cells beneath epidermis/hair follicle walls; spreads laterally then through full epidermal depth; necrosis and sloughing
  • Clinical signs — papules, pustules, thick tenacious scabs over ulceration/granulation/inflammation at oral commissures, spreading to muzzle/nares/buccal mucosa; discrete thick scabs or continuous plaques; fissuring is painful; also ears, coronets, anus, vulva, prepuce; severe gastroenteritis and bronchopneumonia possible; more severe in lambs/kids
  • Gross lesions — raised papules/vesicles/pustules, necrosis, sloughing; generalised form shows oedema of abomasal mucosa and mesenteric lymph nodes
  • Microscopic lesions — striking epidermal proliferation/thickening with long dermal extensions; oedematous, cellular, leucocyte-infiltrated dermis; vacuolar degeneration, vesicle/pustule formation; eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions in keratinocytes
  • Diagnosis — characteristic lesions, virus isolation, AGID, EM

Bovine Papular Stomatitis

Synonym: Infectious ulcerative stomatitis and oesophagitis. Mild disease of young calves. Etiology: Parapox virus.

  • Gross lesions — lips, muzzle, nostrils, gingivae, tongue, oral mucosa; also oesophagus, rumen, reticulum, omasum; small hyperaemic foci on nostril margin/palate/lips develop into whitish papules within a day
  • Microscopic lesions — epidermal hyperplasia, hydropic degeneration, eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions, neutrophilic infiltration, erosion/ulceration
  • Diagnosis — characteristic lesions, virus isolation/identification, EM
Chapter recap
  • Lumpy skin disease — Capripox, cattle/buffalo, fly-borne, ~10% mortality.
  • Pox group — vaccinia/cowpox/buffalopox/camelpox/monkeypox/rabbitpox/mousepox — differ mainly in host range and severity.
  • Sheep/goat pox — "sheep pox cells" (cellules claveleuses of Borrel) are the diagnostic microscopic feature.
  • Orf (contagious pustular dermatitis) — Parapox, oral commissures, zoonotic-style scabby lesions in goats/sheep.
  • Bovine papular stomatitis — Parapox, mild, young calves, oral/upper-GI papules.
  • Universal pox signature — eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusion bodies in keratinocytes.

Rotavirus & Coronavirus Enteric Diseases of Neonates

Rotaviruses and coronaviruses are the two leading causes of neonatal enteric disease across species, both targeting mature villous enterocytes and producing villous atrophy/blunting; coronaviruses additionally cause important respiratory, neurological, and effusive (FIP) syndromes.

Rotaviral diseases — Introduction

Rota viruses are important causes of diarrhoea in young animals and children worldwide (infant children, calves, lambs, pigs, dogs, foals, mice). Highly species-specific.

Neonatal Calf Diarrhoea (Scours) EXAM HOT

  • Susceptibility — calves ~2 weeks old
  • Clinical signs — yellowish, watery diarrhoea, dehydration; morbidity/mortality high
  • Transmission — shed in faeces; direct contact, water contamination, airborne
  • Pathogenesis — ingested virus infects mature differentiated columnar epithelial cells of the apical villus (not crypt cells)
    • Group A rotavirus — necrosis, sloughing, stunted villi, crypt enterocyte hypertrophy
    • Group B rotavirus — multinucleated cells/syncytia on villous surface
  • Gross lesions — congestion and haemorrhage of small intestine
  • Diagnosis — faecal/tissue virus detection; immunofluorescence of faeces (up to 4–6 hr window), EM of faeces (6–10 days post-onset), ELISA (most sensitive)

Porcine Rotaviral Enteritis

Synonyms: white scour, milk scours, three-week scours. Enteric syndrome of piglets 1–4 weeks; diarrhoea, anorexia, depression, vomiting; mortality 7–20%.

Transmissible Gastroenteritis of Swine (TGE)

First described 1946. Highly contagious viral disease of young pigs; vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration; mortality up to 100% in piglets under 2 weeks.

  • Etiology — Corona virus, lacks haemagglutinating property, antigenically related to feline infectious peritonitis and canine corona virus
  • Transmission — carrier pigs, oral/aerosol route; shed in milk, nasal secretions, faeces
  • Pathogenesis — infects mature columnar villous epithelium (not undifferentiated crypt cells) of upper respiratory tract and intestine; replication in 4–5 hrs → sloughing/virus release → marked villous atrophy, mainly in jejunum; crypt cells proliferate to compensate
  • Clinical signs — piglets <10 days: diarrhoea, vomiting, excessive thirst, weight loss, dehydration, death in 2–5 days (mortality ~100%); older piglets/adults: profuse diarrhoea, vomiting, failure to gain weight (morbidity 100%, mortality low)
  • Gross lesions — dehydrated carcass, curdled stomach milk, congested gastric/small intestinal mucosa
  • Microscopic lesions — marked shortening and fusion of small intestinal villi
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs, histopathology, ELISA/IFT/IPT/neutralization test

Porcine Corona Viral Encephalomyelitis

Two clinical entities in newborn piglets — Encephalomyelitis (Canada, 1959) and "vomiting and wasting disease" (England, 1969). Virus enters via respiratory tract and reaches brain by peripheral nerves.

  • Clinical signs (piglets 4–7 days) — anorexia, lethargy, vomiting, constipation; CNS signs — hyperaesthesia, stiff gait, progressive posterior paralysis, prostration, dyspnoea, coma
  • Vomiting and wasting disease — vomiting, inappetence, polydipsia, emaciation, death within 2 weeks
  • Gross/microscopic lesions — neuronal necrosis, non-suppurative encephalomyelitis, congested GI tract; glial nodules, diffuse gliosis, perivascular cuffing
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs, virus identification, differentiate from other coronaviruses

Corona Viral Calf Diarrhoea

Diarrhoea in calves <3 weeks caused by corona virus.

  • Clinical signs — severe diarrhoea 4–5 days, dehydration, death
  • Gross lesions — congestion/inflammation of intestine
  • Microscopic lesions — virus replicates in intestinal epithelium; loss of epithelium, blunting/fusion of villi
  • Diagnosis — immunofluorescence of intestine and mesenteric lymph nodes

Canine Corona Virus Infection

Severe diarrhoeal disease of young dogs, first reported 1971. Signs: diarrhoea, vomiting, dehydration; marked shortening and fusion of intestinal villi (same mechanism as other corona enteritides).

Chapter recap
  • Shared target — both rotavirus and coronavirus infect mature villous tip enterocytes (not crypt cells), causing villous atrophy/blunting.
  • Group A vs B rotavirus — A = necrosis/sloughing/stunting; B = syncytia formation.
  • TGE — coronavirus, near-100% mortality under 2 weeks, related antigenically to FIP and canine corona virus.
  • Coronavirus is versatile — causes enteritis (calf/pig/dog), CNS disease (porcine encephalomyelitis), and effusive disease (FIP, covered in Ch.9).
  • Diagnosis theme — immunofluorescence/ELISA of faeces or intestinal tissue across all these agents.

General Pathology of Bacterial Infection & Mycobacterial Diseases

Bacterial disease pathology begins with how bacteria are structured and how they injure the host, then applies directly to the two classic Mycobacterium granulomatous diseases — Tuberculosis and Johne's Disease — both slow, chronic, and centred on the epithelioid-cell granuloma.

Bacterial basics

  • May be extracellular or intracellular (Mycobacterium, Brucella); contain both RNA and DNA; lack a nucleus and most organelles; reproduce by binary fission
  • Classification — morphology (cocci, chains, bacilli, branching filaments), Gram stain, virulence, and pattern of spread
  • Localised infection may occur at the entry portal (Cl. tetani) or after systemic spread and secondary localisation (Mycobacterium sp.)
  • Some diseases result from ingestion of preformed toxin outside the body (botulism, staphylococcal food poisoning)

  • Adhesins — fimbrial proteins (E. coli, Salmonella), lipoteichoic acid/M protein (streptococci)
  • Pilus tropism in E. coli — Type 1 (mannose) → urinary infection; Type P (galactose) → pyelonephritis; Type S (sialic acid) → meningitis
  • Colonisation resistance — normal microflora block receptor sites and produce bacteriocins
  • Dissemination — aided by collagenase, hyaluronidase; growth is limited by iron availability (lactoferrin), gastric acidity, and high body temperature (birds resist anthrax)

Tuberculosis (TB) EXAM HOT

Chronic wasting disease caused by acid-fast Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex: M. tuberculosis (man/animals), M. bovis (man/animals), M. avium (fowl/pigs/monkeys), M. paratuberculosis (Johne's), M. leprae (human leprosy).

  • Transmission — aerogenous/inhalation, ingestion; rarely cutaneous, congenital, mammary
  • Pathogenesis — escapes macrophage killing → delayed-type (IV) hypersensitivity: local oedema/hyperaemia/neutrophil infiltration → macrophages ingest dead neutrophils with bacilli → bacilli proliferate → macrophage nodule → epithelioid cells form → fuse into multinucleated Langhans' giant cells (horseshoe-arranged peripheral nuclei) → lymphocyte cuff → fibroblast zone → hard tubercle; centre undergoes caseation/calcification → soft tubercle
  • Tubercle structure (inside-out) — central calcified area → caseated pinkish-blue zone → necrosis zone → epithelioid cells → Langhans giant cells → lymphocytes → fibroblast zone
  • Clinical signs — cattle: progressive emaciation, erratic appetite, chronic cough (pulmonary), diarrhoea (intestinal), mastitis (spread via milk); pigs: cervical lymph node abscessation, meningitis, arthritis; horses: cervical spondylitis, stiff neck; sheep/goat: bronchopneumonia, diarrhoea
  • Gross lesions — caseous acinar/lobar bronchopneumonia; encapsulated caseous gritty nodules ("hard to cut"); pleural/peritoneal "pearly disease" (bunch-of-grapes tubercles); intestinal Peyer's patch ulcers in calves; three mammary forms: disseminated miliary, lobular infiltrating, mastitis tuberculosa contagiosa
  • Species variation — buffalo: little calcification, frequent retropharyngeal node involvement; horse: chronic proliferative, rare caseation/calcification; birds: no calcification; swine: caseo-calcareous encapsulated head/neck/mesenteric node foci; dogs/cats: distinct granulomas with caseous centres
  • Diagnosis — clinical diagnosis only late-stage; Ziehl-Neelsen stain for acid-fast bacilli; tuberculin test; formol-saline fixed lesion for lab submission

Johne's Disease (Paratuberculosis)

Chronic wasting illness of ruminants — prolonged course, recurrent diarrhoea, dehydration, emaciation, death. Etiology: Mycobacterium paratuberculosis (bovine, ovine, Scottish strains).

  • Transmission — ingestion; faeces are the primary infection source
  • Pathogenesis — long incubation (up to 2 years); organism enters via intestinal mucosa → bacteraemia → settles in intestinal mucosa, mesenteric lymph nodes, tonsil → chronic granulomatous inflammation; cell-mediated then humoral response; Type III immune complex reaction contributes to intestinal lesions and diarrhoea
  • Clinical signs — emaciation, "hide-bound" dry coat, normal appetite with increased thirst and diarrhoea; sheep/goat: diarrhoea uncommon, emaciation predominates
  • Gross lesions — gelatinous fat wasting; terminal ileum wall thickened, oedematous, mucosa shows transverse corrugation ("cerebral convolution" pattern) that persists even when the wall is stretched; corrugation less marked in sheep/goat; uterine infection can cause congenital infection/abortion
  • Microscopic lesions — mucosa/submucosa/mesenteric nodes infiltrated with foamy epithelioid cells, sometimes fusing into syncytia; oedema, neutrophil/eosinophil infiltration, giant cells; nodular necrosis/calcification described in sheep/goat
  • Diagnosis — bowel washings, rectal pinch smear, Johnin test (double intradermal), CFT, AGID, ELISA
Chapter recap
  • Exotoxin vs endotoxin — exotoxins are secreted, specific, often neurotoxic; endotoxin = LPS from Gram-negative cell wall, causes fever/shock/DIC.
  • TB — Langhans' giant cells, hard→soft tubercle progression, Ziehl-Neelsen stain, tuberculin test.
  • Johne's — M. paratuberculosis; transverse intestinal corrugation is the classic gross lesion; Johnin test for diagnosis.
  • Shared theme — both are chronic granulomatous Mycobacterium diseases with epithelioid/giant cell reaction, differing mainly by target organ.

Actinomycete & Filamentous Bacterial Diseases

These diseases share a distinctive granulomatous pattern built around "club colonies" of branching, filamentous bacteria — visible grossly as characteristic granules (sulphur granules, rosettes).

Actinomycosis ("Lumpy Jaw") EXAM HOT

Infection by Actinomyces bovis — Gram-positive, anaerobic, branching ("ray fungus"), non-sporulating rod, a normal inhabitant of the mouth/pharynx mucosa.

  • Transmission — oral wounds (awns, hull-cutting teeth, FMD lesions) create the necrotic anaerobic conditions the organism needs
  • Pathogenesis — granulomatous inflammation with a central Indian-club-like structure (immune reaction product); neutrophils centrally, then histiocytes/epithelioid cells, then lymphocytes/plasma cells/eosinophils and vascular fibrous tissue peripherally; yellowish pus = "sulphur granules" (large)
  • Gross lesions — suppurative osteomyelitis of the mandible ("lumpy jaw"); medulla filled with granulation tissue and nodules, cortex rarefied, fistulae form; subperiosteal new bone (involucrum); horses show poll evil/fistulous withers; udder shows miliary nodules
  • Diagnosis — pus smear, histopathology

Actinobacillosis ("Wooden Tongue")

Etiology: Actinobacillus lignieresi — Gram-negative aerobe, normal flora of upper respiratory/alimentary tract; infects soft tissue via wounds/abrasions.

  • Clinical signs — mainly tongue and head/neck/thoracic lymph nodes; tongue becomes fibrous, hard, shrunken, immobile — "wooden tongue" (diffuse sclerosing actinobacillosis)
  • Gross lesions — cattle: soft-tissue nodules with central sulphur granules, lymphatic spread causing lymphadenitis/abscess; pigs: similar; sheep: lingual lesions uncommon, subcutaneous head granulomas instead
  • Microscopic lesions — club-like structure around organism colony, encircled by neutrophils, histiocytes, giant cells, lymphocytes, plasma cells, fibrous tissue
  • Diagnosis — Gram-negative rods in pus, histology, agglutination test

Nocardiosis

Purulent, necrotising, occasionally granulomatous lesions of lung, pleura, or skin caused by Nocardia asteroides. Affects dogs and cattle.

  • Gross lesions — pulmonary/pleural infection common in dogs; systemic spread can localise in peritoneal/pleural cavities
  • Microscopic lesions — organism surrounded by necrotic debris, purulent exudate, macrophages/lymphocytes/plasma cells/giant cells, fibrous tissue; filamentous, acid-fast, Gram-positive (unlike Actinomyces, which is acid-fast negative); can cause mastitis, pneumonia, abortion in cattle
  • Diagnosis — tissue section and cultural examination

Ovine Caseous Lymphadenitis (Pseudotuberculosis)

Chronic disease of sheep/goats with lymph node abscessation. Etiology: Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis (C. ovis) — Gram-positive diphtheroid, intracellular, survives via exotoxin and high cell-wall lipid content.

  • Transmission — skin abrasion, inhalation
  • Clinical signs — enlarged superficial lymph nodes rupturing thick green pus; chronic pneumonia, pyelonephritis, paraplegia/ataxia if haematogenous spread reaches CNS
  • Gross lesions — spherical abscess, concentrically laminated on cross-section — alternating fibrous capsule and greenish gritty caseous material ("onion-ring" appearance)
  • Microscopic lesions — central caseous necrosis surrounded by thin fibrous layer, epithelioid cells, lymphocytes; repeated layers form the laminated onion-like mass; calcification without giant cells
  • Diagnosis — needle biopsy of pus, culture, indirect haemagglutination, ELISA

Anthrax & Clostridial Myonecroses

This chapter groups the classic acute, sudden-death bacterial diseases of grazing ruminants: anthrax (Bacillus anthracis) and the spore-forming Clostridium myonecroses/hepatic toxaemias — Black Quarter, Braxy, Black Disease, Gas Gangrene, and Bacillary Haemoglobinuria.

Anthrax EXAM HOT

Synonyms: Splenic fever, charbon. Acute septicaemic disease of herbivores with high fever, rapid course, sudden death, and greatly enlarged spleen, caused by Bacillus anthracis (Gram-positive, capsulated, spore-forming rod).

  • Susceptibility — sheep and cattle most susceptible; also buffalo, horse, mule, goat, pig, rabbit, fowl; man via contact — 'wool sorter's disease' (inhalation) or 'malignant carbuncle' (cutaneous)
  • Transmission — ingestion, wound, flies, inhalation, vaccination; spread by water, flies, wild birds
  • Pathogenesis — incubation 1–14 days; bacilli proliferate in tonsils → lymphatics; spores in intestine develop into bacilli, penetrate wall, multiply in lymphoid follicles; capsule resists phagocytosis and prevents clotting (fibrinolytic); diffusible toxin damages brain/nerve cells; three toxin factors — I (oedema factor), II (protective antigen), III (lethal factor); death in 12–36 hrs, often from asphyxia (toxin on respiratory centre)
  • Key rule — spores do NOT form in the living animal but form when the carcass is opened and exposed to air → "Do not open the carcass"
  • Clinical signs — peracute: sudden death, no signs; acute: high temperature, excitement, weakness, cyanosis, dyspnoea, subcutaneous oedema (sheep/horse); pigs: throat swelling with cervical lymphadenopathy
  • Gross lesions — (PM should NOT be performed) greatly enlarged spleen engorged with dark unclotted blood; swollen oedematous/haemorrhagic lymph nodes; haemorrhages in intestine, liver, kidney; splenomegaly less constant in sheep/horse; swine show pharyngeal/cervical node oedema and haemorrhage
  • Microscopic lesions — numerous bacilli in smears/sections, truncated ends in short chains with capsule; spores never seen in anthrax tissue
  • Diagnosis — sudden death with bloody discharge from orifices; McFadyen's reaction (methylene blue capsule stain); Ascoli precipitation test (white precipitate = positive); FAT

Black Quarter (Blackleg) EXAM HOT

Synonym: Symptomatic anthrax. Acute febrile disease of cattle (6 months–2 years), less often sheep/goat/swine, with emphysematous serohaemorrhagic swelling of heavy muscles. Etiology: Clostridium chauvoei.

  • Transmission — ingestion of spores (cattle); wound infection (sheep)
  • Pathogenesis — organisms multiply in intestine → blood → muscle (thigh, shoulder) → necrotising myositis with gas (gangrene), smell of rancid butter; death in 24–60 hrs from toxaemia
  • Gross lesions — crepitating swelling, "rubber sponge" texture, dark serosanguinous fluid with gas bubbles; muscle black from iron sulphide; dry centre with rancid-butter odour; regional nodes swollen
  • Microscopic lesions — waxy degeneration/coagulation necrosis of muscle fibres, haemorrhage streaks, Gram-positive bacilli
  • Diagnosis — gross lesions, organism demonstration, guinea pig inoculation, FAT; often found dead before signs are seen

Bovine Bacillary Haemoglobinuria (Red Water Disease)

High-mortality disease with fever, anaemia, haemoglobinuria, hepatic necrosis. Etiology: Clostridium haemolyticum. First described California 1916.

  • Susceptibility — mostly cattle; swampy/low-lying areas endemic
  • Pathogenesis — organism ingested, reaches liver, activated by hepatic damage (e.g. liver fluke migration); beta exotoxin causes necrotic infarcts (necrotising and haemolysing toxin)
  • Clinical signs — sudden fever, intravascular haemolytic anaemia (RBC ~1 million/cmm, Hb ~3g/dL), leucocytosis, collapse, death; abortion in pregnant animals
  • Gross lesions — focal hepatic necrotic infarcts (soft, enlarged, friable); swollen kidneys with cortical petechiae; deep red urine
  • Diagnosis — clinical signs/lesions, organism isolation

Braxy (Bradsot)

Acute fatal disease of sheep from toxin of Clostridium septicum, causing haemorrhagic abomasitis. Seen mainly in Scotland, Scandinavia, Canada, northern USA.

  • Transmission — ingestion of frozen grass/feed
  • Pathogenesis — organisms enter via abomasal wall wounds (nematodes, chilled grass) and liberate toxin
  • Clinical signs — colic, abomasal bloat, coma, death within hours
  • Gross lesions — thickened, oedematous, haemorrhagic abomasal (and intestinal) wall
  • Diagnosis — demonstration of large bacilli in tissue sections

Black Disease (Infectious Necrotic Hepatitis)

Acute fatal infection of sheep (rarely cattle) caused by Clostridium novyi type B. First reported Australia.

  • Transmission — ingestion; faecal pasture contamination by carriers
  • Pathogenesis — organisms reach liver via intestine; anaerobic environment from migrating liver flukes activates bacteria → exotoxin → hepatic necrosis
  • Clinical signs — death within hours, often no signs seen
  • Gross/microscopic lesions — multiple hepatic necrotic foci; petechiae on epi-/endocardium; hydropericardium; subcutaneous venous congestion causes dark skin discolouration — hence "black disease"
  • Diagnosis — pathology plus organism isolation

Gas Gangrene (Malignant Oedema)

Acute toxaemic wound infection caused by Clostridium septicum — soft, pitting swelling at infection site.

  • Transmission — soil-borne, wound infection
  • Pathogenesis — anaerobes produce alpha/beta/gamma/delta exotoxins (alpha most dangerous) → capillary injury → oedema/necrosis; all clostridia produce gas except Cl. novyi
  • Clinical signs — high fever, depression, anorexia; rapidly spreading soft/hot/painful swelling turning cold and insensitive; post-parturition (vulva) or post-castration (abdominal) sites common; overlying skin gangrenous; death in 24–48 hrs
  • Gross lesions — oedematous, haemorrhagic, gas-filled tissue; septicaemic haemorrhages; congested oedematous lungs; blood-tinged peritoneal effusion
  • Diagnosis — clinical/lesions, guinea pig inoculation; differentiated from Black Quarter by absence of typical muscle involvement and presence of a wound; affects older animals (BQ affects younger)
Chapter recap
  • Anthrax — B. anthracis; NEVER open the carcass; McFadyen's reaction, Ascoli test.
  • Black Quarter — Cl. chauvoei; rancid-butter odour, gas gangrene of heavy muscles.
  • Bacillary haemoglobinuria — Cl. haemolyticum; liver fluke migration triggers activation; red water/deep red urine.
  • Braxy — Cl. septicum; frozen feed; haemorrhagic abomasitis.
  • Black disease — Cl. novyi type B; fluke migration; dark skin from venous congestion.
  • Gas gangrene — Cl. septicum; wound-associated, differentiate from Black Quarter by wound history and lack of muscle-specific involvement; only Cl. novyi among clostridia does NOT produce gas.

Tetanus & Clostridium perfringens Enterotoxaemias

Clostridium tetani and Clostridium perfringens both cause disease through potent exotoxins rather than tissue invasion — tetanus through a neurotoxin fixed to nerve tissue, and perfringens through a family of enterotoxins graded A through E.

Tetanus (Lock Jaw) EXAM HOT

Acute fatal disease with involuntary contraction of voluntary muscles, caused by toxins of Clostridium tetani (Gram-positive sporulating anaerobe; spores are "drum-stick" shaped).

  • Susceptibility — horses and mules most susceptible
  • Transmission — wound infection (nail prick, castration, docking, shearing, umbilical wound = tetanus neonatorum, or during parturition)
  • Three toxins — Tetanolysin (haemolysin, not important), Tetanospasmin (neurotoxin, responsible for signs), Fibrinolysin (weak)
  • Pathogenesis — incubation 1–3 weeks; anaerobic wound conditions allow spore germination and toxin release; toxin fixes to "protagon," acts on inhibitory synapses, blocking inhibitory neurotransmitter → hyperirritability and spastic muscle contraction
  • Clinical signs — involuntary, persistent, painful muscle contraction; horse moves like a "wooden horse"; raised tail, third eyelid protrusion, jaw stiffness — "lock jaw"; ruminants show milder signs
  • Lesions — no characteristic gross lesion; death from toxaemia; microscopically only neuronal degeneration from anoxaemia; organism remains local, not septicaemic
  • Diagnosis — characteristic clinical signs; demonstration of toxin in serum

Clostridium perfringens type D — Pulpy Kidney Disease

  • Pathogenesis — normal gut inhabitant; excess starch feeding ("overeating disease") + intestinal atony/stasis favour proliferation; epsilon toxin is rapidly absorbed by intestinal mucosa → toxaemia; smaller amounts of alpha and theta toxin also contribute; toxin reaches brain → opisthotonus, convulsion, coma; hyperglycaemia and glycosuria seen (increased hepatic glycogenolysis)
  • Gross lesions — petechial/ecchymotic haemorrhages on epicardium, endocardium, intestinal serosa, abdominal muscles, diaphragm; hydropericardium; catarrhal/haemorrhagic gastroenteritis; swollen, pulpy kidneys (hence the name); symmetrical encephalomalacia
  • Microscopic lesions — kidney: cloudy swelling/necrosis of proximal convoluted tubule epithelium; liver/spleen congested; brain: white matter lysis/liquefaction, oedematous grey matter
  • Diagnosis — glycosuria supports diagnosis; mouse biological/neutralisation test — filtered intestinal contents are lethal to mice IV within minutes to 4 hrs; neutralisation with known type-D antitoxin prevents death, confirming the toxin

  • Cl. perfringens types A–E — A: enterotoxaemia; B: lamb dysentery; C: "struck"/haemorrhagic enterotoxaemia; D: pulpy kidney/overeating disease (epsilon toxin); E: calf/lamb enterotoxaemia.
  • Pulpy kidney — the classic named clostridial disease: overeating → epsilon toxin → swollen pulpy kidney + encephalomalacia; mouse neutralisation test confirms.

Botulism & Glanders

Two very different toxin/invasion-driven diseases: Botulism, a pure food-borne intoxication causing flaccid paralysis, and Glanders, a chronic granulomatous soliped disease with characteristic farcy lesions.

Botulism EXAM HOT

Rapidly fatal motor paralytic disease from ingestion of Clostridium botulinum exotoxin — the toxin must be preformed in food, as the organism cannot generate toxin in the gut.

  • Toxin types — A: "limber neck" of poultry; C: forage poisoning of horses; D: "Lamsiekte"; E: fish/fish products
  • Susceptibility — man and chicken most affected; cattle/horse to a lesser extent; swine, sheep, dogs are resistant
  • Transmission — ingestion of preformed toxin, classically from carrion (decaying flesh) contaminating feed
  • Pathogenesis — absorbed toxin enters peripheral nerves and blocks acetylcholine release; does NOT enter the CNS
  • Clinical signs — cattle: pica/bone-chewing (phosphorus deficiency) ingesting toxin from decaying meat; cattle/horse: forage poisoning from contaminated hay; wound botulism in horses; salivation, paralysis, impaired vision, tongue paralysis → cannot swallow; birds: torticollis — "limber neck" from eating infected fly maggots; death from respiratory failure/asphyxia; no characteristic lesions
  • Diagnosis — neutralisation test with antitoxin; FAT; feed filtrate fed to mice causes paralysis/death with a "wasp-waist" appearance

Glanders (Farcy)

Contagious, usually chronic disease of horses/mules/donkeys with nodules and ulcers of the upper airways, lungs and skin. Etiology: Pseudomonas (Actinobacillus) mallei.

  • Susceptibility — solipeds highly susceptible; man moderately susceptible (zoonotic); sheep/goats occasionally
  • Transmission — ingestion, inhalation, skin invasion
  • Pathogenesis — penetrates pharyngeal/intestinal mucosa → circulation → pulmonary capillaries → septicaemic phase with fever → emboli in pulmonary capillaries → acute perialveolar/peribronchiolar inflammation → serous exudate → penetrates bronchial wall, aspirated into alveoli → bronchopneumonia; neutrophils show karyorrhexis with scattered nuclear fragments — characteristic of glanders; organism disseminates to skin, forming nodules/ulcers
  • Clinical forms — acute: septicaemia with fever; chronic pulmonary: chronic cough, pneumonic signs, purulent oily discharge; chronic cutaneous: ulcers with oily pus, coiled tortuous lymphatics
  • Gross lesions — lungs: miliary nodules, fibrinous pleurisy, punched-out tracheal/laryngeal ulcers; lymphatics: swollen oedematous nodes with yellowish-grey centres; thick tortuous "farcy cords" with "farcy buds" that discharge oily pus and form persistent ulcers
  • Microscopic lesions — granulomatous pulmonary lesions with caseous centre, epithelioid cells, giant cells, lymphocytes; dilated, leucocyte-packed, "beaded" lymph vessels
  • Diagnosis — lesions, CFT, guinea pig inoculation (Straus reaction — suppurative orchitis in males within 4–5 days), confirmed by intradermal mallein test
  • Key differential — unilateral nasal discharge is characteristic of glanders (vs bilateral in strangles)
Chapter recap
  • Botulism — preformed toxin ingested, blocks ACh release at peripheral nerve; "limber neck" in birds; wasp-waist mouse test.
  • Glanders — Pseudomonas mallei; farcy cords/buds; unilateral nasal discharge; Straus reaction; mallein test.
  • Contrast — botulism = intoxication with no lesions; glanders = chronic granulomatous invasive disease with striking lesions.

Staphylococcal & Streptococcal Diseases

These are the two Gram-positive cocci that cause a huge spectrum of pyogenic disease — from simple skin pyoderma to strangles and neonatal septicaemia — via surface adhesins, enzymes and toxins that damage host cells and evade phagocytosis.

Staphylococcal Infections

Staphylococcus aureus causes many purulent conditions: pyoderma, furunculosis, impetigo, and tick pyaemia of lambs (introduced by Ixodes ricinus bites; multiple disseminated abscesses + polyarthritis).

  • Pathogenesis — surface proteins aid adherence; enzymes degrade host proteins (virulence factors); toxins damage host cells; prevents phagocytosis
  • Important S. aureus diseases — Botryomycosis; staphylococcal bovine mastitis (chronic or acute purulent); exudative epidermitis of swine

Botryomycosis

  • Chronic pyogranulomatous inflammation; cattle: mammary glands; horses: castration wounds
  • Multiple firm nodules with purulent centres containing "grains"
  • Microscopic — central suppurative necrosis surrounded by neutrophils, granulomatous tissue (epithelioid cells, lymphocytes, plasma cells), fibrous capsule; eosinophilic "rosettes" (Splendore-Hoeppli material) centrally; Gram stain shows coccal colonies surrounded by rosettes

Exudative Epidermitis of Swine (Greasy Pig Disease)

  • Acute generalised seborrhoeic dermatitis; sows/gilts are carriers, suckling pigs affected
  • Peracute — sudden cutaneous erythema, anaemia, dehydration, death in 24–48 hrs; skin wrinkled/reddened with greasy grey-brown exudate around eyes, ears, abdomen
  • Acute/subacute/chronic — progressively thickened greasy exudate, scab formation, skin thickening
  • Diagnosis — clinical history, isolation of S. aureus

Streptococcosis EXAM HOT

  • Streptococcal mastitis — obligate parasite of bovine mammary gland, cow-to-cow via milker's hands/equipment; enters via teat canal, colonises ducts/acini without deep interstitial invasion; epithelial neutrophilic then macrophage infiltration, fibroblastic proliferation, cystic duct dilation with papillary projections; ends in glandular fibrosis
  • Urogenital infections — S. canis (dogs), S. zooepidemicus (horses) — vaginitis, cervicitis, endometritis, placentitis, abortion, urethritis, pyelonephritis
  • Cervical lymphadenitis of pigs — "swine strangles"/jowl abscess, characterised by abscesses
  • Streptococcal meningitis and arthritis in pigs — S. suis in young pigs, oral/respiratory entry, pharyngitis/cervical lymphangitis progressing to fibrinopurulent meningitis and polyarthritis
  • Neonatal streptococcal infection — suppurative polyarthritis ("joint ill"), meningitis, navel ill, localisation in valvular endocardium, kidneys, choroid of eye

Strangles

Synonym: Adenitis equorum. Acute contagious disease of young horses — sudden fever, mucopurulent upper respiratory inflammation, then submaxillary/pharyngeal lymph node abscessation. Etiology: Streptococcus equi.

  • Transmission — inhalation mainly, ingestion possible
  • Pathogenesis — incubation 4–8 days; pharyngeal/nasal mucosal infection → pharyngitis/rhinitis → lymphatic drainage causes abscessation; may spread to lung, kidney, liver, spleen, brain, tendon sheath, joints; after recovery, hypersensitivity to streptococcal protein may cause purpura haemorrhagica (petechial/ecchymotic haemorrhages)
  • Clinical signs and lesions — oedematous pharyngeal swelling causing dyspnoea ("strangling" appearance); abscess with cream-yellow pus; retropharyngeal abscesses may drain into guttural pouches causing empyema; death from septicaemia/pyaemia
  • Diagnosis — bilateral nasal discharge (vs unilateral in glanders); purpura haemorrhagica as a recognised complication; organism demonstration in pus smears
Chapter recap
  • Botryomycosis — S. aureus, pyogranuloma with Splendore-Hoeppli "rosette" grains.
  • Exudative epidermitis — S. aureus, greasy pig disease, suckling piglets.
  • Streptococcal mastitis — ductal/acinar colonisation, no deep interstitial invasion, ends in fibrosis.
  • Strangles — S. equi; bilateral nasal discharge distinguishes it from glanders; purpura haemorrhagica is a classic post-infection complication.

Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (Barbone) EXAM HOT

Acute, occasionally subacute, febrile disease of cattle/buffalo — sudden onset, high temperature, subcutaneous oedema of throat/brisket, acute gastroenteritis. Etiology: Pasteurella boviseptica.

  • Transmission — ingestion of contaminated feed/water; organism in saliva; droplet infection
  • Pathogenesis — organism reaches blood, spreads septicaemically → petechial/ecchymotic haemorrhages on serous/mucous membranes throughout the body — hence "Haemorrhagic Septicaemia"
  • Clinical signs — high temperature, dullness, dyspnoea; hot painful head/dewlap/neck swelling with subcutaneous inflammatory exudate
  • Gross lesions — petechiae on epicardium, myocardium, pleura, peritoneum, GI tract; swollen haemorrhagic lymph nodes; severely inflamed GI tract with blood-mixed contents
  • Microscopic lesions — fibrinous bronchopneumonia in acute/subacute cases; small ovoid bipolar-staining rods; rabbit: haemorrhagic tracheitis
  • Diagnosis — blood smear; heart-blood swab rubbed on scarified rabbit abdomen — rabbit dies in 24–40 hrs with haemorrhage, especially haemorrhagic tracheitis (pathognomonic)

Fowl Cholera

Etiology: P. multocida (avi septica). Acute/chronic, generalised/local infectious disease of fowl and birds — sudden onset, enteritis, submucous/subserous petechial haemorrhages, enlarged liver/spleen. Transmitted by inhalation or ingestion.

Shipping Fever

Multifactorial respiratory disease of transported calves/young stock.

  • Predisposing factors — stress, heat/cold, fatigue, trauma, insufficient feed/water, anxiety
  • Viral contributors — parainfluenza-3, IBR, enterovirus, bovine adenovirus
  • Bacterial contributors — P. multocida, Streptococcus sp., Pseudomonas sp., Haemophilus sp.
  • Clinical signs — depression, standing aloof, anorexia, dry nose, increased respiratory rate
  • Lesions — oedema/haemorrhage of upper nasal passage, larynx, trachea, regional lymph nodes
Chapter recap
  • Pasteurella = bipolar-staining Gram-negative rods; many "species" are actually strains of P. multocida.
  • Haemorrhagic septicaemia — cattle/buffalo; rabbit inoculation test with pathognomonic haemorrhagic tracheitis.
  • Fowl cholera — P. multocida (avi septica) in birds.
  • Shipping fever — a multifactorial stress+virus+bacteria complex, not a single pathogen disease.

Leptospirosis & Listeriosis

Both diseases are caused by motile, invasive organisms capable of crossing intact skin/mucosa and reaching multiple target organs — Leptospira via spirochaetal septicaemia, and Listeria via active intracellular/neural spread.

Leptospirosis EXAM HOT

Synonym: Weil's disease (man), Stuttgart disease. Zoonotic disease of dogs and large domestic animals from several Leptospira species.

  • Species-host pairing — L. icterohaemorrhagiae (man — Weil's disease), L. canicola (dogs), L. pomona (swine/cattle), L. grippotyphosa (goat)
  • Transmission — ingestion, coitus, abrasions, intact skin, in utero; man infected while swimming; rat is a reservoir; organism survives in water up to 3 months; recovered animals shed for up to a year
  • Detection — dark-field examination of fluid media shows spirochaetes; tissue sections stained black by Levaditi and Warthin-Starry silver stains
  • Pathogenesis — invades blood, causes septicaemia with prolonged fever; settles in liver, kidney, pregnant uterus; jaundice from intravascular haemolysis and hepatic necrosis; anaemia, icterus, haemoglobinuria; organisms shed in urine; interstitial nephritis (focal/diffuse); abortion; death from uraemia
  • Cattle — clinical course
    • Acute — septicaemia, high temperature, icterus, petechiae on visible mucosa
    • Subacute — milder, abortion
    • Chronic — no clinical signs
  • Cattle — lesions — acute: icterus, anaemia, serosal petechiae, centrilobular liver haemorrhages; liver: shrunken/dissociated hepatocytes, congestion, haemosiderin in Kupffer cells; kidney: swollen/red, tubular degeneration/necrosis; spleen: haemosiderosis; acute haemorrhagic meningitis, placental necrosis; subacute: interstitial nephritis with lymphocyte/plasma cell infiltration, placentitis/abortion (Leptospira found in the foetus)
  • Other species — sheep/goat: peracute dullness, abortion, icterus; pigs: abortion, nervous signs, focal interstitial nephritis; horses: periodic ophthalmia (L. pomona); dogs: L. canicola/L. icterohaemorrhagiae — nephritis (kidney), jaundice (liver); focal interstitial nephritis with tubular spirochaete clusters; L. canicola can cause purulent lymphocytic meningoencephalitis and gliosis
  • Diagnosis — organism demonstration in tissue, microscopic agglutination test, inoculation, dark-field urine examination

Listeriosis (Circling Disease)

Caused by Listeria monocytogenes (Gram-positive, rod-shaped, intracellular) — encephalitis, systemic infection, or abortion.

  • Transmission — ingestion of contaminated material/silage/milk, congenital, oral mucosal wounds/tooth cavities → trigeminal nerve; organism excreted in faeces even in absence of disease
  • Pathogenesis — surface leucine-rich proteins (internalins) bind host E-cadherin, allowing epithelial cell penetration (conjunctiva, bladder, intestine); multiplies, destroys cells, released; phagocytosed by macrophages, escapes the phagosome to lie free in cytoplasm, multiplies, carried to tissues causing septicaemia; within macrophages, moves centripetally along the trigeminal nerve to reach the medulla oblongata
  • Clinical signs — encephalitis form — abnormal head/neck position, "circling," nystagmus; more common in ruminants
  • Lesions — medulla — circumscribed perivascular mononuclear cell cuffs, no tissue necrosis; Gram-positive organisms demonstrable in sections
  • Systemic infection — more common in monogastrics; characteristic focal hepatic necrosis
  • Abortion — common in cattle/sheep, last trimester; focal hepatic necrosis with stainable organisms in foetal liver
  • Diagnosis — microabscesses, glial nodules, perivascular lymphocyte cuffing, lymphocytic leptomeningitis with Gram-positive organisms; isolation and culture
Chapter recap
  • Leptospirosis — dark-field microscopy/Warthin-Starry stain; species-host associations (canicola-dog, pomona-swine/cattle, icterohaemorrhagiae-man); interstitial nephritis is a persistent lesion in chronic carriers.
  • Listeriosis — internalins bind E-cadherin; centripetal spread via trigeminal nerve to medulla; "circling disease" from brainstem localisation; also causes abortion and monogastric systemic infection with focal liver necrosis.
  • Both — zoonotic, both can cause abortion, both involve invasion of epithelial or endothelial barriers.

Brucellosis, Swine Erysipelas & Campylobacteriosis

These are the classic reproductive-tract bacterial diseases — pathogens with a special affinity for placenta, foetal membranes and genital tissue, producing abortion as their hallmark lesion, alongside skin/joint disease in erysipelas.

Brucellosis EXAM HOT

Synonyms: Mediterranean/gastric/Malta fever, Bang's disease, undulant fever (humans). Etiology: Gram-negative, rod/coccoid Brucella — B. melitensis (goat, 1887), B. abortus (cattle, 1897), B. suis (pigs, 1914), B. ovis (rams, 1950), B. canis (dogs, 1960).

  • Pathogenesis — ingestion → regional lymph node → intestinal epithelial cells → lymphatics → bacteraemia → localises in reproductive organs, placenta, foetus, mammary gland, lymph nodes, spleen, liver, joints, bone; strong affinity for chorio-allantoic trophoblast due to tissue erythritol content → abortion; phagocytosed by macrophages, prevents phagosome-lysosome fusion, survives and grows intracellularly, evading antibodies, until macrophage rupture releases bacteria
  • Transmission (cattle) — contact, ingestion of infective discharge/aborted foetus/placenta, excreted in milk; coitus rare; bulls resistant
  • Clinical signs
    • Cattle — abortion in 7th–8th month; persistent infection if exposed after puberty
    • Pigs — venereal transmission; abortion in 2nd–3rd month; orchitis in boars; skeletal localisation common
    • Sheep/goats — abortion in ewes; orchitis/epididymitis in males
    • Dogs (B. canis) — abortion at ~50 days; male orchitis/epididymitis
    • Horses — B. abortus + Actinomyces bovis co-infection → "poll evil" and "fistulous withers"
  • Lesions — classical lesion is bacterial granuloma in the lymphoreticular system, WITHOUT multinucleated giant cells; cotyledonary necrosis; oedematous intercotyledonary chorion with sticky brownish exudate; opaque, thickened, leathery remaining chorion; induration of udder/supramammary nodes; enlarged hard epididymis/testicle in bulls with possible rupture
  • Species-specific lesions — pigs: tiny white-yellow nodules in all organs; rams: inflamed epididymal tail; bitches: uterine/placental lesions, bronchopneumonia in aborted pups, osteomyelitis; horses: necrotising purulent lesions at the nuchal ligament thoracic attachment
  • Microscopic lesions — bovine: organisms in chorionic epithelial cells, necrosis/exudate with macrophages/neutrophils, endometrial epithelioid cell collections; mammary gland: diffuse infiltration with Langhans giant cells, later atrophy/fibrosis; pigs: typical granulomas with necrosis; rams: perivascular oedema/lymphocytic infiltration, tubular epithelial hyperplasia/degeneration, escaped spermatozoa provoke granulomatous response; bitches: lymph node hyperplasia/plasmacytosis, orchitis, epididymitis, prostatitis, glomerular hyalinisation
  • Diagnosis — symptoms/lesions, agglutination test, immunostaining/molecular probes

Swine Erysipelas ("Diamond Skin Disease") EXAM HOT

Contagious disease of young pigs — acute: high fever, reddish skin spots, internal haemorrhages; chronic: dyspnoea, arthritis, vegetative endocarditis. Etiology: Gram-positive Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae.

  • Susceptibility — pigs 3–6 months most susceptible; also sheep, cattle, birds; causes "erysipeloid" in man
  • Transmission — skin abrasion, alimentary tract, flies
  • Pathogenesis — ingestion → tonsil/intestine → blood → septicaemia; toxin injures vessel walls causing erythematous skin lesions and internal haemorrhage; organism settles in joints/heart valves causing arthritis and vegetative endocarditis
  • Clinical signs — acute febrile illness, high mortality; bright red rhomboid urticarial swellings on abdomen/neck/ear/thigh — "diamond skin disease" → necrosis/crusting/sloughing; hot painful swollen joints with lameness; dyspnoea/cyanosis with cardiac involvement
  • Gross lesions — serosal/organ oedema and haemorrhage; diamond skin lesions bright red then darkening; distended, thickened, fluid-filled joint capsules
  • Microscopic lesions — lymphoid/neutrophil infiltration; cardiac form shows coarse vegetation on the mitral/bicuspid valve occluding the lumen
  • Diagnosis — symptoms/lesions, blood culture

Campylobacteriosis

Caused by several Campylobacter species (formerly Vibrio) — slender, spirally curved, motile, Gram-negative rods causing genital-tract infection/abortion or GI infection.

  • C. foetus sub sp. venerealis — specific bovine venereal disease, spread by coitus/AI; bulls asymptomatic; cows show infertility, prolonged oestrus, endometritis, abortion at 4–7 months; pathogenesis involves antigenic variation of the organism, producing a persistent, waxing-and-waning infection despite IgA/IgG responses; lesion: subacute diffuse mucopurulent endometritis; diagnosis: organism in placental sections by EM/immunostaining
  • C. foetus sub sp. foetus — infects sheep (oral route, spread by birds); localises in the pregnant uterus → bacteraemia → late abortion, stillbirth, weak lambs; lesions: placental haematomas, foetal membrane separation, oedematous cotyledons, foetal maceration, multiple hepatic necrotic foci in the foetus
  • C. jejuni — normal gut inhabitant; causes diarrhoea, abortion in sheep/goats, mastitis in cattle
  • C. sputorum sub sp. mucosalis — causes swine proliferative ileitis (porcine intestinal adenomatosis, PIA)
Chapter recap
  • Brucellosis — erythritol-driven placental tropism, granuloma WITHOUT giant cells, species-specific Brucella strains and hosts.
  • Swine erysipelas — Erysipelothrix, diamond skin lesions, vegetative endocarditis in chronic form.
  • Campylobacteriosis — C. foetus venerealis (bovine venereal disease, antigenic variation causes persistence); C. foetus foetus (ovine abortion); C. jejuni (diarrhoea/abortion/mastitis); C. mucosalis (porcine intestinal adenomatosis).

Enteric Bacterial Diseases — Salmonellosis & Colibacillosis

Salmonella and enteropathogenic E. coli are the two dominant enteric bacterial pathogens of livestock, both spread faeco-orally and producing disease across a spectrum from septicaemia to localised enteritis depending on age and virulence mechanism.

Salmonellosis (Paratyphoid) EXAM HOT

Occurs in all animals, most common in cattle (S. Dublin, S. typhimurium), horses (S. typhimurium), pigs (S. choleraesuis, S. typhimurium); rare in dogs/cats.

  • Transmission — direct/indirect, contaminated feed/water/environment, ingestion of faecal material, carrier animals
  • Pathogenesis — bacteria adhere via fimbriae; invasion genes (induced by low intestinal O₂) enable epithelial cell invasion; proliferates within enterocytes/macrophages; macrophages transport bacteria to lymph nodes; acid-pH-induced bacterial genes aid intracellular growth; disease ranges from mild enteritis to septicaemia
  • Clinical forms
    • Septicaemia — characteristic in newborn foals/calves/guinea pigs; depression, prostration, high fever (105–107°F), death in 24–48 hrs
    • Acute enteritis — common in adults of all species; high fever, diarrhoea/dysentery, tenesmus, abortion, mortality up to 75%, death in 2–5 days
    • Chronic enteritis — pigs, cattle, horses; persistent diarrhoea with blood/mucus/fibrin casts, moderate fever, emaciation
  • Lesions — enterocolitis with hyperaemia, thickening, haemorrhage, ulceration of ileum/colon mucosa; microscopically haemorrhage, oedema, necrosis, leucocyte infiltration; multiple hepatic necrotic foci with "paratyphoid nodules" (small reticuloendothelial aggregates); splenic/lymph node hyperplasia; septicaemic form shows fibrinoid vessel wall necrosis and glomerular hyaline; ulcerative proctitis; abortion — oedematous placenta with focal haemorrhage/necrosis, foetal oedema
  • Diagnosis — organism isolation/identification, ELISA, DNA probe

Colibacillosis

Three distinct pathogenetic mechanisms of enteropathogenic Escherichia coli:

  • Enterotoxic colibacillosis — colonisation of the intestinal surface and toxin production; most common in young piglets, calves, lambs during the first week of life
  • Enteroinvasive colibacillosis — less common, seen in pigs and cattle; organism penetrates enterocytes, lamina propria, and extends into mesenteric lymph nodes
  • Enteroadherent colibacillosis — pigs and calves; non-toxin-producing serotypes penetrate the glycoprotein coat, adhere to mucosal cells, and destroy the microvillus

Oedema Disease of Swine

Synonym: Enterotoxaemic colibacillosis.

  • Affects healthy pigs; incoordination, limb paralysis, diarrhoea; morbidity 35%, mortality 100%
  • "Oedema disease toxin" (Shiga-like toxin) enters circulation → necrotising arteritis
  • Oedema of stomach wall and colon; fluid accumulation within body cavities

Septicaemic Colibacillosis

  • Organism invades via oral cavity, respiratory system, pharynx, umbilicus; produces endotoxin
  • Signs/lesions — bacterial arthritis, polyserositis (pericarditis, pleuritis, peritonitis), meningitis, ophthalmitis, pyelonephritis, emboli, purulent/fibrinous exudates
  • Notably — no diarrhoea or intestinal lesions in this form
Chapter recap
  • Salmonellosis — three clinical forms (septicaemic in neonates, acute enteritis in adults, chronic enteritis); paratyphoid nodules in liver are diagnostic.
  • Colibacillosis — three mechanisms: enterotoxic (toxin only), enteroinvasive (tissue penetration), enteroadherent (microvillus destruction, no toxin).
  • Oedema disease — Shiga-like toxin, necrotising arteritis, near-universal mortality despite moderate morbidity.
  • Septicaemic colibacillosis — the key exception with NO diarrhoea/gut lesions — purely systemic polyserositis.

Haemophilus Diseases of Swine, Cattle, Sheep & Horses

Haemophilus species are normal oropharyngeal/genital commensals that become opportunistic pathogens under stress, producing a family of serosal/CNS/reproductive diseases across pigs, cattle, sheep and horses.

Contagious Pleuropneumonia of Swine

Etiology: Haemophilus pleuropneumoniae; endotoxin-mediated lesions; respiratory spread.

  • Susceptible: pigs up to 6 months; death within 1–2 days; mortality 50%
  • Clinical signs — fever, respiratory distress, blood-tinged nasal discharge
  • Lesions — fibrinopurulent pneumonia, fibrinous pleuritis, sequestration of lung, meningitis, arthritis
  • Microscopic — alveolar/interlobular oedema, lymphatic dilation, congestion, haemorrhage, intravascular fibrinous thrombus

Glässer's Disease

Etiology: Haemophilus parasuis, normal oropharyngeal resident; affects young pigs, usually after stress.

  • Clinical signs — fever, lameness, respiratory distress, neurological signs
  • Lesions — polyserositis (pericarditis, peritonitis, pleuritis, meningitis, swollen joints)
  • Microscopic — fibrinous inflammation of all serosal surfaces

Infectious Thrombo-Embolic Meningoencephalitis of Cattle (ITEME) EXAM HOT

Etiology: Haemophilus somnus, normal inhabitant of nasal cavity, prepuce, female genital tract of healthy cattle.

  • Rapid course — fever, weakness, staggering, somnolence, ataxia, blindness, paralysis; occasionally pneumonia/arthritis; very high mortality
  • Characteristic lesion — multiple areas of necrosis and haemorrhage in the brain
  • Microscopic — vasculitis, thrombosis, infarction; meningitis with fibrinopurulent exudate

Haemophilus Septicaemia of Lambs

Etiology: Haemophilus agni.

  • Clinical signs — fever, depression, reluctance to move; course of illness ~12 hrs before death
  • Gross lesions — haemorrhages throughout the body, focal hepatic necrosis, splenomegaly

Contagious Equine Metritis (CEM)

Etiology: Haemophilus equigenitalis; spread by coitus, persists in the equine genital tract.

  • Mucopurulent vaginal discharge
  • Purulent necrotising endometritis
Chapter recap
  • Haemophilus diseases share a theme — normal commensal organisms turning pathogenic under stress, producing serosal (polyserositis) or vascular/CNS damage.
  • Contagious pleuropneumonia — 50% mortality, fibrinopurulent pneumonia with sequestration.
  • Glässer's disease — H. parasuis, polyserositis of young stressed pigs.
  • ITEME — H. somnus, thrombotic/haemorrhagic brain necrosis, very high mortality.
  • CEM — H. equigenitalis, venereal, purulent necrotising endometritis.

Mycoplasmal Diseases — Enzootic Pneumonia, CBPP & CCPP

Mycoplasma organisms are the smallest free-living microbes, lacking a cell wall, mostly host-specific and non-invasive, colonising mucosal surfaces. They produce chronic, high-morbidity/low-mortality respiratory diseases in swine, cattle, and goats.

Mycoplasma basics

  • Smallest free-living microbes (300–800 nm), no cell wall, bound only by plasma membrane
  • Mostly host-specific; colonise mucosal surfaces, non-invasive
  • Spread by aerosol, venereal route, or in utero

Porcine Enzootic Pneumonia

Etiology: Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae, M. hyorhinis, M. flocculare.

  • Clinical signs — subnormal weight gain, chronic cough, high morbidity, low mortality
  • Gross lesions — apical/cardiac lobe consolidation and atelectasis, enlarged peribronchial nodes, serofibrinous pleuritis/peritonitis/pericarditis
  • Microscopic — heavy peribronchial/peribronchiolar lymphocyte/plasma cell cuffing, hyperactive mucus cells, neutrophil/lymphocyte/plasma cell accumulation in and between alveoli

Contagious Bovine Pleuropneumonia (CBPP) EXAM HOT

Subacute/chronic disease of cattle with diffuse pneumonia, "marbling of lung," and sequestration. Etiology: Mycoplasma mycoides (a "pleuropneumonia-like organism," PPLO).

  • Calves less susceptible than adults
  • Pathogenesis — droplet spread → bronchiolar inflammation → interlobular septal thickening → alveolar involvement → thrombosis and well-demarcated necrotic sequestra; violent coughing ruptures the fibrous capsule, releasing organisms and spreading infection; ~a quarter of affected herd animals become carriers ("lungers")
  • Clinical signs — acute: dry painful cough, laboured respiration; later: mucopurulent nasal discharge, joint swelling (polyarthritis)
  • Lesions — usually one lung affected; excess pleural fluid with thoracic wall adhesion; cut surface shows "marbled appearance" from interlobular septal exudate infiltration; later necrotic sequestration surrounded by dense connective tissue; abscess formation with lymphocyte/plasma cell infiltration; heavily thickened septa separate lobules into distinct compartments; perivascular/peribronchial lymphocyte-plasma cell cuffing
  • Diagnosis — history, clinical signs, gross/microscopic lesions, organism recovery

Contagious Caprine Pleuropneumonia (CCPP)

Acute, highly fatal disease of goats — upper respiratory involvement, marked chest exudation, pericarditis. Etiology: Borrelomyces peripneumoniae capri / Mycoplasma mycoides var. capri.

  • Clinical signs — dyspnoea, nasal discharge (watery → mucopurulent)
  • Lesions — usually unilateral rhinitis, fibrinous pleuropneumonia, pleurisy, pericarditis; marbling not marked and sequestration NOT a feature (key contrast with CBPP); abundant chest fluid that clots on air exposure
  • Diagnosis — symptoms/lesions, organism isolation from oedema fluid; subcutaneous inoculation of lymph into healthy goats produces local oedematous swelling
Chapter recap
  • Mycoplasma — no cell wall, smallest free-living microbe, non-invasive mucosal coloniser.
  • Porcine enzootic pneumonia — chronic cough, high morbidity/low mortality, apical/cardiac lobe consolidation.
  • CBPP — cattle, "marbled" lung with sequestration, carrier "lungers."
  • CCPP — goats, unilateral rhinitis + pleuropneumonia, NO marbling/sequestration — this is the key CBPP-vs-CCPP differentiator.

Rickettsial Diseases — Anaplasmosis, Q Fever & Ehrlichiosis

Rickettsiae are minute, Gram-negative, obligate intracellular parasites transmitted mainly by arthropod vectors (often with transovarian transmission), with an affinity for endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle, or circulating blood cells — producing vasculitis or blood-cell parasitism as their core lesion.

Rickettsial basics

  • Minute, Gram-negative, obligate intracellular; multiply only within cells
  • Transmitted by arthropod vectors between species; transovarian transmission is common in the vector
  • Affinity for endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle → vasculitis complicated by thrombi and haemorrhage

Anaplasmosis (Gall Sickness) EXAM HOT

Acute or chronic disease of adult cattle — anaemia, extreme weakness, high fever, icterus WITHOUT haemoglobinuria. Etiology: Anaplasma marginale or A. centrale.

  • Transmission — ticks, biting flies, mosquitoes
  • Pathogenesis — invades erythrocytes as initial bodies, develops and infects further RBCs; does NOT cause haemolysis directly — infected erythrocytes are removed by splenic reticuloendothelial phagocytosis, hence no haemoglobinuria
  • Clinical signs — young animals resistant; adults show fever, depression, reduced production, anaemia, icterus, dehydration
  • Haematology — falling erythrocyte count with anisocytosis, poikilocytosis, punctate basophilia; reduced haemoglobin/PCV
  • Gross lesions — severe anaemia, pale mucosa, icterus; markedly enlarged reddish spleen; enlarged liver with rounded edges, distended gall bladder with granular bile; epicardial petechiae, catarrhal gastroenteritis
  • Microscopic lesions — bone marrow hyperplasia, extramedullary haematopoiesis in spleen/liver
  • Diagnosis — A. marginale = coccoid form at the periphery of erythrocytes (more virulent); A. centrale = toward the centre of the cell (milder disease); CFT, FAT

Q Fever

Febrile disease of slaughterhouse workers, first reported Queensland, Australia. Etiology: Coxiella burnetii (Rickettsia burnetii) — minute pleomorphic intracellular rods.

  • Humans acquire infection from freshly slaughtered cattle/sheep or raw milk/butter; high organism concentration in placenta
  • Clinical signs in animals are inapparent/mild; organism can be recovered from asymptomatic cows; in humans — interstitial pneumonia, disseminated epithelioid granulomas
  • Diagnosis — guinea pig inoculation; Giemsa-Machiavello staining of tissue

Ovine and Bovine Ehrlichiosis (Tick-Borne Fever)

Mild, non-fatal febrile disease associated with tick infestation. Etiology: Ehrlichia phagocytophilia. Transmitted by Ixodes ricinus.

  • Clinical signs — fever, lymphopaenia, neutropaenia, thrombocytopaenia
  • Lesion — splenomegaly
  • Diagnosis — organism identification in circulating granulocytes/monocytes (Giemsa/Leishman stain)

Potomac Horse Fever (Equine Monocytic Ehrlichiosis)

Etiology: Ehrlichia risticii. First reported Potomac River, USA, 1979. Seasonal (summer), arthropod-vectored.

  • Clinical signs — biphasic fever, anorexia, colic, diarrhoea, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia (organism seen in circulating monocytes), anaemia, dehydration, shock; mortality ~30%
  • Lesions — no diagnostic lesion; caecal/colonic haemorrhage, watery gastric/small-intestinal contents, enlarged haemorrhagic mesenteric nodes

Canine Ehrlichiosis

Etiology: Ehrlichia canis. Reported in India. Transmitted by Rhipicephalus sanguineus.

  • Pathogenesis — multiplies in reticuloendothelial cells, lymphocytes, monocytes; three intracellular forms — initial bodies, mulberry bodies/morulae, elementary bodies
  • Clinical signs — recurrent fever, serous nasal discharge, photophobia, vomiting, splenomegaly, leucopoenia, thrombocytopenia, anaemia
  • Lesions — GI/urogenital/renal mucosal haemorrhage, oedematous haemorrhagic lymph nodes, limb oedema; perivascular lymphoreticular/plasma cell cuffing in meninges/kidney/liver/lymphoid tissue; bone marrow hypoplasia; hepatic necrosis
  • Diagnosis — organism demonstration in circulating monocyte or colonic epithelium; indirect FAT

Equine Ehrlichiosis

Etiology: Ehrlichia equi. Occurs in USA. Transmitted via blood/ticks.

  • Clinical signs — fever, anorexia, leg oedema, ataxia, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia, raised icteric index, lymphopenia
  • Lesions — petechiae/ecchymoses, oedema of muscle/fascia/subcutis, jaundice, orchitis
  • Diagnosis — organism in neutrophils/eosinophils, indirect IFT, EM
Chapter recap
  • Anaplasmosis — no haemoglobinuria (splenic phagocytosis, not haemolysis); A. marginale (peripheral, virulent) vs A. centrale (central, mild).
  • Q fever — Coxiella burnetii, zoonotic slaughterhouse hazard, high placental concentration.
  • Ehrlichia species by host — phagocytophilia (sheep/cattle tick-borne fever), risticii (Potomac horse fever), canis (dogs), equi (horses).
  • Shared blood picture — most Ehrlichia infections cause leucopenia and thrombocytopenia; diagnosis relies on finding intracellular inclusions in circulating cells.

Chlamydial Diseases — Sporadic Bovine Encephalomyelitis, Polyarthritis & Enzootic Abortion

Chlamydia organisms are non-motile, spherical, Gram-negative obligate intracellular bacteria with a unique two-form developmental cycle, producing a spectrum of disease from CNS infection to joint disease and, most importantly, abortion in small ruminants.

Chlamydial biology

  • Multiply only within host cells; non-motile, spherical, Gram-negative, possess a cell wall
  • Two forms — Elementary Body (EB): never divides, attaches and enters the host cell; Reticulate Body (RB): intracellular, metabolically active, non-infectious, divides by binary fission
  • Developmental cycle — EB attaches/penetrates → converts to metabolically active RB → RB multiplies by binary fission → RB matures back into infectious EB progeny
  • Special stains — Macchiavello (red), Castaneda (blue), Giemsa (dark purple); also wet mounts/phase contrast

Sporadic Bovine Encephalomyelitis EXAM HOT

Etiology: Chlamydia psittaci. Young calves mainly affected, rarely older cattle.

  • Clinical signs — sudden onset, fever 105–107°F, anorexia, depression, excessive salivation, nasal discharge, dyspnoea, cough, diarrhoea, fetlock stiffness, circling/staggering, falling with head extended, paralysis, death in 5–7 days
  • Gross lesions — serofibrinous peritonitis; fibrinous exudate over omentum, liver, spleen, pleura; brain/spinal cord oedema
  • Microscopic lesions — fibrinous peritonitis/pericarditis; severe meningoencephalomyelitis; elementary bodies (Levinthal-Colle-Lillie/LCL bodies) seen in mononuclear cells of serosal exudate and in brain/spinal cord
  • Diagnosis — gross/microscopic lesions, demonstration of elementary bodies

Polyarthritis in Calves

Caused by Chlamydia psittaci.

  • Involves all limb joints plus vertebral and mandibular articulation; joints swollen, synovium oedematous/thickened, synovial fluid contains fibrin flakes
  • Elementary bodies seen in mononuclear cells and synovial cells on Giemsa-stained smears

Enzootic Abortion of Ewes EXAM HOT

Caused by Chlamydia psittaci; also affects goats; of zoonotic importance.

  • Causes severe septicaemia and abortion in pregnant animals; in sheep, abortion occurs in the last month of gestation
  • Placentitis is the main lesion — dark red cotyledons; uterine chorionic surface covered with flaky yellow exudate
  • Microscopic — focal necrosis, oedema, mononuclear cell infiltration; cytoplasmic elementary bodies visible in tissue sections

Chlamydial Abortion in Cattle

Chlamydia psittaci also causes bovine abortion via placentitis; diagnosis by demonstration of elementary bodies.

Other Chlamydial Infections

  • Chlamydial pneumonia of cattle and sheep
  • Polyarthritis of sheep
  • Feline pneumonitis
  • Polyarthritis in foals
  • Follicular conjunctivitis of sheep
Chapter recap
  • Chlamydia cycle — infectious, non-dividing Elementary Body → metabolically active, dividing Reticulate Body → new Elementary Bodies.
  • All C. psittaci diseases covered here (encephalomyelitis, polyarthritis, enzootic abortion, bovine abortion) are caused by the SAME organism, differing by target tissue.
  • LCL (Levinthal-Colle-Lillie) bodies — the elementary bodies seen microscopically, diagnostic across all these syndromes.
  • Enzootic abortion of ewes — the most economically important chlamydial disease; zoonotic; placentitis with flaky yellow exudate is the classic lesion.

General Pathology of Mycotic Infections & Superficial Mycoses

Fungal pathology is understood first through the five-group classification of fungal disease, then applied to the only obligate parasitic fungi of animals — the dermatophytes, which cause superficial, non-invasive Ring Worm and its chronic variant, Favus.

Fungal biology & classification of fungal disease EXAM HOT

  • Fungi are plant-like pathogens; large cells with thick walls containing ergosterol and polysaccharides
  • Produce pathology ranging from acute pyogenic to chronic granulomatous infection; produce mycotoxins and enzymes; some induce strong Type III/IV hypersensitivity
  • Immunosuppression, leucopenia, corticosteroids favour fungal infection; most fungi are saprophytes and non-pathogenic

Five groups of fungal disease

  • 1. Obligate parasites — dermatophytes — live in the keratin layer of skin causing "ringworm" (superficial mycosis); do not live as saprophytes; spread is direct and non-invasive; do NOT produce granulomas
  • 2. Systemic/deep mycoses — establish in otherwise normal individuals, e.g. histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, blastomycosis, sporotrichosis; live as mycelial or yeast forms in soil/plants
  • 3. Subcutaneous mycoses ("mycetomas") — debilitated/immunocompromised hosts most susceptible; classical granulomas; saprophytic fungi enter through skin, grow in mycelial form, localised lesions
  • 4. Localised AND disseminated disease — e.g. aspergillosis, zygomycosis; more common in debilitated animals
  • 5. Yeasts (single-celled fungi) — not highly pathogenic, e.g. Candidiasis, Cryptococcosis; superficial infection, rarely systemic

Lesions & host defence

  • Fungi are seen with H&E or special stains — Gomori methenamine silver, PAS, Gridley stain
  • Splendore-Hoeppli material — homogenous, brightly eosinophilic material surrounding organisms/colonies, believed to be a host antigen-antibody complex product
  • Deep fungal lesions are classical granulomas — central necrotic/purulent core surrounded by epithelioid cells, macrophages, multinucleated giant cells, variable lymphocytes/plasma cells/eosinophils
  • Superficial mycoses do NOT form granulomas — organisms are restricted to superficial skin layers and are non-invasive
  • Defence — alternate complement pathway is the first line against invasive fungi (Aspergillus, Candida); small spores destroyed by macrophages/NK cells; T-cell mediated immunity is required for clearance; some T/NK cells directly kill yeasts like Candida albicans and Cryptococcus neoformans

Ring Worm (Dermatomycosis) EXAM HOT

Superficial infection of the keratinised skin layers and appendages, affecting all species.

  • Etiology — Microsporum (more common in dogs) vs Trichophyton (large animals)
    • Microsporum grows around the hair root, forming a mosaic spore pattern (ectothrix); fluoresces under UV (Wood's lamp)
    • Trichophyton grows both within the hair shaft (endothrix) and outside (ectothrix), producing spores in long chains; does NOT fluoresce
  • Transmission — contact with infected animals; incubation 1 week – 1 month
  • Pathogenesis — fungus grows on keratinised tissue and the hair follicle, splitting it; causes hyperaemia, oedema, vesiculation, parakeratosis; secondary bacterial infection causes follicular suppuration
  • Gross lesions — raised, round, crusty, alopecic patches 2–3 cm diameter; cattle: head/neck/anal region; pigs: trunk; sheep/goat: head/neck/back/shoulders/chest (sheep), face/pinna (goats); dogs/cats: any part; horses: head/neck/chest/shoulder/back; camel: legs/neck
  • Microscopic lesions — mild-moderate mononuclear-to-neutrophilic inflammation in the stratum corneum
  • Diagnosis — characteristic lesions, fungal demonstration, Wood's lamp

Favus ("Honeycomb" Ring Worm)

Chronic dermatophytosis caused by Trichophyton schoenleinii, Microsporum gypseum, Microsporum gallinae.

  • Dogs — head and paws most affected; cats — ears affected
  • Gross lesions — dense felt-like fungal aggregates with central depression; crust removal reveals a cup-shaped depression with raised borders; confluent lesions give a "honeycombed" appearance
  • Microscopic lesions — fungus confined to the horny layer; intense hyperaemia and cellular infiltration in the corium
  • Diagnosis — microscopic examination of skin scrapings for spores and mycelia
Chapter recap
  • Five fungal disease groups — obligate dermatophytes (no granuloma) → systemic/deep mycoses → subcutaneous mycetomas → localised+disseminated → opportunistic yeasts.
  • Splendore-Hoeppli material — eosinophilic host antigen-antibody complex coating fungal organisms/colonies.
  • Ringworm — Microsporum (ectothrix, UV-fluorescent) vs Trichophyton (endo+ectothrix, non-fluorescent).
  • Favus — honeycomb crust appearance, confined to the horny layer.
  • Key rule — superficial dermatophytoses never form granulomas; deep mycoses always do.
Chapter 28

Systemic Mycoses I — Aspergillosis & Coccidioidomycosis

Both diseases in this chapter are classic deep/systemic mycoses producing granulomatous pulmonary and disseminated disease — Aspergillosis primarily in birds, Coccidioidomycosis in mammals including man.

Aspergillosis EXAM HOT

Synonyms: Brooder pneumonia, mycotic pneumonia. Affects mainly poultry, occasionally mammals — dyspnoea, diarrhoea, granulomatous lesions. Etiology: Aspergillus fumigatus (and other Aspergillus spp.).

  • Transmission — inhalation of spores; chicks can hatch already infected since the fungus invades the egg; common in brooder-raised chicks, hence "brooder pneumonia"
  • Gross lesions — birds show diffuse pneumonia, nodular pulmonary form, or diffuse air sac infection; can also involve GI tract, liver, spleen, kidney, ovaries; lung shows greyish nodules with hyperaemic zone, bluish mycelial/spore centre; air sac thickened with white mouldy growth
  • Microscopic lesions — granuloma with central caseation containing organisms; initial neutrophil/macrophage infiltration, later eosinophils, epithelioid cells, foreign body giant cells, fibrous capsule
  • Cattle — nodular lung lesions; placentitis in pregnant animals causing abortion (organism isolable from foetal stomach)
  • Diagnosis — nodule content examination, histology with PAS stain demonstrating club-like conidiospores of A. fumigatus

Coccidioidomycosis (Coccidioidal Granuloma)

Granulomatous disease caused by Coccidioides immitis — affects man, dog, cattle, sheep, horse, pig; comparatively benign in animals.

  • Transmission — inhalation of spores, ingestion, cutaneous wounds; fungus produces mycelia on culture but not in tissue — instead forms spherules (sporangia), 5–50 µm, double-contoured wall
  • Clinical signs — horses: emaciation, leg oedema, anaemia, leucocytosis; dogs: fever, cough, dyspnoea
  • Gross lesions — lung/lymph node lesions resemble tuberculosis: granulomatous nodules with caseous centres, some calcified; lymph node suppurative foci with thick yellow pus; disseminated canine form: grey nodules in lung, lymph nodes, liver, spleen, bone marrow, meninges
  • Microscopic lesions — granuloma with spherules surrounded by epithelioid cells; in cattle, central area resembles actinomycotic rosettes with acidophilic clubs; ruptured sporangia release young spherules → neutrophil predominance; mature spherules → epithelioid cell predominance (both exudative and proliferative reactions present); organisms may be seen within Langhans giant cells; Gridley's stain highlights the double-contoured wall
  • Diagnosis — fungal demonstration in lesions; intradermal "coccidioidin" sensitivity test
Chapter recap
  • Aspergillosis — A. fumigatus, "brooder pneumonia" in poultry (egg-transmitted), nodular granuloma with club-shaped conidiospores.
  • Coccidioidomycosis — C. immitis, spherules with double-contoured wall, lesions closely mimic TB.
  • Shared theme — both cause pulmonary granulomas; the fungal form seen in tissue (conidiospores vs spherules) is the key differentiating diagnostic feature.
Chapter 29

Systemic Mycoses II — Candidiasis, Histoplasmosis & Epizootic Lymphangitis

This chapter covers a mucosal opportunist (Candidiasis) alongside two related organisms in the same genus — Histoplasma capsulatum (systemic Histoplasmosis) and its equine-adapted relative Histoplasma farciminosum (Epizootic Lymphangitis).

Candidiasis (Moniliasis, Thrush)

Sporadic disease of the upper alimentary tract in man, swine, poultry (thrush) and the mammary gland in dairy animals. Etiology: Candida sp.

  • Hypovitaminosis A and prolonged antibiotic use predispose; in poultry, young chicks/turkeys are affected — oesophagus, crop, proventriculus
  • Clinical signs — listless, stunted, poor feathering
  • Gross lesions — crop shows a "Turkish towel" appearance with raised ulcerated patches and pseudomembrane; diphtheritic membrane in small intestine
  • Microscopic lesions — pseudohyphae penetrate the keratinised squamous crop epithelium roughly perpendicularly; lymphocyte/histiocyte submucosal infiltration
  • Diagnosis — demonstration of the causative fungus in lesions

Histoplasmosis

Infectious granulomatous disease of man, dogs, cattle, horses, sheep (dogs most often affected). Etiology: Histoplasma capsulatum.

  • Whitish-brown organism with two spore sizes — microconidia (3–4 µm), macroconidia (8–12 µm); grows as mycelium in soil/room temperature, as a yeast form in culture at 37°C and in the body
  • Transmission — soil-borne, inhaled
  • Clinical signs — two forms: benign inapparent, or fatal disseminated (emaciation, diarrhoea, vomiting, ascites, chronic cough, anorexia, pyrexia, anaemia, lymph node swelling, spleno-/hepatomegaly)
  • Gross lesions — discrete nodules, organomegaly (spleen, lymph nodes, liver, adrenals); pulmonary nodules
  • Microscopic lesions — marked proliferation of reticuloendothelial cells forming macrophages and epithelioid cells; cell swelling, degeneration, karyolysis; lymphocyte/plasma cell infiltration; no giant cell formation; benign form has a fibrous capsule, malignant form lacks capsulation
  • Diagnosis — histology of RE cells, culture, "histoplasmin" skin test, CFT, AGPT

Epizootic Lymphangitis EXAM HOT

Disease of skin and superficial lymphatics of horses, mules, donkeys (rarely humans); endemic in India. Etiology: Histoplasma farciminosum — yeast-shaped in tissue, cannot be microscopically differentiated from H. capsulatum.

  • Transmission — unknown; likely requires infective material contacting previously injured skin
  • Clinical/gross features — chronic indurative skin ulceration especially on limbs, thickened superficial lymphatics, enlarged regional lymph nodes, abscess formation, purulent discharge, followed by new indolent ulcers; can present as conjunctivitis or naso-lacrimal infection; rarely generalises to internal organs; chronic course over months but usually resolves
  • Microscopic lesions — granulomatous cutaneous reaction with abundant macrophages, sometimes multinucleated giant cells; cytoplasm distended with oval organisms (~2×3 µm) enveloped by a thin clear unstained capsule; central fungal mass demonstrable with H&E; lymphocytes, plasma cells, some neutrophils; secondary bacterial infection leads to draining abscesses
  • Diagnosis — demonstration of typical fungi in tissue sections, cultures, stained exudate smears
Chapter recap
  • Candidiasis — "Turkish towel" crop appearance in poultry, predisposed by hypovitaminosis A/antibiotics.
  • Histoplasmosis — H. capsulatum, yeast form in tissue, dogs most affected, NO giant cells (contrast with most granulomas).
  • Epizootic lymphangitis — H. farciminosum, equine skin/lymphatic disease, endemic in India, indistinguishable microscopically from H. capsulatum.
  • Key exam point — Histoplasmosis lacks giant cells, unlike TB, Coccidioidomycosis, and most other granulomatous mycoses.
Chapter 30

Opportunistic & Invasive Mycoses — Cryptococcosis & Zygomycosis

Two contrasting patterns of opportunistic fungal disease: Cryptococcosis, a slow-growing encapsulated yeast forming classic granulomas in the respiratory tract and CNS, and Zygomycosis, a rapidly angio-invasive mould causing thrombosis and infarction rather than organised granulomas.

Cryptococcosis (Torulosis)

Disease of many species caused by a yeast-like fungus, usually affecting the respiratory tract and CNS. Etiology: Cryptococcus neoformans.

  • Transmission — inhalation; opportunistic in immunocompromised animals
  • Susceptibility — cattle, sheep, pigs, horses, cats, dogs, and other species
  • Gross lesions — typical granulomas project from and displace organs; affects any part of the respiratory tract from external nares to lungs; also cerebrospinal meninges and brain
  • Microscopic lesions — granulomas contain masses of proliferating organisms; fungi are ovoid/spherical, thick-walled, yeast-like; capsule stains selectively with PAS and mucicarmine techniques (glycogen); rapid multiplication produces cystic lesions especially in brain, adrenal, lymph nodes, mammary gland
  • Diagnosis — characteristic microscopic lesions, organism demonstration in tissue sections

Zygomycosis (Phycomycosis) EXAM HOT

Disorders caused by fungi of the class Zygomycetes — saprophytes of decaying vegetation that become pathogenic with intercurrent disease, long-term antibiotics, or immunosuppression.

  • Gross lesions — involves skin/oral/nasal mucosa; lungs, placenta, mammary gland, rumen, stomach, kidney, brain are common sites; Mucormycosis: cattle — placentitis, abortion, rumen/abomasal infection; horses — placentitis/abortion; dogs — digestive tract lesions
  • Microscopic lesions — diffuse, NOT granuloma-forming; highly invasive, spreads through blood vessels causing thrombosis, infarction, and disseminated infection; lesions show macrophages, multinucleated giant cells, necrosis, neutrophils resembling pyogranulomatous inflammation with necrosis and pus; fungi appear as pleomorphic, branching, basophilic-staining hyphae on H&E
  • Diagnosis — demonstration of characteristic organisms in the (pyogranulomatous, not classical granulomatous) lesions
Chapter recap
  • Cryptococcosis — encapsulated yeast, PAS/mucicarmine-positive capsule, classic granulomas, respiratory/CNS tropism.
  • Zygomycosis — angio-invasive branching hyphae, causes thrombosis/infarction, diffuse pyogranulomatous (NOT classical granulomatous) reaction.
  • Key contrast — Cryptococcus forms organised granulomas; Zygomycetes are vessel-invasive and diffuse — this distinction is a favourite exam trap.
Chapter 31

Mycotoxicosis — Aflatoxicosis

Mycotoxicoses are disorders from ingesting fungi or their toxic metabolites in feed — distinct from true fungal infections, since the fungus itself need not invade the host. Aflatoxicosis is the best-studied and historically most important mycotoxicosis.

Mycotoxicosis — general concept

  • Disease/death from ingesting fungi or their toxins (mycotoxins) in/on feed
  • No common clinical or pathological features across mycotoxicoses — many are hepatotoxic, but effects range from neoplasia to neurological dysfunction
  • Interest expanded rapidly after the 1960s discovery of aflatoxicosis

Aflatoxicosis EXAM HOT

Highly toxic and carcinogenic metabolites of Aspergillus flavus and A. parasiticus, produced on cereal grains, groundnuts and seeds under favourable conditions.

  • At least 17 aflatoxins exist, structurally related to coumarins; most important: B1, B2, G1, G2, M1 (B/G = blue/green UV fluorescence); B1 is most prevalent and most toxic; M1 (a B1 metabolite) is excreted in milk
  • Most commonly contaminated foods — groundnuts, maize, cottonseed
  • History — first described 1952 as "Hepatitis X" in dogs; traced (1955) to contaminated groundnut meal in dog food; "Turkey X disease" (1961, UK) killed over 100,000 turkeys, leading to identification of Brazilian groundnut meal and eventual chemical isolation of aflatoxins

Susceptibility factors

  • Young animals more susceptible than adults; males more than females; poor nutrition increases susceptibility
  • Young turkeys/ducklings much more susceptible than chickens; rats more susceptible than mice (especially to carcinogenesis)
  • Natural disease reported in dogs, cats, pigs, cattle; rare in sheep/goats; horses relatively resistant

Mechanism

  • Binds nucleic acids, disrupts polyribosomes → interferes with nucleic acid and protein synthesis; impairs T-cell function
  • Covalently binds cellular DNA → mutations in proto-oncogenes/tumour suppressor genes, particularly p53 — a susceptible site is the guanosine at codon 249 of p53, causing a G→T transversion, commonly found in hepatocellular carcinomas

Acute aflatoxicosis

  • From ingesting large quantities; severe hepatic necrosis within hours; jaundice, widespread haemorrhage, elevated serum hepatic enzymes
  • Necrosis pattern varies by species — periportal (turkeys), midzonal (ducklings, chickens, cats, adult rats), centrilobular (rabbits — actually stated as pigs/cattle/dogs/guinea-pigs)
  • Oedema and haemorrhage of gallbladder wall consistent in pigs and dogs

Chronic aflatoxicosis

  • More common form; lower dose over time; effects develop over days–months, pathological changes seen within a week
  • Signs — decreased growth rate, lowered productivity, eventual liver disease signs
  • Most striking/consistent lesion in all species — marked proliferation of small bile ductules at the periphery of hepatic lobules
  • Hepatocyte changes — fatty change, swelling, necrosis (less extensive than acute); progressive connective tissue proliferation → periportal fibrosis/cirrhosis; nodular regeneration with megalocytic hepatocytes
  • Carcinogenicity is well established — hepatomas, hepatocellular carcinomas, cholangiocarcinomas produced experimentally in ducklings, turkeys, rats, guinea-pigs, trout, sheep, pigs, monkeys; also oesophageal/gastric/colonic/renal carcinoma in rats; among domestic animals, hepatocellular carcinoma from natural aflatoxin poisoning has been observed only in pigs

Diagnosis

  • Detection of aflatoxin in feed/blood serum plus characteristic gross/histopathological liver findings
  • Biological testing — bile duct hyperplasia in duckling liver; death/abnormalities in hatched chicks given aflatoxin via a shell hole into the air sac
  • Chemical testing — fluorotoxinometer for total aflatoxins; ELISA for aflatoxin B1; dot-ELISA for B1/B2/G1 in maize/cottonseed/groundnut; dot immunobinding assay; column/thin-layer chromatography
Chapter recap
  • Aflatoxin B1 — most prevalent/toxic; M1 is the milk-excreted metabolite; produced by A. flavus/A. parasiticus.
  • Mechanism — inhibits protein/nucleic acid synthesis; p53 codon 249 mutation links aflatoxin to hepatocellular carcinoma.
  • Acute vs chronic — acute = massive hepatic necrosis in hours; chronic = bile ductule proliferation + fibrosis/cirrhosis + megalocytic hepatocytes.
  • Susceptibility — young > adult, male > female, ducklings/turkeys > chickens, pigs are the only domestic species with documented natural hepatocellular carcinoma.
  • Diagnosis — duckling bile duct hyperplasia bioassay + ELISA/chromatography confirmation.

Neurotoxic & Gangrenous Mycotoxicoses

This chapter groups three mycotoxicoses that share a striking clinical presentation — nervous system dysfunction or peripheral vascular gangrene — from Dallis grass poisoning, mouldy corn poisoning (Equine Leukoencephalomalacia), and Ergot poisoning.

Dallis Grass Poisoning

Caused by Claviceps paspali growing on Dallis grass (a US pasture plant); affects cattle, sheep, horses.

  • The ergot (sclerotium) is smaller and browner than that of Claviceps purpurea
  • Clinical signs — mainly nervous: hyperirritability, excitability, muscular incoordination; many animals recover with a feed change
  • Gross lesions are few; microscopic studies not well characterised

Equine Leukoencephalomalacia (Mouldy Corn Poisoning) EXAM HOT

A specific syndrome of horses and donkeys among the many "mouldy corn poisoning" syndromes caused by various mycotoxin-producing moulds contaminating corn.

  • Clinical signs — drowsiness, circling, progressing to paralysis
  • Gross lesions — softening and liquefactive necrosis, chiefly of the white matter of the cerebrum; oedema and congestion, but little cellular inflammatory reaction

Ergot Poisoning (Ergotism)

Caused by Claviceps purpurea, parasitising small grains and many grasses; the sclerotium (hard black elongated body) is "ergot," replacing the seed/grain of the maturing plant.

  • Toxins — derivatives of lysergic acid (ergotamine) and isolysergic acid (ergocristine); act by stimulating adrenergic nerves and smooth muscle (basis for its oxytocic use on the uterus); prolonged vascular smooth muscle contraction is the main cause of poisoning
  • Gangrenous form (chronic) — dry gangrene of limbs, tail, ears; after weeks of ingestion, distal extremities may slough; early signs: lameness, irregular gait, foot pain; affected parts are cold and insensitive; clear demarcation line with a proximal inflammatory zone; occasionally moist rather than dry gangrene; in birds, comb/tongue/beak become gangrenous
  • Other signs — GI musculature involvement (indigestion, colic, vomiting, diarrhoea/constipation); abortion in pregnant animals; agalactia (may be the only sign in pigs)
  • Spasmodic form (rare) — convulsive/nervous form: toxic flexor contractions, muscle trembling, opisthotonus, tetanic spasms, convulsions, death — believed due to failing CNS blood supply
  • Lesions — seen in gangrenous cases; congestion and sometimes haemorrhage in viscera; gangrenous ergotism is identical in appearance to fescue poisoning
Chapter recap
  • Dallis grass poisoning — Claviceps paspali; purely nervous signs, usually recovers with feed change.
  • Equine leukoencephalomalacia — mouldy corn; liquefactive necrosis of cerebral WHITE matter, minimal inflammation.
  • Ergot poisoning — Claviceps purpurea; gangrenous form (dry gangrene, sloughing extremities) vs rare spasmodic/convulsive form.
  • Exam trap — gangrenous ergotism looks identical to fescue poisoning grossly.
Chapter 33

Hepatotoxic, Nephrotoxic & Reproductive Mycotoxicoses

The final group of mycotoxicoses targets specific organ systems with distinctive mechanisms: Facial Eczema and Lupinosis damage the liver via photosensitisation and progressive necrosis; Ochratoxicosis is purely nephrotoxic; Oestrogenic mycotoxicosis disrupts reproduction; and the Trichothecenes are a broad, highly cytotoxic group affecting multiple systems.

Facial Eczema

First described in sheep, New Zealand; also affects cattle. Caused by the mycotoxin sporidesmin, from Pithomyces chartarum, a saprophyte usually associated with rye grass pastures.

  • Mechanism — mainly hepatotoxic; the eponymous lesion is hepatotoxic photosensitisation from circulating phylloerythrin
  • Distribution — sheep: face and ears most severely affected; cattle: udder and teats
  • Microscopic lesions — cholangio-hepatitis with necrosis of biliary epithelium, fibrosis, regenerative hyperplasia; focal hepatic necrosis and regenerative hyperplasia; haemorrhages in gallbladder and urinary bladder walls are common

Lupinosis (Phomopsis Lupinosis)

Caused by the mycotoxin phomopsin, from Phomopsis leptostromiformis, growing on sweet/bitter lupines. Occurs in New Zealand, Australia, Europe, South Africa; mainly sheep, also cattle/horses/pigs.

  • Clinical signs — anorexia, jaundice, death within days of exposure; elevated SGOT, LDH, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin; pronounced jaundice; photosensitisation may accompany
  • Gross lesions — liver enlarged, yellow, friable; early hepatic lesions show focal necrosis (mainly central/midzonal); progresses to scarring and distortion of the lobular pattern; bile ductule hyperplasia

Ochratoxicosis ("Mouldy Nephrosis" of Swine) EXAM HOT

Nephrotoxic mycotoxins (isocoumarin derivatives of phenylalanine) produced by Penicillium viridicatum and Aspergillus ochraceus. Ochratoxin A is the most important toxin naturally causing disease in pigs and poultry; widely reported in India. Horses suspected susceptible; dogs experimentally susceptible.

  • Same fungi also produce citrinin, a related nephrotoxin producing a similar syndrome and believed to contribute to (but not primarily cause) nephropathy in pigs/birds
  • Clinical signs — polydipsia, polyuria, dehydration, significantly retarded growth
  • Lesions restricted to the kidneys — enlarged, grey-yellow, firmer than normal; targets the proximal convoluted tubule especially in pigs
  • Microscopic — epithelial cells lose brush border, become shorter with enlarged vesicular nuclei; later pyknosis/necrosis, sloughing into lumen; secondary peritubular fibrosis progressing to sclerosis of most of the kidney including glomeruli
  • Other species — in dogs/chickens, lesions also develop outside the kidney: fatty change/focal hepatic necrosis, ulcerative enteritis/colitis/proctitis, lymphoid tissue necrosis
  • Suspected cause of abortion in cattle fed mouldy hay; suspected cause of fatal chronic nephropathy in humans

Oestrogenic Mycotoxicosis (Zearalenone/F-2 Toxin)

Fusarium roseum and other Fusarium species produce zearalenone (F-2 toxin), an oestrogenic mycotoxin contaminating maize.

  • Sows — vulvovaginitis, oestrus stimulation, enlarged vulvae/mammary glands/teats, occasional vaginal prolapse; infertility, nymphomania, or pseudopregnancy; atrophic ovaries, grossly enlarged uterus/cervix; early-pregnancy ingestion causes embryonic death
  • Young male pigs — feminisation: testicular atrophy, gynaecomastia (enlarged, sometimes milk-secreting mammary tissue)
  • Microscopic lesions — ovarian follicular atresia, uterine oedema and cellular proliferation of all layers, mammary ductular proliferation; stillbirths, small litters, neonatal mortality also occur
  • Zearalenone toxicity is suspected to cause similar reproductive suppression and reduced fertility in cattle

Trichothecenes

A group of highly toxic mycotoxins from several Fusarium species and other fungi, contaminating grain in the field; toxin production increases with cool storage temperatures, so natural poisoning is more common in cooler climates.

  • No single well-defined clinical/pathological syndrome exists — multiple toxins usually coexist in the same contaminated grain, complicating identification
  • T-2 toxin and DAS (diacetoxyscirpenol) — highly toxic, cause contact necrosis of skin/mucous membranes (mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, rumen, stomach); potent inhibitors of protein synthesis, explaining their cytotoxicity
  • Vomitoxin (DON, deoxynivalenol) — causes anorexia and vomiting, especially in pigs; acts on CNS receptors without producing morphological lesions; rapid recovery once removed from diet
  • Clinical signs (cattle, pigs, horses, poultry) — oral ulceration, anorexia, vomiting, diarrhoea, haemorrhage, leucopenia, jaundice, incoordination
  • Specific lesions — necrosis of all lymphoid organs, bone marrow, and GI epithelium; also liver, kidney, heart, pancreas necrosis
  • "Mouldy corn poisoning" in cattle — believed due to T-2 toxin; characterised by extensive haemorrhage
  • Satratoxin (stachybotryotoxin) — from Stachybotrys sp.; causes Stachybotryotoxicosis in horses, cattle, sheep, pigs, poultry, humans (reported USSR, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Finland, France, South Africa) from mouldy straw/hay; disease and lesions resemble T-2 toxin poisoning
Chapter recap
  • Facial eczema — sporidesmin from Pithomyces chartarum; hepatotoxic photosensitisation, sheep face/ears, cattle udder/teats.
  • Lupinosis — phomopsin from Phomopsis leptostromiformis on lupines; jaundice, hepatic necrosis progressing to scarring.
  • Ochratoxicosis — Penicillium/Aspergillus, purely renal (proximal convoluted tubule), pigs and poultry.
  • Zearalenone (F-2 toxin) — oestrogenic Fusarium toxin, vulvovaginitis in sows, feminisation in young boars.
  • Trichothecenes — T-2/DAS = contact necrosis + protein synthesis inhibition; vomitoxin = CNS-mediated vomiting with no lesions; satratoxin = stachybotryotoxicosis from mouldy straw.

General Pathology of Helminthic & Protozoal Infections

Parasitic disease follows different rules from bacterial/viral disease: adult helminths mostly do NOT multiply within the host, and protozoa are single-celled eukaryotes with very varied life cycles. Understanding these two frameworks explains every disease in this part.

Helminths — key principles EXAM HOT

  • Life cycle usually alternates sexual reproduction in the final host with asexual multiplication in an intermediate host/vector — except Strongyloides, whose larvae can become infective inside the intestine itself
  • Two consequences of the adults' lack of multiplication
    • Disease is usually caused by the inflammatory response against eggs or larvae, not the adults (e.g. Schistosomiasis)
    • Disease severity is proportional to the number of parasites (10 hookworms cause little damage; 1000 hookworms consume 100 ml blood/day and cause severe anaemia)
  • Three phyla — Platyhelminthes (trematodes and cestodes); Nemathelminthes (nematodes); Acanthocephala (thorny-headed worms)

Eight mechanisms of helminth damage

  • 1. Mechanical interference — obstruct vessels (D. immitis in right ventricle/pulmonary artery, Elaeophora schneideri in carotids, Strongylus vulgaris in mesenteric arteries, Spirocerca lupi in aorta); obstruct ducts (liver flukes in bile duct, ascarids in gut); attach to functional tissue (hookworms, strongyles); act as foreign body (schistosome ova)
  • 2. Invade/displace tissue — causing necrosis and hypersensitivity (hookworm/Habronema/Onchocerca larvae in skin; giant liver flukes/ascarid larvae in liver; Coenurus/Echinococcus in brain; Trichinae/cysticerci in muscle)
  • 3. Suck blood — causing anaemia (hookworms, stomach worms)
  • 4. Consume host food — tapeworms, ascarids
  • 5. Induce/predispose to neoplasia — Spirocerca lupi (oesophageal sarcoma), S. haematobium (bladder carcinoma in man), Cysticercus fasciolaris (liver)
  • 6. Introduce secondary infection — lungworms, hookworms, Stephanurus dentatus
  • 7. Eat host tissue directly — ascarids, stomach worms
  • 8. Secrete toxins — haemolysins, histolysins, anticoagulants (hookworms, nodule worms, strongyles)

Protozoa — key principles EXAM HOT

  • Subkingdom Protozoa (Kingdom Protista) — single-celled eukaryotes; most are free-living/non-pathogenic
  • Important pathogenic genera — Leishmania, Trypanosoma, Giardia, Trichomonas, Histomonas, Entamoeba, Eimeria, Isospora, Cryptosporidium, Besnoitia, Neospora, Toxoplasma, Sarcocystis, Plasmodium, Babesia, Theileria, Balantidium

  • Helminth rule — most adults don't multiply in the host, so disease severity tracks parasite BURDEN, and lesions are often driven by eggs/larvae, not adults.
  • Eight damage mechanisms — mechanical obstruction, tissue invasion, blood-sucking, nutrient competition, oncogenesis, secondary infection, direct tissue consumption, toxin secretion.
  • Protozoa — five key groups by locomotion/life cycle: Mastigophora (flagella), Sarcodina (pseudopodia), Apicomplexa (no locomotion, most pathogens here), Microspora (spores), Ciliophora (cilia, dikaryotic).
Chapter 35

Trematode (Fluke) Diseases — Fascioliasis, Amphistomiasis & Nasal Schistosomiasis

Trematodes ("flukes") are flatworms with complex life cycles requiring a snail intermediate host. This chapter covers the three major fluke diseases of Indian livestock: liver flukes, stomach/rumen flukes, and blood flukes of the nasal mucosa.

Fascioliasis EXAM HOT

A very important disease of cattle in India, caused by Fasciola hepatica and Fasciola gigantica (a larger fluke, Fascioloides magna, also occurs in cattle/sheep/horse/deer, rarely pigs).

  • Pathogenesis — hermaphroditic, deposits ova in bile passages, reaching intestine and faeces; intermediate host: Lymnaea snail; infective metacercariae ingested → encyst in duodenum → penetrate wall → peritoneal cavity (peritonitis) → penetrate liver → reach bile duct → mature → cause cholangiohepatitis, bile duct irritation/obstruction, predisposition to secondary bacterial infection; Fascioloides magna larvae wander the peritoneal cavity before invading the liver
  • Clinical signs — acute/subacute forms in sheep are usually fatal (liver damage, death from haemorrhage/anaemia); chronic form: anaemia, unthriftiness, submandibular oedema ("bottle jaw"), reduced milk yield
  • Gross lesions — from adult flukes in bile duct (obstructive jaundice) or wandering flukes through the parenchyma (haemorrhagic foci, tortuous tunnels); liver firm and hard to cut; advanced cases: bile ducts hard and calcified — "pipe stem"/"clay pipe" liver, grating sound on cutting; early cases show hepatocyte degeneration and eosinophilic inflammation, later histiocytes/giant cells invade necrotic (coagulative) areas; cyst formation with calcification; chronic biliary obstruction → "clay pipe cirrhosis"
  • Microscopic lesions — cholangiohepatitis, biliary epithelium shows papillary/glandular hyperplasia; duct walls infiltrated with eosinophils/lymphocytes/macrophages, thickened by fibrous proliferation; small granulomas around eggs lodged in small bile ducts
  • Diagnosis — oval, pedunculated, golden-brown eggs in faeces; postmortem hepatic lesions

Amphistomiasis (Stomach Fluke Disease)

Common parasitic disease of cattle (less so sheep), caused by Paramphistome flukes; big operculated egg with distinct yolk.

  • Pathogenesis — adult flukes are non-pathogenic; metacercariae swallowed, young flukes reach intestine, migrate to rumen/reticulum via duodenum/abomasum, mature in 6 weeks–4 months; immature flukes attach to intestinal mucosa causing irritation and enteritis — "immature amphistomiasis"
  • Clinical signs — persistent foetid diarrhoea, bottle jaw, emaciation
  • Gross lesions — ascites, hydropericardium, duodenal thickening with fibrocatarrhal inflammation
  • Microscopic lesions — immature flukes seen in the deeper mucosa and gut wall
  • Diagnosis — operculated eggs with yolk/germ cells in dung; emaciation, diarrhoea, bottle jaw

Nasal Schistosomiasis

Blood flukes (Schistosoma spp.) live in host blood vessels; multiple species affect different hosts and sites.

  • Species — S. nasale (nasal mucosa veins, cattle/buffalo); S. spindale/S. indicum (mesenteric/portal veins, cattle/buffalo/sheep/goat); S. incognitum (portal veins, pigs/dogs); S. nairi (elephants); S. japonicum (linked to hepatocellular carcinoma in man); S. haematobium (linked to urinary bladder carcinoma in man)
  • Life cycle — ova excreted in faeces → miracidium develops outside → enters snail intermediate host → releases cercariae → penetrate skin of definitive host → metacercariae → reach vein and mature
  • Lesions — S. nasale adults cause nasal granuloma/"snoring disease" in cattle (not seen in buffaloes); phlebitis, venous thrombosis; consume erythrocytes causing anaemia; discharged blood pigment engulfed by macrophages as Bilharzial pigment; dead worms provoke toxic reaction; eggs adhering to endothelium rupture and form pseudotubercles — schistosome eggs centrally surrounded by a pink-staining area known as an "actinobody"
  • Cutaneous lesions — cercarial skin penetration causes urticaria, itching, tiny nodules; cercariae can penetrate skin of hosts where full development fails; cercaria dies in the dermis — the basis of "cercarial dermatitis" ("swimmer's itch," "collector's itch," "swamp itch") seen in agricultural workers/swimmers
  • Diagnosis — nasal washings examined for Napoleon-cap-shaped S. nasale eggs; histopathology of rectal mucosa/liver biopsy
Chapter recap
  • Fascioliasis — F. hepatica/gigantica; "pipe stem"/"clay pipe" liver from calcified bile ducts; Lymnaea snail intermediate host.
  • Amphistomiasis — IMMATURE flukes cause disease (adults harmless); bottle jaw + foetid diarrhoea.
  • Nasal schistosomiasis — S. nasale causes nasal granuloma/snoring disease in cattle; "actinobody" = egg + pink Splendore-Hoeppli-like reaction; basis of cercarial dermatitis.
  • Shared feature — all three require a snail intermediate host.

Cestode (Tapeworm) Diseases & Larval Cyst Diseases

Tapeworm disease has two faces: the relatively mild adult intestinal infection, and the far more damaging larval "bladder worm" stage, which migrates to and encysts in organs — the basis of cysticercosis, coenurosis, and hydatid disease.

Monieziasis & Taeniasis

Tapeworm infection causes more damage in young animals.

  • Etiology — Moniezia expansa (cattle); M. benedini and M. expansa (sheep/goat); Dipylidium caninum, Taenia pisiformis, T. hydatigena, Multiceps multiceps, Diphyllobothrium latum (dogs)
  • Pathogenesis — injury/enteritis from suckers and hooks; absorption of host nutrients; obstruction and volvulus; D. latum causes macrocytic anaemia by absorbing vitamin B12, folic acid and iron
  • Clinical signs — diminished appetite; dogs show severe anal irritation, dragging themselves on their buttocks ("scooting")

Ascarid & Nematode Migratory Diseases — Ascariasis

Ascarids are among the most common roundworms of the GI tract, host-specific, and unusual in that their pathology depends heavily on which migratory pathway the larvae take — a classification worth memorising in full.

Ascariasis EXAM HOT

  • Pathogenesis — infective larvae released in the gut after ingestion of embryonated eggs; most ascarids need no intermediate host; the prepatent period (interval between ingesting infective form and eggs appearing in faeces) varies by species

Three larval migration patterns

  • Type 1 — larvae penetrate intestinal wall, pass through liver, lung bronchiole, trachea, then are swallowed back into the alimentary tract where adults develop — e.g. Ascaris lumbricoides (man, pigs), Parascaris equorum (horses), Toxocara canis (dogs)
  • Type 2 — larvae migrate through liver and lungs AND somatic tissues, sometimes causing prenatal foetal infection — e.g. T. canis, Neoascaris vitulorum (cattle)
  • Type 3 — larvae penetrate the intestinal wall and return directly to the intestinal lumen without migrating elsewhere — e.g. Toxascaris leonina, T. cati (cats, lion, dogs)
  • Poultry — Ascaridia galli (intestine, more pathogenic) and Heterakis gallinae (caeca)

Lesions

  • Adults feed on intestinal contents, sometimes migrate into stomach, hepatic or pancreatic duct — bile duct obstruction causes jaundice
  • Large worm numbers → intestinal obstruction, intussusception, rupture; penetrating worms cause peritonitis
  • Liver "milk spots" — larval burrowing destroys parenchyma, the tract fills with blood/debris, neutrophils/eosinophils crowd around it, healing by scar tissue leaves foci visible on the capsular surface as "milk spots"; heavy infection → confluent lesions, fibrosis obliterating whole lobules
  • Adult worms cause inanition and growth retardation
  • Migrating infective larvae cause central caseous necrosis surrounded by epithelioid cells, eosinophils, lymphocytes, neutrophils in various tissues
  • In swine lung, larvae cause haemorrhage, dyspnoea, cough — described as "thumps"
  • Visceral larva migrans — Toxocara larvae migrating in abnormal hosts (man and other animals)

Diagnosis — thick-shelled eggs with unsegmented yolk.

Chapter recap
  • Three migration types — Type 1: liver→lung→trachea→swallowed (Ascaris, Parascaris, T. canis); Type 2: also somatic tissue/prenatal (T. canis, N. vitulorum); Type 3: direct return to lumen, no migration (Toxascaris).
  • Liver "milk spots" — the classic gross lesion from larval migration tracks healing by scarring.
  • "Thumps" — larval lung migration in swine causing dyspnoea/cough.
  • Visceral larva migrans — Toxocara larvae in an abnormal (paratenic) host, notably humans.

Strongyle & Hookworm Diseases — Strongylosis, Ancylostomiasis, Bunostomiasis & Haemonchosis

This chapter groups the major blood-sucking nematodes of the GI tract: the equine large strongyles, the classic hookworms of carnivores/cattle, and Haemonchus, the "barber's pole worm" of the abomasum.

Strongylosis (Equine)

Adult worms occupy the caecum and large colon of equines; direct life cycle, no intermediate host.

  • Large strongyles (blood suckers) — Strongylus vulgaris, S. edentatus, S. equinus; small strongyles are less injurious individually but occur in far greater numbers
  • S. vulgaris — double-toothed, attached to caecal mucosa; larvae migrate into arterioles (cranial mesenteric artery, aorta), depositing thrombus along their path, may occlude the artery lumen; thrombosis/aneurysm of aorta and iliac arteries → hind-limb weakness and severe abdominal pain; verminous enteritis and verminous aneurysm are the classic lesions
  • S. equinus — triple-toothed, found in caecum, rarely colon; ingested larvae penetrate intestine, enter blood, migrate through liver→heart→lung, coughed up and swallowed to reach large intestine
  • S. edentatus — toothless; adults attach to caecal/colonic mucosa; ingested larvae penetrate the ventral colon wall and encyst in the abdominal wall for a long time, later reaching the small-intestine serosa where they form small raised nodules ("worm nests") with a central caseous necrotic area surrounded by erythrocytes/leucocytes/macrophages/oedema; larva then penetrates back into the large-intestinal lumen
  • Diagnosis — faecal egg examination; light infections are near-universal and harmless, so worm burden should be assessed before treatment

Ancylostomiasis (Hookworm Disease) EXAM HOT

Seen in all animals except horses; blood suckers causing chronic haemorrhagic anaemia.

  • Etiology — A. caninum, A. braziliense (dog/cat); Bunostomum phlebotomum (cattle); B. trigonocephalum (sheep/goat)
  • Pathogenesis — infective larvae penetrate the skin causing severe dermatitis; can also infect aberrant hosts producing "creeping eruption"; skin becomes thickened/scaly — "Cooly's itch," "water itch," "ground itch"; larvae reach lungs via veins, break into alveoli causing haemorrhage and possible secondary bacterial pneumonia/fibrosis; coughed up, swallowed, reach the small intestine; adults have buccal teeth and a powerful oesophagus, drawing inflamed mucosa into the buccal cavity, damaging epithelium and sucking blood; secreted anticoagulant keeps blood flowing even after the worm changes attachment site; bacteria enter through denuded epithelium
  • Blood loss — each parasite may suck 0.8–1 ml blood per 24 hrs; erythrocyte count may fall to 25% of normal; anaemia is hypochromic, microcytic with haematopoietic hyperplasia
  • Clinical signs — diarrhoea with dark tarry faeces; hyperaemia, emaciation, weakness in chronic cases; "bottle jaw" condition
  • Diagnosis — demonstration of hookworm eggs in faeces

Bunostomiasis

Hookworm disease of cattle/sheep/pigs from Bunostomum sp., in the small intestine — poor growth, blood loss, anaemia, anasarca. Calves 4–12 months most commonly affected, more common in winter.

  • Life cycle — direct; egg hatches to larva; larvae enter through skin AND mouth; after skin penetration, enter blood stream → heart → lungs → alveoli (4th stage larvae) → pharynx → swallowed → intestine; larvae penetrate intestinal wall then return to the lumen without further migration; prepatent period of B. phlebotomum is ~8 months
  • Clinical signs — constipation, unthriftiness, anaemia; severe infestations: pale mucous membranes, anasarca under the jaw and along the belly, prostration, death in 2–3 days in cattle
  • Gross lesions — worms in the first few feet of small intestine; deeply blood-stained intestinal contents; ≥100 worms in calves = significant infestation; >2000 worms = likely fatal
  • Diagnosis — hookworm eggs with blunt ends and deeply pigmented embryonic cells

Haemonchosis

Haemonchus contortus (family Trichostrongyloidea) inhabits the abomasum of cattle, sheep, goat.

  • Pathogenesis — direct life cycle; ingested larvae mature in the abomasum, attach to mucosa, suck blood, causing lacerations that predispose to gastritis
  • Clinical signs — anaemia (most outstanding sign) with submandibular/ventral oedema; diarrhoea, progressive weakness, emaciation; "self-cure" phenomenon may occur as the animal develops immunity
  • Gross lesions — pale mucous membranes/viscera; abomasal mucosa shows shallow ulcers and haemorrhages; ascites, hydropericardium, hydrothorax; hyperplastic bone marrow changes
  • Diagnosis — symptoms plus demonstration of ova
Chapter recap
  • S. vulgaris — verminous aneurysm from arterial larval migration is the classic named lesion.
  • S. edentatus — worm nests via abdominal-wall encystment then serosal migration.
  • Ancylostomiasis — skin penetration ("ground itch") → lung migration → intestinal blood-sucking with anticoagulant secretion; hypochromic microcytic anaemia.
  • Bunostomiasis — same hookworm mechanism, cattle-specific, calves 4–12 months, winter peak.
  • Haemonchosis — abomasal blood-sucker, "self-cure" phenomenon via acquired immunity.

Filarial Nematode Diseases — Spirocercosis, Dirofilariasis & Setariasis

Filarial (thread-like) nematodes require an insect intermediate host and localise in specific vascular or serosal sites — the aorta/oesophagus (Spirocerca), the right heart (Dirofilaria), or aberrantly in the CNS (Setaria).

Spirocercosis

Bright red Spirocerca lupi adults found in nodules in the oesophageal wall and aorta of dog, fox, wolf, cat.

  • Life cycle — embryonated eggs in faeces ingested by coprophagous beetles (intermediate host), where larvae develop; if the beetle is eaten by a transport host (frog, snake, lizard, bird), larvae persist there; final host eats the infected beetle/transport host → larvae penetrate the stomach → via blood reach the upper thoracic aortic wall → later move to the adjacent oesophagus and develop within cystic nodules, releasing eggs into the oesophagus through a small pore
  • Clinical signs — oesophageal obstruction causing persistent vomiting; sudden death from aortic wall rupture
  • Gross/microscopic lesions — aortic nodules initiate aneurysm formation, which may rupture causing fatal haemorrhage; roughened intimal aortic surface; oesophageal nodules near the cardia may contain one or several worms; granuloma with central worm surrounded by connective tissue, neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells; eosinophils are usually absent; thoracic vertebral exostoses from aberrant larval migration; fibrosarcoma and osteosarcoma near spirocercal lesions are reported metastases
  • Diagnosis — small gelatin-capsule-shaped eggs with a coiled larva; postmortem demonstration of aortic/oesophageal lesions

Dirofilariasis (Heart Worm Disease) EXAM HOT

Synonym: Canine filariasis. Dirofilaria immitis is an important filarid infecting dogs, cats, foxes, wolves, rarely horses and humans (also D. repens).

  • Pathogenesis — after copulation, microfilariae circulate in blood; ingested by mosquitoes on biting; develop in the mosquito body cavity over ~10 days; infective larvae migrate to mosquito mouthparts; on a fresh bite, larvae enter subcutis/muscle, grow for 3–4 months, then enter veins and reach the right ventricle where they may live for years
  • Gross/microscopic lesions — mild infections cause little damage; large worm numbers obstruct blood flow → right ventricular hypertrophy, chronic venous congestion of liver/spleen/lungs, ascites; dead worms form emboli causing pulmonary infarction; worms in pulmonary arteries cause intimal fibrosis and arterial hypertrophy; circulating microfilariae themselves cause no disturbance, but if they die, small granulomatous foci occur; immune complex glomerulonephritis is a recognised complication
  • Diagnosis — microfilariae in peripheral blood; ELISA and IFA for adult/microfilarial antigens

Setariasis (Cerebrospinal Nematodiasis) EXAM HOT

Filariad worms of the genus Setaria normally live in the peritoneal cavity of most domestic animals without causing serious disease.

  • Setaria digitata — normal peritoneal-cavity parasite of cattle; usually neither the adults nor circulating microfilariae cause disease in cattle; disease occurs when it aberrantly invades the CNS of OTHER species (sheep, goat, horse) causing cerebrospinal nematodiasis — paralysis and blindness
  • Clinical signs — incoordination, weakness, paralysis; sheep/goats — "lumbar paralysis"; horses — "kumri" (loin weakness), prevalent in India; young horses/mules 1–2 years show difficulty walking, sway/wobble from side to side, frequent falling, difficulty rising (from cervical spinal cord trauma)
  • Gross/microscopic lesions — lesions in brain and spinal cord, severity depending on parasite number; narrow tortuous tracks of haemorrhage/softening trace the larval path; immature worms wander randomly, leaving necrotic paths; bilateral symmetrical areas of malacia; gitter cells and eosinophil/neutrophil infiltration surround the tracts
  • Diagnosis — careful microscopic examination of CNS tissue and CSF for larvae
Chapter recap
  • Spirocercosis — aorta→oesophagus migration; coprophagous beetle intermediate host; associated with fibrosarcoma/osteosarcoma; eosinophils notably absent from the granuloma.
  • Dirofilariasis — mosquito-borne, right ventricle localisation, immune complex glomerulonephritis as a complication.
  • Setariasis — normal in cattle peritoneum, but causes cerebrospinal nematodiasis ("kumri" in horses, lumbar paralysis in sheep/goats) when it aberrantly enters the CNS of OTHER species.

Coccidiosis

Acute or chronic haemorrhagic enteritis affecting cattle, rabbits and poultry, characterised by invasion and destruction of intestinal epithelium, diarrhoea and dysentery caused by coccidia of the genus Eimeria/Isospora.

Pathogenesis & life cycle

  • Oocysts (resistant, mature coccidia) are voided in faeces; sporozoites develop inside the oocyst
  • Ingested by a susceptible animal → sporozoites escape → invade intestinal epithelium → asexual multiplication (schizogony) forming merozoites
  • Asexual multiplication stops; merozoites differentiate into male (microgametocyte) and female (macrogametocyte)
  • Union of the two gametocytes produces a new oocyst, which ruptures the epithelium and is passed in faeces

  • Clinical signs — dysentery with tenesmus, leading to anaemia, dehydration, emaciation
  • Lesions — destruction of epithelial cells; surviving epithelium proliferates, causing villous hyperplasia with papillary projections packed with parasites; mucous membrane becomes thickened, oedematous, denuded, haemorrhagic

  • Liver studded with small-to-large round grey nodules, necrosed, containing large numbers of coccidia
  • Early lesions — destruction of biliary epithelium; longer course — proliferation of epithelium becomes the main feature, thrown into papillary folds resembling adenomatous hyperplasia

Coccidiosis in birds

  • Mortality may reach 90–100%
  • E. tenella is the most pathogenic species

  • Hepatic coccidiosis — nodular liver in rabbits, biliary epithelial proliferation resembling adenomatous hyperplasia.
  • Core lesion — villous hyperplasia + haemorrhagic, denuded mucosa from epithelial destruction.

Piroplasmosis — Babesiosis & Theileriosis

Both diseases are tick-borne piroplasms parasitising blood cells — Babesia directly destroys erythrocytes causing haemolytic anaemia, while Theileria first multiplies in lymphocytes (forming the diagnostic Koch's blue bodies) before entering erythrocytes.

Babesiosis (Piroplasmosis, Tick Fever) EXAM HOT

Babesia occurs in erythrocytes, destroying them and giving rise to fever, anaemia, jaundice and haemoglobinuria. Transmitted by ticks (intermediate host).

  • Incidence — Bos indicus breeds relatively resistant due to tick resistance; seasonal incidence follows tick population
  • Cattle (Bovine piroplasmosis / Texas fever / Red water) — caused by B. bigemina (most common in India, pear-shaped forms in pairs in erythrocytes), B. bovis; vector: Boophilus annulatus
  • Pathogenesis — multiplies by cell division, destroying the host cell and invading another, causing haemoglobinaemia/haemoglobinuria
  • Clinical signs — fever, anorexia, depression initially; icterus, haemoglobinuria, ascites later; haemolytic anaemia manifested by thin watery blood with falling Hb, RBC count, PCV
  • Gross lesions — emaciated carcass with oedema, subcutaneous/subserous/intramuscular haemorrhages; enlarged spleen and liver; gall bladder distended with dark green bile
  • Microscopic lesions — haemoglobinuric nephrosis (lower nephron nephrosis); centrilobular and paracentral hepatic necrosis; oedema; excess pleural/pericardial/peritoneal fluid; Babesia demonstrable in brain capillaries
  • Other species — Equine: B. caballi and B. equi (latter more pathogenic); Canine: B. canis (larger, pear-shaped, haemoglobinuria constant) vs B. gibsoni (smaller, ring form, chronic, haemoglobinuria/jaundice NOT seen); Sheep/goat: B. motasi (longer, virulent) vs B. ovis (less severe)
  • Babesia vs Plasmodium — Babesia completely catabolises haemoglobin (no pigment); Plasmodium retains brownish haemozoin pigment — this absence of pigment differentiates the two
  • Immune response — sporozoites invade RBCs via alternate complement pathway activation; infected erythrocytes incorporate Babesia antigen into their membrane, inducing opsonising antibodies → removal by mononuclear phagocyte system; also antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC); premunition = immunity dependent on continued presence of the parasite in a chronic carrier state
  • Diagnosis — blood smear identification, enzyme immunoassay, ELISA, DNA probes

Theileriosis EXAM HOT

Acute disease of cattle — high fever, superficial lymph gland swelling, rough hair coat, salivation, respiratory distress, caused by Theileria species, transmitted by ticks.

  • Etiology — T. parva (East Coast Fever, Africa, not in India); T. lawrencei (Buffalo disease, Africa); T. annulata (seen in India); T. mutans (India, no symptoms)
  • Pathogenesis — rod/comma/ring-shaped forms; schizogony occurs in lymphocytes and monocytes; on tick-borne entry, infects lymphocytes/monocytes of blood, lymph gland, spleen; the schizonts are Koch's blue bodies — pathognomonic, seen in lymph-gland biopsy; after Koch's blue body breakup, organisms enter erythrocytes
  • Gross lesions — lymph node enlargement; white foci in renal cortex; punched-out abomasal ulcers; pulmonary oedema/emphysema; subcutaneous/intramuscular oedema; excess pericardial/pleural fluid; splenomegaly, hepatomegaly; congested meninges with focal brain haemorrhage; death may result from asphyxia (respiratory distress from pulmonary oedema)
  • Microscopic lesions — lymphatic cell proliferation in lymph nodes/spleen/Peyer's patches with Koch's blue bodies; anaemic changes evident in bone marrow
  • Immune mechanisms — survivors develop solid immunity; parasite invades both T and B cells, triggers interleukin-2 pathway; infected lymphocytes enlarge to lymphoblasts and proliferate; since the parasite divides along with the host cell, rapid clonal expansion of parasitised cells occurs, often overwhelming and fatal; cytotoxic CD8+ cells kill infected lymphoblasts
  • Diagnosis — organism demonstration in erythrocytes/lymphocytes (Koch's blue bodies); ELISA for seroepidemiology
Chapter recap
  • Babesiosis — B. bigemina (cattle, most common in India), Haemoglobinuric nephrosis, gall bladder distended with dark bile; NO pigment retention (differs from Plasmodium).
  • Species pairs — B. canis (severe, haemoglobinuria) vs B. gibsoni (mild, no haemoglobinuria); B. motasi (virulent) vs B. ovis (mild).
  • Premunition — immunity that persists only while the parasite persists.
  • Theileriosis — Koch's blue bodies (schizonts in lymphocytes) are pathognomonic; T. annulata is the Indian species of clinical importance.
  • Shared vector — both diseases spread by ticks; both cause anaemia, but through different mechanisms (direct RBC lysis vs lymphocyte proliferation then RBC invasion).

Trypanosomosis

A group of diseases affecting mammals, domesticated and wild animals, man and birds, caused by various species of Trypanosoma — flagellated blood protozoa transmitted mainly by biting flies, capable of remarkable antigenic variation to evade the host immune system.

Etiology — species and named diseases EXAM HOT

SpeciesDisease
T. evansiSurra
T. equiperdumDourine
T. equinumMal de caderas
T. congolense, T. vivaxWasting disease in cattle
T. cruziChaga's disease (American trypanosomosis)
T. gambienseSleeping sickness in man
T. bruceiNagana (tsetse fly disease, African trypanosomosis)

Life cycle

  • Common vectors — biting flies of the group Tabanus, Stomoxys, tsetse (Glossina), etc.
  • Definite cyclic development occurs in true invertebrate hosts; trypanosomes multiply through various forms in the digestive tract, finally migrating as infective forms to the salivary glands and injected into the mammalian host with the vector's saliva during a bite — salivarian transmission
  • In T. cruzi, the infective stage is in the hind gut and excreted in the arthropod's faeces (Triatoma sp.) at the time of the bite — stercorarian transmission

Pathogenesis & immune evasion

  • American trypanosomes (T. cruzi) invade host cells; African trypanosomes do NOT
  • Following entry, African trypanosomes multiply rapidly by binary fission in the bloodstream → parasitaemia
  • Variable Surface Glycoproteins (VSGs) — the parasite's unique ability to undergo almost endless antigenic variation through changes in surface glycoproteins, evading antibody clearance
  • Trypanosomes may also enter the interstitial space and multiply there; chronic disease can cause glomerulonephritis and vasculitis from continuous immune complex formation
  • Evasion strategies — promoting suppressive regulatory cells or B-cell system exhaustion; becoming non-antigenic; rapid, repeated antigenic variation; immunosuppression can lead to death from secondary bacterial pneumonia or sepsis

Surra EXAM HOT

Acute or chronic disease of all domestic animals, chiefly equines, caused by T. evansi; camels also affected. Always fatal in camels and horses.

  • Clinical signs — horses — intermittent fever, serous nasal discharge, petechiae/ecchymoses of visible mucosae, oedema of dependent parts, incoordination, icterus, anaemia
  • Clinical signs — cattle/buffaloes — milder; fever, aimless circling, salivation, teeth grinding, coma; may affect semen quality in bulls; irregular oestrus, abortion, stillbirth in cows
  • Blood picture — progressive anaemia (falling RBC/Hb); hypoglycaemia from parasite glucose consumption
  • Gross lesions — enlarged spleen and lymph nodes with follicular hyperplasia; petechiae/ecchymoses of mucosa and serosa
  • Microscopic lesions — lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate in various organs including brain and spinal cord; trypanosomes detectable in body fluids
  • Diagnosis — blood smear examination (acute phase); repeated sampling/buffy coat smear (chronic phase); suspected blood inoculated into highly susceptible rodents or dogs

Other named trypanosomal diseases

  • Mal de caderas — acute infection similar to Surra, affecting horses
  • Chaga's disease — human trypanosomiasis, can also affect dogs and cats, damaging brain and myocardium
  • Dourine — disease of equines transmitted by coitus, characterised by oedematous genital-tract lesions; lesions are mononuclear or granulomatous inflammation
  • Nagana — African trypanosomiasis causing gradual wasting; metacyclic trypanosomes injected during feeding multiply at the subcutaneous site, provoking a local skin reaction (chancre); within the chancre, metacyclic forms change to trypomastigote form and enter blood directly or via lymphatics
  • Diagnosis (general) — demonstration of T. cruzi (or relevant species) in blood/tissue; wet film and stained blood smear examination
Chapter recap
  • Species-disease table — evansi=Surra, equiperdum=Dourine, cruzi=Chaga's, brucei=Nagana, gambiense=sleeping sickness.
  • Salivarian vs stercorarian — most trypanosomes transmitted via vector saliva; T. cruzi via vector faeces.
  • VSGs — variable surface glycoproteins are the key immune-evasion mechanism of African trypanosomes.
  • Surra — always fatal in camels and horses; hypoglycaemia is a distinctive blood finding.
  • Dourine — the only trypanosomal disease transmitted by coitus.

Toxoplasmosis

A zoonotically important disease of all species, including man, caused by Toxoplasma gondii — a small, crescent-shaped intracellular protozoan parasite with the domestic cat as its definitive host and an extremely wide range of intermediate hosts.

Transmission

  • Infection in cats — ingestion of infected meat or sporulated oocysts
  • Exposure to cat faeces or materials contaminated by cat faeces containing oocysts
  • Exposure to infected tissues/meat containing tachyzoites and bradyzoites
  • Congenital transmission; also via milk and semen

Pathogenesis EXAM HOT

  • Obligate intracellular parasite; attacks most organs, especially the reticuloendothelial system and CNS
  • Sporozoites/bradyzoites penetrate and multiply in intestinal epithelium → cell destruction → released toxoplasma spreads to other organs via blood → localises as tissue cysts
  • When immunity fails, cysts rupture → large numbers of inflammatory cells invade surrounding tissue → characteristic granulomatous lesion develops

Clinical signs

  • Varies with species and age; most infections go unnoticed
  • Dogs — most common in puppies; neurological signs, diarrhoea or pneumonia; often occurs alongside canine distemper virus infection
  • Cats — pneumonia, encephalitis, pancreatitis
  • Sheep/goats — abortion with necrosis and inflammation of placental cotyledons; congenital infection of lambs/kids with encephalitis
  • Not common in cattle and horses

Lesions by organ

  • Toxoplasma found in macrophages, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells; tissue cysts occur in brain, liver, kidney, skeletal muscle
  • Brain — diffuse necrotising and non-suppurative infiltration of parenchyma; lymphocytes accumulate in the Virchow-Robin spaces; tachyzoites scattered singly or in pairs in the parenchyma
  • Liver — large, sharply defined areas of coagulation necrosis; tachyzoites found in Kupffer's cells, in cysts, or scattered in necrotic/normal tissue
  • Lungs — attacks type I/II pneumocytes, bronchial epithelium, macrophages, fibroblasts, endothelial and smooth muscle cells; grossly small grey tumour-like masses; alveolar lining becomes cuboidal/columnar, "foetal lung"-like appearance; alveoli filled with large mononuclear cells, leucocytes, cells lining alveoli with Toxoplasma aggregates
  • Lymph nodes — enlarged, firm, congested; extensive coagulation necrosis; tachyzoites in necrotic areas, in endothelial cells, or free in tissue
  • Intestine — ulcers, necrosis, granuloma formation; organisms demonstrable in muscularis and within granulomas
  • Pancreas — acute necrotising lesions with lymphocyte infiltration, oedema, swelling
  • Eyes (humans) — granulomatous chorioretinitis in adults

Diagnosis

  • Antibody detection in live animals — Complement Fixation Test, Haemagglutination Test, Latex Agglutination Test, Indirect Fluorescent Antibody, ELISA
  • Demonstration of Toxoplasma in tissue sections; isolation of the organism; immunostaining techniques
Chapter recap
  • Toxoplasma gondii — cat is the definitive host, near-universal intermediate host range; small crescent-shaped protozoan.
  • Two forms — active tachyzoites (spread, cause acute necrosis) vs dormant bradyzoites in tissue cysts (reactivate when immunity fails).
  • Sheep/goat abortion — necrosis/inflammation of placental cotyledons is the classic reproductive lesion.
  • Organ-specific lesions — brain (Virchow-Robin cuffing), liver (coagulation necrosis in Kupffer cells), lung ("foetal lung" appearance).
  • Zoonotic importance — major public-health concern, especially in pregnant women.

General Pathology of Nutritional Deficiency & Fat-Soluble Vitamins (A, D, E, K)

Nutritional pathology begins with the general causes of deficiency (primary vs secondary) and the macronutrients, then covers the four fat-soluble vitamins — A, D, E, K — whose deficiencies produce classic, named syndromes across species.

General causes of nutrient deficiency EXAM HOT

  • Primary cause — reduced intake (poor-quality diet, inadequate quantity/starvation)
  • Secondary cause — intake normal but not available to the body: diarrhoea, vomition, anorexia, malabsorption, chronic enteritis, neoplasms; increased demand (growth, pregnancy, lactation, fever, hyperthyroidism); reduced storage in liver disease

Lipids, carbohydrates & protein

  • Fat deficiency — essentially does not exist in domestic animals as a dietary deficiency; occurs when fat absorption is impaired (biliary/pancreatic disease); earliest change is skin dryness → erythema, alopecia, scaling, ulceration, poor wound healing; epidermis thickens with hyperkeratosis; in cats, fatty change of liver/kidney with adrenal mineralisation develops
  • Carbohydrates — no specific deficiency state; dogs/cats need no dietary source; gluten enteropathy (Irish Setters) shows villus atrophy and intraepithelial lymphocytic infiltration; reduced available carbohydrate precipitates ketosis (cattle) and pregnancy toxaemia (sheep/cattle)
  • Protein deficiency — most pronounced in young growing animals; failure to grow, cessation of epiphyseal cartilage proliferation, reduced osteoblastic activity, failure of collagen formation, atrophy of endocrine glands/thymus/lymphoid tissue, fatty liver, anaemia, hypoproteinaemia, oedema; complicated by superimposed infection

Vitamin A (Retinol) — "Anti-infection vitamin" EXAM HOT

  • Sources — green plants, fish liver oils; carotene/xanthene converted to vitamin A in the intestine; stored in liver Kupffer cells (as retinol), adipose tissue, blood (as lipoprotein)
  • Functions — bone growth, maintenance of epithelial surfaces, night vision
  • Deficiency lesions — epithelial metaplasia — ciliated columnar epithelium (respiratory tract) transforms to keratinised stratified squamous epithelium throughout the GI, respiratory, ocular, skin, urogenital tract; urinary transitional epithelium metaplasia may cause urinary calculi; skin shows acanthosis, dry bran-like scales (pityriasis), hoof cracks in horses; bile duct/gall bladder show cystic dilation and papillomatosis; renal tubular hyperplasia leads to fibrosis
  • Reproductive tract — sterility in bulls, abortion, retained placenta, separation of maternal/foetal membranes
  • Xerophthalmia — loss of mucus-secreting cells, keratinisation of corneal/scleral membrane; night blindness (nyctalopia) results from failure of rhodopsin (aldehyde) regeneration
  • Bone growth effects — retarded growth, interference with epiphyseal cartilage growth, no capillary invasion, no osteoblastic activity; calves show no skull growth while the brain continues to grow, causing herniation, skeletal muscle paralysis, blindness (optic nerve pressure), encephalopathy; birds show defective bone growth and hydrocephalus
  • Poultry (Nutritional roup) — adults: emaciation, decreased egg production/hatchability, increased embryonic malformation, caseous material in eyes; chicks: decreased growth, caseous ocular/nasal material; lesions: plaque-like cheesy membrane and small white nodules in pharynx/oesophagus, gout, glandular duct occlusion from squamous metaplasia
  • Congenital anomalies — ocular dermoids, anophthalmos/microphthalmos, anasarca, cleft palate, malformed limbs in calves

Vitamin D (Anti-rachitic factor)

  • Sources — fish liver oil, sun exposure, sun-cured hay; D2/calciferol from UV on plant ergosterol; D3/cholecalciferol from UV on 7-dehydrocholesterol in skin
  • Functions — intestinal calcium absorption, raised serum Ca/P, bone mineralisation
  • Causes of deficiency — lack of skin irradiation, cold-climate winters, indoor housing, smoke-screened areas, furry coats, liver/intestinal disease; excess lush green feed raises vitamin A which antagonises vitamin D
  • Rickets (young animals) — stunted growth, "bowed legs," pot-bellied appearance, kyphosis, scoliosis, enlarged bone/joint ends, "rickety rosary" at costochondral junctions
  • Osteomalacia (adult rickets) — occurs after endochondral ossification has ceased; increased bone resorption/atrophy, compact bone becomes spongy, fractures/deformities common, no calcification
  • Poultry — decreased egg production/hatchability, thin-shelled eggs, pliable beak, bent keel bone
  • Hypervitaminosis D — hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria, metastatic calcification, renal damage, osteoporosis from osteoclastic activity

Vitamin K (Anti-haemorrhagic vitamin)

  • Sources — K1: green plants/fruits/liver oils; K2: synthesised by intestinal microflora; K3: Menadione (synthetic)
  • Functions — hepatic synthesis of prothrombin and factor X; role in calcium metabolism
  • Causes of deficiency — obstructive jaundice (no bile), malabsorption, intestinal disorders, liver cirrhosis, reduced bacterial synthesis (antibiotics/coccidiostats), newborn pigs, sweet clover poisoning (decreased prothrombin activity)
  • Haemorrhagic syndrome (poultry) — subcutaneous and abdominal-cavity haemorrhage, anaemia; adult birds synthesise vitamin K, but a low-vitamin-K diet produces eggs/chicks deficient in it, causing haemorrhage/death during wing banding

Vitamin E (Anti-sterility factor) EXAM HOT

  • Sources — germ of grains, green plants, oil seeds; forms: α, β, γ tocopherols
  • Functions — natural antioxidant; "vitamin A sparer" (prevents its GI destruction); protects membranes/essential fatty acids from oxidation; selenium is the "vitamin E sparer" (enhances transport/retention)
  • Causes of deficiency — concentrate feeding without greens; milk deficient in vitamin E; diets rich in unsaturated fatty acids (e.g. cod liver oil, destroys α-tocopherol); fish/meat/bone meal containing sodium sulphite
  • Named syndromes — calves: "White muscle disease" (muscular dystrophy); lambs: "Stiff lamb disease"; horses: Azoturia; rats: males show spermatozoa destruction/seminiferous tubule degeneration, females show early foetal death; minks/pigs: "Yellow fat disease" (Steatitis) from oxidised fatty acid deposition as ceroid
  • Pigs — rapidly growing animals; dyspnoea, diarrhoea, vomiting, melena, paralysis, icterus; lesions: generalised oedema, hepatic necrosis, gastric ulceration, haemorrhagic diathesis, pulmonary oedema/ascites
  • Poultry — Encephalomalacia ("Crazy chick disease") — ~5 weeks age; muscular weakness, ataxia, spasms, torticollis, paralysis, death; cerebral oedema, cerebellar haemorrhage, neuronal degeneration (cerebellum mainly damaged from polyunsaturated fatty acid excess)
  • Exudative diathesis — capillary endothelial damage, increased vascular permeability/plasma leakage, subcutaneous/breast/wing/intramuscular/pericardial haemorrhage, microcytic anaemia
  • Nutritional muscular dystrophy — sulphur amino-acid deficiency in chicks/poults/ducklings, worsened by cod liver oil feeding; arteriolar microthrombosis → breast/thigh muscle degeneration/necrosis
Chapter recap
  • Primary vs secondary deficiency — reduced intake vs normal intake that isn't available/utilised.
  • Vitamin A — epithelial keratinising metaplasia is the unifying lesion across every organ system; xerophthalmia and nyctalopia are classic.
  • Vitamin D — rickets (young, "rickety rosary") vs osteomalacia (adult, spongy bone).
  • Vitamin K — prothrombin/factor X synthesis; haemorrhagic syndrome in poultry.
  • Vitamin E — "White muscle disease" (calves), "Stiff lamb disease" (lambs), "Yellow fat disease" (minks/pigs), "Crazy chick disease" (poultry encephalomalacia); selenium is the vitamin E sparer.
Chapter 45

Water-Soluble Vitamins — Vitamin C & the B-Complex

Unlike the fat-soluble vitamins, the water-soluble vitamins — C and the B-complex — act mainly as coenzymes in metabolism (Krebs cycle, transamination, decarboxylation), so their deficiencies tend to affect rapidly dividing tissues, nerves, and blood formation rather than epithelium alone.

Vitamin C (Ascorbic acid, Antiscorbutic factor)

  • Sources — green plants, citrus fruits; synthesised in the intestine of ALL animals EXCEPT guinea pig and primates; stored in all tissues, most in adrenals; destroyed by heating/cooking
  • Functions — strong reducing agent; tyrosine/phenylalanine/DOPA metabolism; formation of hydroxyproline/hydroxylysine (collagen constituents); synthesis of specific mRNA for incorporating hydroxyproline into collagen; adrenal steroid secretion; formation of osteoid, dentine, ground-substance mucopolysaccharides
  • Scurvy (man) — mesenchymal derivatives not formed properly; delayed wound healing, low tensile strength (scars break open), defective intercellular cement between endothelial cells causing haemorrhage
  • Bone changes — impaired endochondral ossification, decreased osteoblastic activity/osteoid formation, cartilage calcification normal but resorption fails → soft deformed bones, epiphyseal/periosteal haemorrhage
  • Haemorrhagic diathesis — deficient intercellular cement in capillary endothelium → bleeding in subcutis, muscle, joints, legs, bones, gums
  • "Battery sickness" — birds reared in batteries for 3–4 months, Germany; paralysis and atrophy of striated muscle

Vitamin B Complex — general features

  • Coenzymes in the Krebs cycle — called "energy-releasing vitamins"
  • Synthesised in the rumen except in young animals; water-soluble; abundant in yeast

B1 — Thiamine (Anti-neurotic/anti-beriberi factor) EXAM HOT

  • Sources — whole grains, meat; destroyed by milling and polishing
  • Functions — component of thiamine pyrophosphate; essential for acetylcholine synthesis; nervous system heavily affected; lactic acid utilisation in carbohydrate metabolism
  • Causes of deficiency — thiaminases in feed; heat/increased requirement in birds; destruction by thiamine analogues (amprolium); thiaminase in certain fish; increased coccidiostat use/coccidiosis in birds; ruminants synthesise thiamine in the forestomach, but young animals need dietary supplementation
  • Poultry — anorexia, polyneuritis, paralysis starting at the toes; bird sits on flexed legs, retracted head — "star gazing attitude"; peripheral nerve degeneration, cardiac atrophy
  • Pigs — cardiac dilatation, myocardial necrosis, sudden death
  • Humans — "Beri Beri" from polished-grain diet; peripheral neuritis, oedema, myocardial weakness
  • "Chastek paralysis" — cats/foxes fed raw fish containing thiaminase; neuronal degeneration/necrosis causing paralysis
  • Horses — bracken fern/horsetail (thiaminase-containing feed) causes incoordination and bradycardia

B2 — Riboflavin

  • Sources — green grass, meat, milk; also synthesised in rumen
  • Functions — component of flavoproteins; oxygen transfer from plasma to tissue fluid
  • Poultry — clubbed down feathers in chicks, poor feathering, leg paralysis, dwarfing; curled toe paralysis — inward-curling toes, outstretched legs, swollen/discoloured sciatic/brachial nerves with Schwann cell degeneration/proliferation; adults: decreased egg production/hatchability, "dead in shell" eggs
  • Pigs — decreased growth, diarrhoea, rough coat, crippling, conjunctivitis/eyelid oedema/cataract, stillbirths, normocytic normochromic anaemia; humans: cheilitis (mouth corner cracks)

B3 — Niacin (Anti-pellagra factor)

  • Sources — alfalfa, animal by-products; synthesised in caecum/large intestine from tryptophan (also needs B1, B2, B6); increased corn/millet diet reduces tryptophan → niacin deficiency; cats cannot convert tryptophan to niacin
  • Pellagra (humans) — 3 D's: Dermatitis, Diarrhoea, Dementia
  • Pig pellagra — from excess corn feeding; dermatitis, diarrhoea, anaemia; necrotic enteritis, hyperkeratosis, alopecia
  • Canine pellagra ("Black tongue"/Stuttgart's disease) — tongue cyanosis, oral ulcers, diarrhoea/vomiting, dehydration, fever; haemorrhagic enteritis, nerve myelin degeneration
  • Poultry — decreased growth/feathering, GI inflammation, decreased egg production/hatchability; young birds: Perosis (chondrodystrophy) — short, thick, misshapen long bones (also linked to Mn, Zn, choline, biotin deficiency)

B6 — Pyridoxine

  • Functions — tryptophan→niacin metabolism; coenzyme for transaminases/decarboxylases/phosphorylases; melanin formation; antibody production; porphyrin biosynthesis
  • Deficiency — microcytic hypochromic anaemia (pigs); dermatitis (acanthosis, hyperkeratosis); ataxia/convulsions in young animals (calves, piglets, puppies); renal tubular regeneration forming calculi

Pantothenic Acid ("Chick anti-dermatitis factor")

  • Essential part of Coenzyme A — carbohydrate metabolism, TCA cycle, antibody formation, neural function (acetylcholine), cholesterol/hormone precursor
  • Pigs (increased corn feeding) — loss of flesh, scaly skin, diarrhoea, stiff hind limbs; animal rests on hocks with hind legs extended, foreleg "leap" — "goose-stepping gait"; haemorrhagic/necrotic colitis, brain/spinal neuronal necrosis, brachial/sciatic nerve degeneration
  • Ducks — anaemia, since heme is not synthesised without pantothenic acid
  • Poultry — decreased growth, poor feathers, dermatitis, beak/eyelid corner inflammation; breeders: decreased egg production/hatchability, oedematous/haemorrhagic or stunted embryos; copper antagonises pantothenic activity

Biotin

  • "Sulphur-containing vitamin"; carboxyl enzymes for fat/carbohydrate/glucose metabolism; protein synthesis (keratin); growth, epidermal maintenance, bone development, reproduction; avidin is its antivitamin; synthesised by ruminal microflora
  • Poultry — impaired feathering, dermatitis, beak/eyelid/footpad fissures; severe: decreased growth, skin fissures, deformed beak, chondrodystrophy
  • Fatty Liver and Kidney Syndrome (FLKS) — biotin-responsive metabolic disorder in young birds; reduced dietary fat decreases biotin available for gluconeogenesis → hypoglycaemia and nervous tissue damage; fat accumulates in the liver

Folic Acid (Pteroylglutamic acid)

  • All animals except pigs synthesise it in the intestine; active form is tetrahydrofolic acid (FH4), essential for amino acid metabolism, purine/pyrimidine synthesis, nucleic acid metabolism, cell proliferation; haemopoietic system very sensitive; in poultry, needed with lysine for feather pigmentation
  • Deficiency — macrocytic anaemia (sprue/pernicious anaemia), agranulocytosis, leucopenia, megaloblastic bone marrow changes
  • Poultry (often from sulphonamide therapy) — macrocytic megaloblastic anaemia, retarded growth, poor feather pigmentation, chondrodystrophic leg deformities, turkey cervical deformity; layers: reduced egg production/hatchability, embryo deformities

Vitamin B12 (Cyanocobalamin, Animal Protein Factor)

  • Cobalt-containing vitamin from meat/meat products/milk, bacterial synthesis; essential for thymidine biosynthesis, nucleic acid metabolism/RNA-DNA production, carbohydrate/fat/protein metabolism, associated with folic acid metabolism
  • Deficiency cause — absence of intrinsic factor (haemopoietin from gastric parietal cells), or cobalt deficiency
  • Signs — poultry: decreased egg production/hatchability; humans: macrocytic (pernicious) anaemia

Choline

  • Rich sources: yeast, fish meal; synthesised from methionine; function: fat metabolism, hepatic fatty acid oxidation — neutral fat entering the liver is phosphorylated and converted to phospholipids, a step requiring choline; low choline → low phospholipids → neutral fat accumulates → fatty change
  • Deficiency — fatty liver in pregnancy toxaemia of ewes; perosis/slipped tendon in poultry
Chapter recap
  • Vitamin C — synthesised by all animals except guinea pigs and primates; collagen/hydroxyproline formation; scurvy = defective collagen and capillary cement substance.
  • Thiamine (B1) — "star gazing attitude" in poultry, "Chastek paralysis" in cats/foxes fed raw fish, Beri Beri in humans.
  • Riboflavin (B2) — curled toe paralysis, clubbed down feathers.
  • Niacin (B3) — Pellagra's 3 D's; cats cannot convert tryptophan to niacin; canine "black tongue."
  • Pantothenic acid — "goose-stepping gait" in pigs.
  • Biotin — Fatty Liver and Kidney Syndrome (FLKS) in young birds.
  • Folic acid / B12 — both cause macrocytic (megaloblastic) anaemia; B12 needs intrinsic factor/cobalt.
  • Choline — lipotropic factor; deficiency causes fatty liver (ewe pregnancy toxaemia) and poultry perosis.

Macrominerals — Potassium, Sodium/Chloride, Calcium, Phosphorus, Magnesium & Sulphur

The macrominerals are needed in gram quantities and govern osmotic balance, nerve/muscle excitability, and skeletal mineralisation — deficiencies and excesses of calcium and phosphorus in particular produce some of the best-known bone diseases in veterinary pathology.

General causes of mineral deficiency

  • Primary — poor-quality diet (especially roughage), inadequate quantity (starvation)
  • Secondary — poor nutrient absorption; decreased digestive secretions (hepatic/pancreatic disease); diarrhoea; interference with storage/utilisation (e.g. thyroiditis reducing iodine uptake); increased excretion (potassium loss in diarrhoea, calcium loss in hyperparathyroidism); increased requirement (pregnancy, lactation, hyperthyroidism)

Potassium

  • Functions — intracellular; cardiac muscle relaxation; carbohydrate/protein metabolism; controls gastric HCl secretion; chief cellular component; nerve/muscle action
  • Deficiency is not seen naturally in animals; experimental hypopotassaemia in lab animals shows heart striation loss/necrosis/RE infiltration/scarring, and muscle flabbiness/weakness/paralysis; increased desoxycortisone administration leads to hypopotassaemia

Sodium & Chloride

  • Sodium: extracellular fluid/plasma; Chloride: both intra- and extracellular fluid
  • Functions — osmotic pressure of blood/interstitial fluid/cells; salt reduces blood viscosity; chloride forms gastric HCl; acid-base balance
  • Deficiency causes — vomiting/diarrhoea, sweating, milk salt loss in sows, Addison's disease (excess urinary excretion)
  • Heat exhaustion — hot-weather working horses; increased sweating → salt loss → osmotic imbalance, derangement of O2/metabolite exchange, nervous symptoms
  • Dehydration mechanism — salt deficiency → reduced ECF osmotic pressure → renal water reabsorption → reduced blood volume/haemoconcentration → raised plasma protein/colloidal osmotic pressure → interstitial water drawn into capillaries → dehydration
  • Poultry — decreased egg production, retarded growth, neuromuscular dysfunction, death

Salt Poisoning EXAM HOT

Fowls and pigs are highly susceptible.

  • Poultry — access to brine vats, salt settling at hopper bottoms, reduced water intake; signs: diarrhoea, thirst, anorexia, progressive weakness, inability to stand, convulsions, death; lesions: ascites, visceral gout, nephritis
  • Swine — causes: drinking brine, excess salted fish meal, gastroenteritis; signs: anorexia, rapid pulse/respiration, convulsions, paralysis; chronic: somnolence, drooping ears, incoordination, blindness/deafness; gross lesions: hyperaemia, cerebral/meningeal oedema, GI/hepatic/pulmonary hyperaemia, myocardial degeneration; microscopic: eosinophilic meningoencephalitis with perivascular cuffing of eosinophils/lymphocytes, neuronal degeneration

Calcium EXAM HOT

  • Functions — bone ossification/cartilage calcification; activates hydrolytic enzymes; nerve cell excitation; regulated by vitamin D, PTH, calcitonin; milk production/egg laying/reproduction; normal blood Ca = 10–11 mg/100ml (8 mg% = tetany, 6 mg% = tetany/paralysis/coma/death)
  • Deficiency causes — reduced dietary intake, malabsorption, vitamin D deficiency, increased renal excretion, parathyroid deficiency, insoluble complex formation
  • Osteomalacia — bone softening, especially pregnancy; fractures, distortion, leg bowing, abnormal back curvature
  • Milk fever (Post-Parturient Paresis, Eclampsia) — rapid blood-calcium depletion at lactation onset; tetany, incoordination, muscle spasms, unconsciousness, death; animal lies with head turned to flank; heavy milkers with low dietary calcium
  • Poultry — rickets, osteomalacia → osteoporosis in layers, thin-shelled eggs; Cage layer fatigue — sudden paralysis with legs extended in caged hens
  • Excess calcium / Metastatic calcification — normal Ca:P ratio 2:1; hypercalcaemia from excess Ca/vitamin D, parathyroid/bone tumours, or renal disease (P retention → Ca withdrawal); alveolar calcification in lungs, mucosal calcification in stomach
  • Dystrophic calcification — normal blood calcium; calcium deposits in already-dead tissue (caseous TB, atheromatous vessel walls, abscess, tumour, parasites, thrombi, actinomycosis, scars, necrotic areas)
  • Poultry excess — nephropathy, gout

Phosphorus

  • Functions — mechanical bone/tooth strength; body fluid electrolyte; nucleic acid/phospholipid/protein/enzyme component; acid-base balance; metabolic energy transfer, protein synthesis, carbohydrate metabolism
  • Aphosphorosis — endemic in soil/plant-deficient regions; cattle show poor growth, lameness, bone disease, reproductive disorders, "pica" (depraved appetite), reduced milk yield, rickets/osteomalacia
  • Lamsiekte — South Africa, P-deficient soil; lameness, depraved appetite, chewing dead-animal bones, leading to fatal Clostridium botulinum infection
  • Excess phosphorus (primary) — Ca withdrawal from bones causing osteoporosis, from concentrate/cottonseed meal feeding; horses on bran diet: "Miller's disease"/"Bran's disease"
  • Excess phosphorus (secondary) — kidney disease → increased P retention → hypocalcaemia (Ca withdrawal from bone) → "Renal rickets"

Magnesium

  • Intracellular; cofactor for many enzymes; bone growth/development; muscle contraction; Mg:Ca ratio of 1:3.5 needed for bone growth
  • Grass tetany / grass staggers / wheat poisoning — pregnant/parturient animals on lush grass or young wheat shoots; causes: poor Mg absorption, increased ruminal ammonia formation, increased potassium ingestion, thyroid hyperactivity; calves raised only on milk also develop hypomagnesaemia; incoordination progressing to recumbence
  • Pigs — "Stepping syndrome" — continuous hind-leg lifting

Sulphur

Birds fed excess anticoccidal drugs may develop "Sulphur rickets" — sulphur replaces phosphorus in bone.

Chapter recap
  • Salt poisoning — eosinophilic meningoencephalitis is the classic microscopic lesion in pigs; ascites/visceral gout/nephritis in poultry.
  • Milk fever — sudden post-parturient hypocalcaemia in heavy milkers; head turned to flank is the classic posture.
  • Metastatic vs dystrophic calcification — metastatic = elevated blood Ca deposits in normal tissue; dystrophic = normal blood Ca deposits in already-dead tissue.
  • Phosphorus — deficiency causes "pica"/Lamsiekte (bone-chewing → botulism); excess causes "Miller's/Bran's disease" or "Renal rickets."
  • Magnesium — grass tetany in lush-pasture-grazing pregnant/lactating animals.
Chapter 47

Trace Elements — Manganese, Iron, Copper, Cobalt, Zinc, Iodine, Fluorine & Selenium

The trace elements are needed only in milligram quantities but their deficiencies (and, for several, their excesses) produce dramatic, often geographically-named syndromes — this is one of the richest sources of "named disease" exam questions in veterinary nutrition.

Manganese

  • Sources — natural feeds, rice bran is a very good source; concentrated in liver; cofactor for Krebs cycle enzymes, works closely with Mg, affects bone/eggshell formation
  • Birds are most susceptible to Mn deficiency — chondrodystrophy in young growing birds; layers: thin, porous, soft eggshells, decreased production/hatchability, dead embryos
  • Perosis (slipped tendon) — adult birds; Mn is essential for cartilage matrix synthesis; long bones shorten, epiphyseal cartilage fails to ossify, gastrocnemius tendon slips

Iron

  • Sources — soil, myoglobin, cytochrome/catalase/peroxidase enzymes; ferrous form absorbed most readily, acid pH favours absorption; phosphorus/phytic acid form insoluble complexes with iron; copper enhances iron absorption; stored in liver, spleen, bone marrow, skeletal muscle
  • Piglet anaemia — microcytic hypochromic anaemia; baby pigs raised on cement floors, sow's milk deficient in iron, malabsorption from diarrhoea, blood-sucking parasite infestation; iron deficiency causes secondary folic acid deficiency and decreased neutrophil phagocytic activity; develops by 21 days of age
  • Excess — haemochromatosis (hepatic iron accumulation)

Copper EXAM HOT

  • Functions — controls intestinal iron absorption; haemoglobin synthesis; cytochrome oxidase component; phospholipid synthesis; activates oxidase/tyrosinase/succinic oxidase
  • Piglets — microcytic hypochromic anaemia, from soil deprivation (concrete/wooden floors) and low-copper sow's milk
  • Sheep — "Steely wool" — wool becomes hair-like, loses colour from decreased melanin
  • Sway back / Enzootic ataxia — England, Australia; sheep/goats grazing molybdenum-rich grass (Mo antagonises Cu); incoordination, hind-limb weakness, spastic paralysis, blindness; demyelination and cerebral softening/necrosis
  • Cattle — "Falling disease" — Australia, USA; sudden collapse and death, anaemia, weight loss, diarrhoea; myocardial fibrosis, elastic tissue degeneration
  • Skeletal effects — depressed osteoblastic activity, no cartilage osteoid deposition, osteoporosis, long-bone swelling above the pastern, rib/femur/humerus fractures, infertility, retained placenta, calf rickets
  • Excess copper — sheep drenched with CuSO4 for stomach worms → gastritis/enteritis/death; poultry: depression/convulsions/coma; humans: Wilson's disease

Cobalt

  • Constituent of vitamin B12; decreased cobalt → decreased ruminal microflora → decreased vitamin B12; deficiency causes anaemia
  • Cattle/sheep/goats — loss of appetite, anaemia, pale mucosa, emaciation, fatty liver, rough coat, haemosiderosis, death
  • Regional named syndromes — New Zealand: Hill sickness; Australia: Enzootic marasmus; England: Pine; USA: Grand Traverse disease

Zinc

  • Growth of skeleton; epithelial tissue maintenance/formation; egg production; enzyme component; phytic acid and calcium interfere with absorption
  • Pigs — Parakeratosis — from excess dietary Ca/P interfering with Zn absorption; imperfect skin keratinisation, circumscribed erythematous abdominal/thigh areas
  • Cattle — parakeratosis and alopecia
  • Sheep — wool loss, thick wrinkled skin, papules→pustules→crusts
  • Poultry — poor growth/appetite/feathering, scaly leg/foot skin; young birds: chondrodystrophy, decreased egg production/hatchability, embryo skeletal deformities

Iodine

  • Component of thyroxine hormone
  • Excess ("Iodism") — conjunctivitis, lacrimation, hyperkeratosis, rhinitis
  • Deficiency — "Goitre" — thyroid enlargement; iodine-deficient soil regions (Great Lakes, Alps, Himalayas); goitrogenic substances (corn silage, soybean meal) contribute

Fluorine

  • Function — tooth and bone development; deficiency does NOT occur naturally in animals
  • Fluorosis — excess fluorine (>2 ppm water) from rock phosphate feeding, industrial pollution
  • Chronic — teeth — lateral incisors of cattle show opaque, chalky, mottled, streaked/pitted enamel; enamel hypoplasia; caries
  • Chronic — bones — metacarpal/metatarsal/sternum/mandible/phalanges become shorter, thicker, heavier, broader; decreased marrow space; exostoses, osteomalacia, osteoporosis; porous, brittle, fracture-prone; shifting lameness; anaemia from suppressed haemopoiesis
  • Acute poisoning — excess sodium fluoride in swine/poultry parasite control, or sodium monofluoroacetate (rat poison) ingestion by dogs; excitement, vomiting, convulsions, death

Selenium EXAM HOT

  • Component of glutathione peroxidase (an antioxidant, similar role to vitamin E)
  • Deficiency — alkaline soils, excess sulphate fertiliser reduce plant Se content; <0.05 ppm in feed causes deficiency
  • Deficiency diseases — muscular dystrophy (white muscle disease); pancreatic atrophy (reduced lipase → fat/fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption → secondary vitamin E deficiency); neonatal mortality, unthriftiness, chronic diarrhoea, infertility/abortion/retained placenta, dietetic hepatosis in swine
  • Chronic Se poisoning — "Alkali disease" — skin/hoof affected: alopecia, rough mane/tail coat, hoof cracks ("bob tail disease"), hoof detachment, lameness (keratin structure altered when sulphur is replaced by Se); lesions: chronic venous congestion of lung/liver, myocardial necrosis, organ oedema, hepatitis/nephritis
  • Poultry — muscular dystrophy, exudative diathesis, encephalomalacia, decreased hatchability; responds to Se + vitamin E supplementation
  • Acute Se poisoning ("Blind staggers") — from seleniferous weeds (Astragalus, Oxytropis); GI/respiratory signs, continuous walking, butting hard objects, blindness, paralysis, weakness, respiratory failure, cyanosis, death; Se antagonises sulphur, interfering with sulphur-containing enzymes/amino acids
Chapter recap
  • Copper deficiency — Sway back (Mo antagonism), "Falling disease," "Steely wool" — all classic named syndromes.
  • Cobalt deficiency — regional names: Hill sickness (NZ), Enzootic marasmus (Aus), Pine (England), Grand Traverse disease (USA).
  • Zinc deficiency — parakeratosis in pigs/cattle from excess dietary Ca/P.
  • Fluorosis — mottled/pitted teeth + shifting lameness from exostoses/osteoporosis.
  • Selenium — deficiency = white muscle disease; excess = "Alkali disease"/"Bob tail disease"; "Blind staggers" is the acute poisoning form.

Metabolic & Production Diseases

These are the classic "production diseases" of high-yielding livestock — metabolic disorders that emerge from the physiological stress of pregnancy, parturition, and lactation rather than simple dietary deficiency, several of which were introduced under specific minerals in earlier chapters and are consolidated here.

Hypomagnesaemic Tetany EXAM HOT

Two clinical syndromes are associated with hypomagnesaemia.

  • Hypomagnesaemia in calves — occurs in animals raised on a low-magnesium diet, usually milk-only; signs: excitement, tetany, convulsions, death
  • Grass tetany in adult cattle — animals fed exclusively on lush, succulent grass or young growing wheat; tetanic convulsive seizures; cows in advanced pregnancy show symptoms first; high dietary potassium reduces magnesium absorption

Piglet Anaemia

  • Hypochromic, microcytic (iron-deficiency) anaemia from dietary iron deficiency or diarrhoea
  • Baby pigs raised without soil access; sow's milk contains no iron or copper; pigs normally obtain iron from soil, unavailable on concrete/wooden floors
  • Anaemia develops by 21 days of age

Pregnancy Toxaemia (Pregnant Ewe Paralysis)

  • Occurs in the last weeks of pregnancy, typically in ewes carrying twins/triplets
  • Caused by toxic waste products of mother and foetuses plus dietary factors causing ketosis/starvation
  • Symptoms — depression, sleepiness, coma
  • Lesions — fatty change in liver, kidney, heart; terminal subepicardial petechiae/ecchymoses

Milk Fever (Post-Parturient Paresis, Parturient Hypocalcaemia, Eclampsia)

  • Disorder of dairy cows and sheep occurring shortly after parturition, resulting in hypocalcaemia
  • Clinical signs — tetany, incoordination, muscular spasms, unconsciousness, death; animal characteristically lies with head turned to the flank
  • Common in heavy-milking cows not fed sufficient calcium/phosphorus during pregnancy; blood calcium is not maintained because of low parathyroid activity in these animals

Sway Back (Enzootic Ataxia)

  • Sheep in England/Australia; caused by copper deficiency
  • Incoordination and weakness of hind legs; spastic paralysis and blindness; lesions more pronounced in newborn lambs
  • Demyelination and extensive softening/necrosis in cerebral hemispheres

Azoturia EXAM HOT

Synonym: Paralytic myoglobinuria, "Monday morning sickness."

  • Occurs in working horses following a few days of rest while on full rations
  • Clinical signs — trembling, profuse sweating, recumbency; raised serum CK, ALP, AST
  • Gross lesions — muscle discolouration foci and haemorrhage; swollen congested kidneys; brown urine (myoglobinuria) in bladder
  • Microscopic — myofiber degeneration/necrosis: swollen, eosinophilic, hyaline, fragmented myofibers with lost striations, macrophage infiltration of necrotic areas; renal tubular necrosis with myoglobin casts

Post-Parturient Haemoglobinuria

  • Acute haemolytic anaemia of dairy cows, usually multiparous cows a few weeks post-partum
  • Haemoglobinuria from severe haemolysis, RBC count <1 million; erythrocytes may contain Heinz bodies
  • Clinical signs — icterus, oedema, laboured breathing
  • Lesions — centrilobular hepatic necrosis, haemoglobinuric nephropathy
  • Pathogenesis related to hypophosphataemia

Ketosis EXAM HOT

  • Condition where ketone bodies (acetone, acetoacetic acid, β-hydroxybutyric acid) appear in blood and urine, from decreased blood glucose or decreased glucose utilisation; fatty acid oxidation compensates for the glucose deficit, generating ketone bodies as an alternate energy source
  • Causes — starvation, diabetes mellitus, pregnancy, lactation, loss of appetite, digestive disorders
  • Primary ketosis — simple starvation; Secondary ketosis — starvation secondary to another disease
  • Symptoms — anorexia, depression, coma, sweet smell (from ketone bodies)
  • Diagnosis — urinalysis positive for acetoacetic/β-hydroxybutyric acid; milk analysis for acetone; hyperlipaemia with hepatocyte fatty change; acidosis (alkali ion depletion from ketone acid neutralisation); hypoglycaemia
  • Mechanism — oxidation of free fatty acids via acetyl-CoA depends on continuous oxaloacetate supply for the TCA cycle; when active gluconeogenesis consumes oxaloacetate, acetyl-CoA is diverted to ketogenesis instead; small amounts of ketone bodies are normally generated/oxidised, but excess production leads first to ketonuria, then (exceeding renal excretion capacity) to ketosis; develops via starvation (increased gluconeogenesis demand), diabetes mellitus (inability to use glucose — the ONLY hyperglycaemic cause), and pregnancy/lactation (increased glucose demand, both hypoglycaemic)
  • Bovine ketosis — can develop from starvation or diets with excess butyric acid, but usual form is spontaneous ketosis in lactating dairy cows on an increased nutrition plane; mammary gland's immense lactose-synthesis demand exceeds available glucose → hypoglycaemia; signs: anorexia, depression, occasional excitability, incoordination, terminal coma; outstanding lesion is severe hepatic fatty change (shared with pregnancy toxaemia of ewes)
Chapter recap
  • Shared theme — most of these are metabolic "production diseases" of pregnancy/parturition/lactation stress, not simple dietary deficiencies.
  • Milk fever — sudden post-partum hypocalcaemia, head turned to flank.
  • Azoturia — "Monday morning sickness," rested horse on full rations, myoglobinuria + renal tubular necrosis.
  • Post-parturient haemoglobinuria — hypophosphataemia-linked haemolysis with Heinz bodies.
  • Ketosis — diabetes = hyperglycaemic cause; starvation/pregnancy/lactation = hypoglycaemic causes; severe hepatic fatty change is the outstanding lesion in bovine ketosis and ewe pregnancy toxaemia alike.

Heavy Metal Toxicology — Arsenic, Lead, Mercury & Copper

The four classical heavy metal poisonings of livestock share a pattern of GI corrosion and/or neurological damage, but each has a distinctive clinical and pathological signature that allows differentiation on history, signs, and tissue analysis alone.

Arsenic EXAM HOT

  • One of the most important toxicological hazards to farm animals, though less frequent now due to declining arsenical pesticide/wood-preservative use; drinking water >0.25% arsenic is potentially toxic to large animals; herbivores commonly poisoned via contaminated forage; lead arsenate mistaken for lime causes dairy cattle toxicity; sheep poisoned via arsenical dips
  • Clinical signs — acute, mainly GI and cardiovascular; direct capillary damage causes plasma transudation, blood loss, hypovolaemic shock; profuse watery ("rice water"), sometimes blood-tinged diarrhoea; severe colic, dehydration, weakness, weak pulse, cardiovascular collapse; onset within hours, up to 24 hrs; peracute cases found dead; subacute cases: colic, anorexia, staggering, bloody diarrhoea, polyuria then anuria, partial hind-limb paralysis, trembling, stupor, cold extremities, subnormal temperature; chronic (rare): wasting, thirst, brick-red mucous membranes
  • Post-mortem lesions — GI mucosal inflammation/reddening, oedema, vessel rupture, epithelial/subepithelial necrosis, possible perforation; fluid, foul-smelling, blood-tinged GI contents with epithelial shreds; diffuse hepatic/renal/visceral inflammation; hepatic fatty degeneration/necrosis; renal tubular damage; cutaneous exposure causes dry/leathery skin necrosis
  • Diagnosis — arsenic in liver/kidney (toxic >3 ppm wet weight) or stomach contents (first 24–48 hrs); urinary arsenic elevated for up to 14 days; sudden colic + bloody/watery diarrhoea with mucosal shreds is highly suggestive — no other metal/metalloid causes such rapid-onset GI damage
  • Organic arsenicals — from excess arsenic feed additives in pig/poultry diets; pigs show reduced weight gain, incoordination, posterior paralysis, quadriplegia (animals stay alert, retain appetite); blindness is characteristic of arsanilic acid specifically; ruminants show inorganic-like signs from phenylarsonic toxicosis; lesions: demyelination/gliosis of peripheral nerves, optic tract/nerve

Lead Poisoning

One of the most common causes of metallic poisoning in dogs and cattle.

  • Sources — lead-based paints, seedling/harvesting activities, improper used-oil/battery disposal, lead smelter vegetation, roadside contamination from leaded petrol exhaust, lead arsenate insecticide
  • Cattle — signs within 24 hrs: ataxia, blindness, salivation, eyelid twitching, jaw champing, bruxism, tremors, convulsions; subacute (sheep/older cattle): anorexia, rumen stasis, colic, dullness, constipation→diarrhoea, blindness, head pressing, bruxism, hyperaesthesia, incoordination
  • Horses — chronic syndrome: weight loss, depression, weakness, colic, diarrhoea, laryngeal/pharyngeal paralysis ("roaring"), dysphagia, aspiration pneumonia
  • Birds — anorexia, ataxia, weight loss, wing/leg weakness, anaemia, GI signs
  • Dogs — anorexia, colic, vomiting/diarrhoea, anxiety, hysterical barking, jaw champing, salivation, blindness, ataxia, muscle spasms, opisthotonus, convulsions (or CNS depression instead of excitation)
  • Post-mortem lesions — few gross lesions in acute cases; paint/battery fragments in GI tract; gastroenteritis from irritant action; cerebral congestion, flattened cortical gyri; histologically: endothelial swelling, laminar cortical necrosis, white matter oedema; renal tubular necrosis with intranuclear acid-fast inclusion bodies; osteoporosis reported in affected lambs
  • Diagnosis — blood lead 0.35 ppm (normal 0.05–0.25 ppm) diagnostic; liver/kidney cortex ~10 ppm; anaemia with basophilic stippling, anisocytosis, poikilocytosis, polychromasia, hypochromasia; ALA (aminolevulinic acid) >500 mg/100ml (normal ~140) is a sensitive exposure indicator

Mercury

  • Organic vs inorganic forms have dissimilar toxicity; organic mercurials are less toxic; both forms convert to methyl mercury in water bodies, bioaccumulating up to 300-fold in fish
  • Sources — inorganic — mercuric/mercurous chloride, mercuric oxide/iodide/nitrate; dogs/cats especially susceptible to mercurial ointments (absorption + licking); mercury vapour absorbed via respiratory tract (very toxic to sheep/cattle)
  • Sources — organic — fungicidal seed dressings (ethyl/methyl mercuric compounds), antiseptics (mercurochrome, thiomersal), diuretics (mersalyl); secondary poisoning from eating animals fed mercurial fungicides
  • Clinical signs — inorganic — vomiting, diarrhoea, colic, polydipsia, albuminuria/anuria (severe); cattle: rapid death, colic, subnormal temperature; inhalation: dyspnoea, cough, nasal discharge, fever, mucosal bleeding
  • Clinical signs — organic — CNS stimulation and locomotor abnormality after weeks of latency; blindness, excitation, abnormal chewing, incoordination, convulsions; cats: hind-leg rigidity, hypermetria, cerebellar ataxia, tremors (often irreversible)
  • Post-mortem lesions — gastroenteritis, stomatitis, gingivitis, acute parenchymatous nephritis; pulmonary oedema, hydrothorax, hydropericardium, epicardial/endocardial haemorrhage (vapour inhalation); renal convoluted tubule necrosis; organic form: neuronal degeneration (sensory), perivascular cuffing in cerebrocortical grey matter, cerebellar granular layer atrophy, Purkinje cell damage
  • Diagnosis — mercury in stomach contents/kidney cortex/liver (inorganic); tissue (blood, kidney, brain) and feed levels (<1 ppm normal) for organic form; differential includes other metals/insecticides and feline parvovirus cerebellar disease

Copper Toxicity EXAM HOT

Sheep affected most often, though other species also susceptible.

  • Sources — acute — excessive therapeutic dosing, contaminated feed; foot-rot bath water/udder contamination; copper edentate for swayback treatment even at recommended dose in undersized lambs
  • Sources — chronic — orchard copper spray grazing, copper salt-lick/supplemented diets, algicide-treated ponds, chicken manure mixed with silage, mine/smelter contamination; hepatotoxic plants (Heliotropium, Senecio) causing abnormal copper accumulation; high Cu:Mo ratio in plants (low Mo intake favours hepatic Cu storage)
  • Acute clinical signs — nausea, vomiting, salivation, purgation, violent abdominal pain, dehydration, tachycardia, shock, death; deep green faeces from copper-chlorophyll compound
  • Chronic — three stages — Stage 1 (2–3 months): no clinical signs except decreased ruminal fermentation/stasis; Stage 2 (14–25 days): hepatic impairment — anorexia, depression, weakness, thirst, diarrhoea; Stage 3 (haemolytic crisis, 2–5 days): generalised icterus, haemoglobinaemia, haemoglobinuria, recumbency; severe hepatic insufficiency causes death; survivors may still die of subsequent renal failure; herd mortality often <5% but >75% of affected animals die; losses continue up to 2 months after correcting the diet
  • Post-mortem — acute — severe gastroenteritis with abomasal erosion/ulceration; blood found coagulated at death; icterus in animals surviving >24 hrs
  • Post-mortem — chronic — generalised icterus; enlarged, yellow, friable liver; gall bladder distended with thick greenish-brown bile; swollen gunmetal-coloured kidneys with haemorrhagic mottling; port-wine urine; enlarged spleen with dark "blackberry jam" parenchyma; centrilobular hepatic and renal tubular necrosis; spongy brain degeneration with astrocyte damage
  • Diagnosis — elevated blood copper (5–20 µg/ml vs normal ~1 µg/ml) during haemolytic crisis; liver Cu >150 ppm wet weight (sheep) is significant; acute: blue-green ingesta, deep green faeces, elevated faecal (8000–10000 ppm) and kidney (>15 ppm) copper
Chapter recap
  • Arsenic — rapid-onset "rice water" diarrhoea; no other metal produces such speedy GI damage.
  • Lead — ataxia/blindness/bruxism in cattle; "roaring" in horses; basophilic stippling + raised ALA are diagnostic blood findings.
  • Mercury — inorganic = GI/renal; organic = delayed-onset CNS/cerebellar signs, often irreversible.
  • Copper — three-stage chronic disease culminating in a haemolytic crisis; "blackberry jam" spleen and gunmetal kidneys are classic; high herd mortality once the crisis begins despite low overall incidence.

Nitrate/Nitrite & Fluorine Toxicology

Nitrate/nitrite poisoning and fluorine toxicology both arise mainly from environmental/agricultural contamination rather than deliberate exposure, and both have a distinctive acute presentation (methaemoglobinaemia vs corrosive GI damage) alongside a separate chronic bone/tooth syndrome for fluorine.

Nitrates and Nitrites EXAM HOT

  • Closely linked, difficult to differentiate, since nitrate converts to nitrite in the body and both are toxic; ruminants convert plant nitrate to nitrite in the rumen, then to ammonia (used by rumen microflora for protein synthesis); toxicity occurs when nitrate→nitrite reduction outpaces nitrite→ammonia conversion, allowing nitrite to accumulate and be absorbed
  • Reduction depends on fermentable carbohydrate supply (provides hydrogen ions); poor feeding reduces microbial biotransformation, increasing nitrite production; horses have limited GI nitrate reduction; ruminants most commonly affected, though humans/horses/pigs/dogs also poisoned; pigs most susceptible to nitrite; non-ruminants susceptible to nitrite but NOT nitrate (no rumen flora); a single high dose is more toxic than the same/higher dose divided over 24 hrs; food deprivation increases toxicity, concentrate-rich feed reduces it
  • Sources — >80 nitrate-accumulating plants (roots/stems > leaves); nitrate-rich soil/groundwater/silage; chemical fertilisers, herbicides (2,4-D), excess rain, low light, frost/drought, low soil pH/temperature/aeration, soil Mo/S/P/Fe deficiency, plant viral infection all favour plant nitrate accumulation; also nitrate fertiliser ingestion, contaminated shallow well/pond water, animal waste, sewage, silo juices, industrial effluent; roughage-only diets more susceptible than concentrate-fed; young animals more susceptible; hypovitaminosis A, pre-existing anaemia, methaemoglobinaemia, fasting all aggravate toxicity; monensin facilitates rumen nitrate→nitrite conversion
  • Acute clinical signs — abrupt onset, dyspnoea with violent respiratory effort/gasping, rapid respiration (predominant sign), salivation, colourless urine, vomiting, diarrhoea, colic, rapid weak pulse, progressive cyanosis, muscular weakness, ataxia, recumbency, terminal anoxic convulsions, death within 2–24 hrs; bluish mucosal discoloration and chocolate-brown blood are characteristic, with NO change in coagulation time; can cross the placenta and convert foetal Hb to Met-Hb, causing abortion
  • Chronic (rare) — poorly nourished animals on contaminated water/nitrate-rich fodder; abortion, poor mammary development, infertility, lower birth weight; ewes show hypothyroidism/hypovitaminosis A; ataxia, tremors, immunosuppression, increased mastitis/metritis/diarrhoea incidence
  • Post-mortem lesions — dark chocolate/coffee-brown poorly-clotting blood; brown-stained tissues; congested/inflamed intra-abdominal organs; petechial/large serosal haemorrhages; generalised cyanosis; blood-tinged pericardial fluid
  • Diagnosis — chocolate-brown blood/tissue; ruminal/intestinal content nitrate/nitrite analysis; urinary nitrite; meth-Hb and serum nitrite (diphenylamine spot test); ocular fluid diphenylamine test in dead animals; response to methylene blue treatment

Fluorine Toxicology

  • Highly reactive, found combined with many minerals (aluminium, iron, calcium, silicates, phosphates); emitted from aluminium/steel/phosphate-fertiliser/brick/pottery/ferro-enamel industries, volcanic activity; low-level chronic exposure causes gradual bone/tooth accumulation without early signs — damage becomes apparent only after long periods

Acute fluorine poisoning

  • From accidental ingestion of fluorine salts (sodium fluoride vermifuge, sodium fluoroacetate rodenticide, sodium fluorosilicate) or excessive phosphate rock licking; pigs most commonly affected — >4-5% sodium fluoride in feed is lethal; lethal fluorosilicate dose: 100g (equine), 200g (bovine)
  • Clinical signs — vomiting, anorexia, salivation, ruminal stasis, abdominal pain, gastroenteritis, diarrhoea, weakness, constant chewing, dyspnoea, excitability, tremors, pupillary dilation, tetany, clonic convulsions, sudden collapse, coma, death (respiratory/cardiac failure)
  • Lesions — haemorrhagic gastroenteritis, hepatic/renal congestion; NO bone/dental lesions in acute poisoning

Chronic fluoride poisoning (Fluorosis) EXAM HOT

  • From prolonged small-dose ingestion (industrial pollution, contaminated water, fluoride-rich mineral/phosphate rock supplements); non-fatal but productivity-reducing; 95–96% of total body fluorine deposits in bone/teeth (which act as a "sink"), giving a very long latent period; safe ingestion levels vary by species (young cattle/milking cows <30 ppm, beef cattle <40, sheep <50, pigs <70, horses <90, chickens <150 dry-weight ppm)
  • Dental signs — earliest/mildest is mottling — light yellow/green/brown/black horizontal spots/bands; molars/premolars worst affected (hard to examine); progresses to pitting, brittle uneven teeth, gum-level wear, or shedding; young animals show delayed permanent incisor eruption, oblique eruption, enamel hypoplasia; mastication difficulty causes poor growth and adult acetonaemia
  • Osteofluorosis signs — sudden-onset lameness (moving/diagonal, one leg then another), unthriftiness, stiffness, painful gait; most marked in loins/hip joints/hind limbs; visibly enlarged/thickened bones — proximal metatarsal medial surface, mandible, sternum, metacarpals, ribs, spine; periosteal thickening causes lateral exostoses; articular surfaces spared, but joint spurring/bridging causes spinal rigidity; hyperostosis, osteoporosis, chalky-white roughened appearance, fracture-prone
  • Other signs — weight loss, intermittent diarrhoea, polydipsia/polyuria, aplastic anaemia (diminished marrow cavity), reduced milk/wool production, anoestrus; fluoride crosses the placenta but no apparent foetal toxic effect known
  • Post-mortem — chalky-white roughened enlarged bones with lateral exostoses; mottled/cavitated/unevenly broken teeth; diminished, gelatinous, aplastic-anaemic bone marrow; microscopically thickened dense bony trabeculae with sharp heavy outlines; renal tubular degeneration, mild glomerular change, arteriolar thickening/fibrosis; spongiosa atrophy, defective osseous calcification, enamel/dentine hypoplasia; severe gastroenteritis is ABSENT (unlike acute poisoning)
  • Diagnosis — X-ray shows sclerosis, perosis, hyperostosis, increased abnormal porosity, periosteal feathering/thickening
Chapter recap
  • Nitrate/nitrite — chocolate-brown blood + bluish mucosa are pathognomonic; methylene blue is both diagnostic and therapeutic; pigs are most nitrite-susceptible.
  • Acute vs chronic fluorosis — acute = GI/nervous signs with NO bone/tooth lesions; chronic = mottled teeth + osteofluorosis with NO severe gastroenteritis — mirror-image lesion patterns.
  • Fluorosis — 95-96% of body fluorine sinks into bone/teeth, explaining the very long latency before signs appear.

Plant Toxin Poisoning — Cyanogenetic Plants, Strychnine & Oxalate

Three of the most important plant-source poisonings in grazing livestock, each with a distinct mechanism: cyanide blocks cellular respiration at cytochrome oxidase, strychnine blocks spinal inhibitory neurotransmission, and oxalate chelates blood calcium and crystallises in the kidney.

Cyanogenetic Plant Poisoning EXAM HOT

  • Plants containing hydrocyanic acid (HCN) or cyanogenic/cyanogenetic glycosides; cyanide exists as free HCN or bound glycoside; free HCN releases by physical disruption (mastication, trampling), stress (drought, frost), or enzyme action (β-glycosidase, hydroxynitrile lyase) from the plant itself, other plants, or GI bacteria; in ruminants, ruminal microflora enzymes liberate free HCN from the glycoside
  • Susceptibility — cattle and buffaloes most susceptible; sheep/goats relatively less susceptible (forestomach enzyme differences); monogastrics (pig, horse) relatively resistant (gastric acidity destroys the glycoside)
  • Poisoning occurs when plant HCN content is high and large amounts are ingested quickly; starved/underfed animals released to graze ingest toxic amounts rapidly
  • Sources — >120 cyanogenetic plants; commonly: Sorghum species, Acacia leucophloea, Prunus sp., linseed, sugarcane tops, Zea mays, Nerium oleander, Eucalyptus, others; in India, green Sorghum fodder (immature plants/young shoots) is the most common source, plus accidental Acacia leucophloea pod/leaf ingestion by sheep/goats
  • Mechanism — acute poisoning causes cytotoxic anoxia (the actual cause of death) via cyanide's high affinity for cytochrome oxidase, blocking cellular respiration
  • Clinical signs — usually acute in the field; sudden death (10–25 minutes after ingesting large amounts) with no premonitory signs; if less severe: dyspnoea, anxiety, restlessness, stumbling gait, tremors, recumbency, terminal clonic convulsions; excitement, staggering, muscle tremors, hyperaesthesia, lacrimation, hypersalivation, bright red mucosa, pupil dilation, nystagmus, terminal convulsions; mucosae congested/cyanotic terminally; chronic toxicity (unproven) suspected to cause urinary incontinence, hair loss, arthrogryposis
  • Post-mortem lesions — abomasal/small intestinal/tracheal/pulmonary/cardiac congestion and/or haemorrhage; unclotted, bright red blood; "bitter almonds" smell on opening the stomach/rumen; HCN detectable in stomach/ruminal contents, liver, muscle (send muscle if PM is delayed 1–2 days)
  • Diagnosis — history of grazing near suspected plants, sudden death, acute anoxic syndrome, bright red blood/mucosa, picrate paper test for HCN in plant material or GI contents

Strychnos nux-vomica (Strychnine) Poisoning

  • Uncommon in farm animals; usually from accidental ingestion of Strychnos nux-vomica seeds or powdered nux-vomica used as bait against dogs/foxes/rats; historically used in human/veterinary medicine as an analeptic/nervine tonic/ruminotoric, introduced as a rodenticide in 1690, now rarely in therapeutic use
  • Sources — dogs/cats via seed or bait ingestion, or eating poisoned birds/rodents; others via treated peanuts/wheat
  • Mechanism — acts on recurrent inhibitory interneurons (Renshaw cells) of the spinal/medullary reflex arc; competitive glycine receptor antagonist, blocking glycine-mediated post-synaptic inhibition; loss of motor inhibition → uncontrolled spinal reflex excitation, extensor muscle stimulation, extensor rigidity, tonic seizures
  • Clinical signs — onset 10 minutes–2 hours; initial nervousness, restlessness, muscle tremors, stiffness, hyperirritability, intermittent tonic spasms triggered by noise/touch/light; progresses to continuous tetanic seizures with marked rigidity; antagonistic skeletal contraction — extended limbs, neck curved upward/backward (opisthotonus), since extensors dominate flexors; intermittent convulsions initially, with any slight stimulus triggering full tetanic spasm; "saw horse" stance; dilated pupils; elevated temperature; death from asphyxia (prolonged respiratory muscle paralysis), usually within hours, up to 48 hrs; animal remains conscious until near death; vomiting is rare
  • Post-mortem lesions — no specific lesions except anoxic petechiae from respiratory arrest during spasms; dark venous blood; engorged lungs/cerebral meninges; stomach empty or with meal remnants
  • Diagnosis — clinical picture, myoglobinuria, strychnine detection in urine, stomach contents, liver, kidneys, or suspected baits; rapid rigor mortis onset

Oxalate Poisoning

  • Oxalic acid readily forms insoluble salts with calcium/magnesium; sodium/potassium/ammonium oxalate salts are highly soluble and can induce toxicity
  • Sources — palatable oxalate-containing plants forming a large part of ruminant rations; toxicity requires >10% oxalic acid dry weight; form matters — acid oxalate (Oxalis, Rumex) vs oxalate ion (certain Chenopodiaceae); common plants: Amaranthus retroflexus, Beta vulgaris, Rumex sp., Halogeton glomeratus, spinach, others
  • Mechanism — gastric calcium normally reacts with ingested oxalate to form insoluble, unabsorbable calcium oxalate excreted in faeces (more efficient in ruminants); when intake exceeds this conversion capacity, soluble oxalate is absorbed via intestinal mucosa and chelates blood calcium → acute hypocalcaemia; calcium oxalate crystals accumulate in renal tubules causing severe renal damage; crystals may also form in the brain
  • Clinical signs — after ~4 hours of feeding on oxalate-rich plants: dullness, lowered head, anorexia, herd isolation; followed by excessive frothy salivation, progressive incoordination, coma with deep irregular respiration; convulsive episodes may occur
  • Post-mortem lesions — lungs filled with dark-red/purplish blood (most deaths are asphyxia-related); petechial haemorrhage and cyanosis at various sites; crystalline masses in renal tubules (concentrated in the cortex); microscopically: calcium oxalate crystals, ruptured tubules, degenerative changes
  • Diagnosis — history of grazing oxalate-rich plants, clinical signs, post-mortem findings
Chapter recap
  • Cyanide — blocks cytochrome oxidase → cytotoxic anoxia; bitter almonds smell + bright red unclotted blood are pathognomonic; cattle/buffalo most susceptible, monogastrics most resistant.
  • Strychnine — glycine receptor antagonist at Renshaw cells; opisthotonus + "saw horse" stance; animal stays conscious until near death.
  • Oxalate — chelates blood calcium (acute hypocalcaemia) + forms renal tubular crystals; ruminants tolerate more than monogastrics due to gastric calcium-oxalate conversion.

Toxicology of Agrochemicals — Organochlorine & Organophosphorus Insecticides

Agrochemical toxicology is dominated by two insecticide classes with opposite mechanisms: organochlorines disrupt neuronal sodium/chloride channel function directly, while organophosphates poison acetylcholinesterase, causing acetylcholine accumulation — a distinction that gives each a recognisably different clinical picture.

Pesticides — general context

  • USEPA definition — any substance/mixture intended for preventing, destroying, repelling, or mitigating any pest
  • Insecticide classes — Organochlorines (DDT, endrin, heptachlor); Organophosphates (parathion, sumithion, malathion); Carbamates (carbaryl, aldicarb)
  • Herbicides — dinitro compounds, phenoxyacetic acids (2,4-D, 2,4,5-T), chloroaliphatic acids (dalapon), triazines (atrazine, simazine), bipyridiniums (paraquat, diquat), substituted ureas, dinitroanilines
  • Rodenticides — warfarins, zinc phosphide, fluoroacetate
  • Sources of exposure — accidental (sprayed-crop/water ingestion, contaminated concentrate feed, contaminated containers, treated walls/pillars, improper storage, aerial-spray drift, water contamination); intentional (residues in produce/animal products, suicide); occupational (manufacturing workers, faulty spraying)

Organochlorine Insecticides EXAM HOT

First-generation insecticides ("chlorinated hydrocarbons"), used as contact insecticides/ectoparasiticides.

  • Sources — ingestion of contaminated feed/water; inhalation/skin absorption from topical ectoparasiticide application
  • Absorption/fate — water-insoluble but oil/organic-solvent soluble; rapidly absorbed from oily preparations, penetrate intact skin as oily solution/emulsion (dieldrin is exceptional in also absorbing from dry powder); all compounds penetrate insect cuticle more readily than mammalian skin/intestinal mucosa, explaining greater insect toxicity; DDT is dehydrochlorinated to persistent DDE, excreted as conjugates in urine; methoxychlor is rapidly degraded/excreted via faeces (low toxicity, low accumulation); aldrin is metabolised to dieldrin (more toxic than the parent compound); except methoxychlor, organochlorines are stored in body fat (not other vital organs)
  • Mechanism — neuro-poisons; high lipid solubility lets them enter neural membranes and disrupt sodium channel function; DDT reduces potassium transport, inactivates sodium channel closure, inhibits Na+-K+ and Ca2+-Mg2+ ATPases, inhibits calmodulin-Ca2+ binding (releasing neurotransmitter); cyclodiene compounds antagonise GABA receptors at chloride channels and inhibit Ca2+-Mg2+ ATPase; lindane (an HCH isomer) binds GABA receptors, inhibiting GABA-dependent chloride influx, though less potently than cyclodienes (hence less toxic)
  • Clinical signs — dichlorodiphenylethanes: initial CNS stimulation then depression, death from respiratory failure; chronic: liver damage, hypoglycaemia, reduced liver glycogen, blood lactacidaemia, hyperkalaemia; cyclodiene poisoning resembles DDT poisoning but more severe: teeth grinding, difficult respiration, eyelid snapping, frequent urination, backward walking, wall climbing, aimless jumping, violent frenzied behaviour
  • Three clinical categories — 1) Behavioural: anxiety, aggressiveness, abnormal posturing, jumping over unseen objects, wall climbing, "madness syndrome"; 2) Neurological: hypersensitivity to stimuli, facial/eyelid fasciculation, fore/hindquarter muscle spasm/twitching, jaw champing, hyperthermia (may progress to coma if death doesn't occur first); 3) Cholinergic: vomiting, marked salivation, mydriasis, diarrhoea, micturition
  • Post-mortem lesions — no specific CNS lesions; acute aldrin poisoning may cause hepatitis and acute tubular nephrosis; chronic DDT/methoxychlor toxicosis may produce focal centrilobular hepatic necrosis
  • Diagnosis — exposure history, clinical/PM findings, feed and biological sample (liver, kidney in dead; blood, milk in live) analysis for organochlorine compounds

Organophosphorus Insecticides (OPIs) EXAM HOT

Constitute the bulk of agricultural/veterinary/public-health insecticide use, preferred for high selectivity, low mammalian toxicity and rapid degradation, but responsible for frequent poisoning from indiscriminate/careless use; also implicated in cancer, reproductive disorders, peripheral neuropathy, impaired immunity, neurobehavioural change. Examples: chlorpyriphos, diazinon, dichlorvos, malathion, parathion, TEPP, and the nerve agents sarin/soman/tabun.

  • Mechanism — inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE), the enzyme hydrolysing acetylcholine to acetic acid + choline; two cholinesterase types exist — true/specific AChE (erythrocytes, muscle, nervous system) and pseudo/butyrylcholinesterase (BuChE, non-specific, plasma/nervous system); toxicological effects arise specifically from AChE inhibition and resulting endogenous acetylcholine accumulation (BuChE inhibition produces no apparent toxicity); death typically occurs when brain AChE is inhibited ~95%; erythrocyte cholinesterase is a good (though imperfect) surrogate index; acetylcholine rises 2–3 fold in blood/brain during OPI poisoning in cats/dogs/rabbits
  • Symptom categories
    • Muscarinic (smooth muscle, heart, exocrine glands) — bronchoconstriction, increased bronchial secretion, hypersalivation (watery drooling saliva), lacrimation, sweating, increased GI tone, involuntary urination/defecation, diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, dyspnoea, hypotension, bradycardia, miosis
    • Nicotinic (skeletal motor endplates, autonomic ganglia) — muscular weakness, twitching, fasciculation, cramps, tremors, atrophy; respiratory muscle weakness → dyspnoea/cyanosis
    • CNS — restlessness, convulsions, ataxia, respiratory/circulatory centre depression → cardiac/respiratory arrest, tremors, coma; death is from asphyxial respiratory failure
    • Delayed neuropathy — from phosphate trimesters, most commonly triorthocresyl phosphate (TOCP); distal lower limb functional disturbance progressing to weakness/flaccidity; caused by neurotoxic esterase (NTE) inhibition — "organophosphorus-induced delayed neurotoxicity" (OPIDN); onset delayed even after a single toxic dose
  • Route-dependent first symptom appearance — spray/dust: respiratory and ocular; oral: gastrointestinal; dermal: localised sweating
  • Timing — acute toxicity more common than chronic; onset usually 10 minutes–2/3 hours; symptom duration typically 1–5 days; fatal untreated poisoning kills within 24 hours; onset/duration varies by compound metabolism, biodistribution, and AChE affinity
  • Post-mortem lesions — no characteristic lesions; common findings: haemorrhagic gastroenteritis, pulmonary oedema, hepatic/renal degenerative change (usually secondary to clinical signs)
  • Diagnosis — history/circumstantial evidence; symptomatic evidence differentiated from organochlorine poisoning by specific clinical signs

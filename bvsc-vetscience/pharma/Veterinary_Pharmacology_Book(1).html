Chapter 01

Autonomic Nervous System

Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) is also known as the visceral, vegetative or involuntary nervous system. It is widely distributed throughout the body controlling autonomic and vegetative functions. The ANS regulates smooth muscles, cardiac muscle and secretory glands, maintaining homeostasis.

Definition & Functions

  • Regulates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and secretory glands (not endocrine glands)
  • Maintains respiration, blood pressure, heart rate, GI activity and urinary output
  • Mediates the body's response to emergency situations
  • Conserves energy and maintains homeostasis

Anatomical Divisions of the Nervous System

DivisionTypeKey Feature
CNSBrain + Spinal cordIntegration and coordination centre
ANS (PNS)InvoluntaryInnervates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands at neuroeffector junctions
SMS (PNS)VoluntaryInnervates skeletal muscle at neuromuscular junctions

Afferent Fibres

  • First link in the reflex arcs of ANS — arise from visceral structures
  • Generally {em("non-myelinated")}; cell bodies in dorsal root ganglia of spinal nerves
  • Convey information to brain → processed → response via efferents

Central Connections

StructureRole

Medulla oblongataControls blood pressure and respiration
HypothalamusPrincipal locus of integration — body temperature, water balance, carbohydrate metabolism, sexual reflexes
Cerebral cortexControls volitional and conditioned autonomic responses

Efferent Fibres

Two neurons — Preganglionic and Postganglionic — plus effector cells are generally involved. Preganglionic fibres exit the spinal cord and terminate in ganglia; acetylcholine (ACh) is the neurotransmitter at autonomic ganglia (nicotinic cholinergic synapse). Postganglionic fibres exit from ganglia and innervate effector cells — ACh mediates muscarinic transmission; norepinephrine mediates adrenergic transmission.

Principles of Dual Innervation

  • Antagonistic effects: In organs with both divisions, effects are usually opposed — e.g., heart, bladder, bronchi, GI tract

Neurohumoral Transmission

Neurohumoral transmission implies that nerves transmit messages across a synapse and neuroeffector junction by the release of humoral or chemical messengers. The four main events are: axonal conduction, synthesis and release of neurotransmitter, receptor events, and catabolism of neurotransmitter.

Axonal Conduction

  • Passage of impulse along a nerve fibre — dependent on selective changes in permeability of axonal membrane to electrolytes
  • Resting membrane potential ≈ −85 mV — due to leaking of K⁺ ions against concentration gradient
  • Action potential = reversal of polarization — result of permeability changes at axonal surface
  • Suprathreshold stimulus → Na⁺ influx → depolarisation → propagated impulse

Receptor Events

Neurotransmitter binds to postjunctional receptors (ionotropic or metabotropic). Ionotropic receptors cause direct ion channel opening. Metabotropic receptors (G-protein coupled) activate second messengers (cAMP, IP₃, DAG). The net result is either excitation or inhibition of the effector cell.

Criteria for Neurotransmitter

  • Must be synthesized and stored in the neuron
  • Released by neuronal stimulation in a calcium-dependent manner
  • Must produce a response in postsynaptic cell identical to nerve stimulation
  • Specific mechanism for termination of action must exist

Chapter 04

Cholinergic Pharmacology — I

Acetylcholine (ACh) is the neurotransmitter at parasympathetic neuroeffector junctions, autonomic ganglia, adrenal medulla, somatic myoneural junctions and certain CNS regions.

Synthesis of Acetylcholine EXAM HOT

ACh is synthesized in cholinergic nerve endings: Choline + Acetyl-CoA → ACh, catalysed by choline acetyltransferase (also called choline acetylase). Uptake of choline is the rate-limiting step, blocked by Hemicholinium which depletes ACh.

Storage and Release

  • Most ACh stored in ionic solution within small synaptic vesicles; some free ACh in cytoplasm
  • Transport of ACh into vesicles blocked by vesamicol
  • Depolarisation → Ca²⁺ influx → fusion of axonal and vesicular membranes → exocytosis (quantal release)
  • Mg²⁺ inhibits ACh release; Ca²⁺ ionophores stimulate release
  • Botulinum toxin — inhibits release (paralysis); Black widow spider venom — massive release

Destruction of Acetylcholine

ACh is hydrolysed immediately after release by cholinesterase → choline + acetate. Choline is actively recycled into the prejunctional nerve cell.

Cholinergic Pharmacology — II

Cholinergic receptors are of two types: Muscarinic (G-protein coupled) and Nicotinic (ligand-gated ion channels). Named after muscarine (from Amanita muscaria) and nicotine respectively.

Muscarinic Receptors EXAM HOT

G-protein coupled receptors. Blocked by atropine (alkaloid from Atropa belladonna). Located on autonomic effector cells: heart, blood vessels, eye, smooth muscle, glands of GI/respiratory/urinary tracts, sweat glands and CNS. Five subtypes: M1, M2, M3, M4, M5 — of which M1, M2, M3 are most important.

Anticholinesterase Drugs

Anticholinesterases act by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase (AChE), thereby increasing the concentration of ACh at cholinergic synapses. They are classified as reversible and irreversible inhibitors.

Classification EXAM HOT

TypeDrugsDuration
Reversible (short-acting)EdrophoniumMinutes (ionic bond only)
Reversible (medium-acting)Physostigmine, Neostigmine, Pyridostigmine, DistigmineHours (carbamylation)
IrreversibleOrganophosphorus compounds: DFP, TEPP, Parathion, Malathion, Sarin, TabunPermanent (phosphorylation) — "aging"

Mechanism of Action

AChE has two sites: anionic site (binds quaternary N of ACh) and esteratic site (hydrolytic site). Edrophonium binds electrostatically to the anionic site only (very short-lived). Physostigmine/Neostigmine form carbamyl-enzyme complex at esteratic site (hydrolysed slowly in hours). Organophosphates phosphorylate the esteratic site irreversibly — enzyme cannot be regenerated once "aged".

Organophosphate Poisoning — DUMBELS EXAM HOT

DUMBELS Mnemonic

Treatment of Organophosphate Poisoning

  • Atropine — muscarinic antagonist; large doses to block all muscarinic effects; does NOT reactivate enzyme
  • Pralidoxime (2-PAM) — reactivates AChE if given early (before "aging"); ineffective for carbamates; also relieves nicotinic effects
  • Supportive care: maintain airway, anticonvulsants (diazepam)
Memory — Reversal

Catecholamines

Catecholamines are compounds containing a catechol nucleus (3,4-dihydroxybenzene) with an amine side chain. Endogenous catecholamines: Epinephrine (EPI), Norepinephrine (NE), Dopamine. Synthetic: Isoproterenol.

Adrenergic Receptor Potency Order EXAM HOT

Receptor EffectPotency Order
α-adrenergic (smooth muscle contraction)EPI ≥ NE >> Isoproterenol
β-adrenergic (smooth muscle relaxation)Isoproterenol > EPI >> NE
OverallNE = potent α; ISO = potent β; EPI = both α and β

Synthesis of Catecholamines EXAM HOT

Precursor = Tyrosine (phenylalanine → tyrosine in liver). Tyrosine → DOPA (cytoplasm) → Dopamine (cytoplasm) → NE (granules) → Epinephrine (adrenal medulla). Tyrosine hydroxylase is the rate-limiting enzyme — inhibited by α-methyl-ρ-tyrosine → catecholamine depletion. Activated by cAMP-dependent protein kinases.

Storage and Release

Storage in granular vesicles complexed with ATP and chromogranin (kept inactive). Vesicles also contain ascorbic acid and dopamine-β-hydroxylase. Release = Ca²⁺-dependent exocytosis triggered by nerve depolarisation.

Inhalant Anaesthetics

Inhalant anaesthetics are volatile liquids or gases that produce unconsciousness when inhaled. Their potency is expressed as MAC (minimum alveolar concentration). Lower MAC = higher potency.

Properties of Ideal Inhalant Anaesthetic EXAM HOT

  • Chemically stable, non-flammable, non-explosive
  • High potency (low MAC), wide safety margin
  • Rapid induction and recovery
  • Non-irritating to airways, pleasant odour
  • No toxicity to liver, kidney, heart
  • Analgesic and muscle relaxant properties
  • Not metabolised significantly

Pharmacokinetics — Uptake and Distribution EXAM HOT

Factors determining speed of induction: (1) Blood:gas partition coefficient — lower = faster induction (less agent needed to equilibrate). (2) Oil:gas partition coefficient = potency. (3) Alveolar ventilation — ↑ ventilation = faster induction. (4) Cardiac output — ↑ CO = slower induction (more drug taken away from lungs). (5) Solubility in blood.

Thiopental (Thiopentone) EXAM HOT

Thiopental — ultra-short-acting barbiturate. Highly lipid soluble → rapid entry into brain → rapid induction. Rapidly redistributes from brain → muscle → fat → terminates action. Wide safety margin, smooth induction and recovery. Used in cats and dogs (and exotic species). Minimal cardiovascular effects at clinical doses.

Hypnotics and Sedatives

Hypnotics and sedatives depress CNS activity. Sedatives reduce anxiety at lower doses; hypnotics induce sleep at higher doses. The distinction is dose-dependent. Main drug classes: Benzodiazepines, Barbiturates, Non-benzodiazepine hypnotics and Phenothiazines.

Benzodiazepines EXAM HOT

Benzodiazepines enhance GABA-A receptor function → ↑ frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening (allosteric modulation at BZ site). They do NOT activate GABA-A on their own — require endogenous GABA. The BZ site is between α and γ subunits.

Pharmacological Effects of Benzodiazepines

  • Anxiolytic: reduce anxiety without significant sedation at low doses
  • Sedative-hypnotic: induce sleep at higher doses
  • Anticonvulsant: first-line for status epilepticus (IV diazepam, lorazepam)
  • Muscle relaxant: via spinal cord GABA receptors
  • Anterograde amnesia: forget events after drug administration
  • Minimal respiratory/CV depression compared to barbiturates
BZ Reversal
Flumazenil — competitive BZ antagonist; reverses sedation/respiratory depression. Short-acting — resedation can occur. Not used for chronic BZ overdose (risk of seizures).

Barbiturates enhance GABA-A at a different site (barbiturate binding site) → ↑ duration of Cl⁻ channel opening (vs BZ = frequency). At high doses, directly activate Cl⁻ channel (without GABA). Hence more dangerous — no ceiling effect. Examples: Phenobarbital (anticonvulsant), Thiopental (induction), Pentobarbital (euthanasia, sedation).

Neuromuscular Blockers — I

Neuromuscular blocking drugs (NMBDs) interrupt transmission at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), producing skeletal muscle relaxation. They are classified as depolarising or non-depolarising (competitive) blockers.

Neuromuscular Junction Physiology EXAM HOT

Motor nerve releases ACh → binds NM nicotinic receptors on motor end plate → Na⁺/K⁺ influx → end plate potential → action potential → muscle contraction. ACh is rapidly hydrolysed by AChE. NMBDs act by either mimicking (depolarising) or competitively blocking (non-depolarising) ACh at NM receptors.

Depolarising Neuromuscular Blockers EXAM HOT

Succinylcholine (Suxamethonium) — the only clinically used depolarising NMBD. Structure: two ACh molecules joined → acts like ACh but is resistant to AChE → persistent depolarisation → Phase I block (fasciculations then flaccid paralysis). With repeated/prolonged doses → Phase II block (resembles non-depolarising block). Hydrolysed by plasma pseudocholinesterase.

Order of Muscle Paralysis EXAM HOT

Small fast muscles (face, eye, digits) → intercostal muscles → limb muscles → diaphragm. Recovery is in reverse order. The diaphragm is last to be paralysed and first to recover (has highest safety factor for neuromuscular transmission).   • Euthanasia component in some protocols (large animals) — must ensure unconsciousness first

Mechanism of Action EXAM HOT

  • All conventional antipsychotics: potent dopamine D2 receptor blockade
  • Block limbic system and mesocortical dopamine pathways
  • Initially: firing of dopamine neurons ↑; dopamine turnover ↑
  • Over time: diminished DA activity in basal ganglia → extrapyramidal effects
  • Atypical antipsychotics: weaker D2 block + 5-HT2A block → ↓ extrapyramidal effects, better negative symptom control

Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS) EXAM HOT

NMS — rare but life-threatening reaction to antipsychotics: fever, muscle rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability. Treat with: stop drug immediately, dantrolene (muscle relaxant), bromocriptine (dopamine agonist), supportive care.

Chlorpromazine — Detailed Profile

  • Prototype phenothiazine; aliphatic group — low potency, high sedation
  • Routes: oral, IM, IV (IV can cause severe hypotension)
  • Potent local anaesthetic; irritant to tissues
  • Lowers seizure threshold — avoid in epileptics
  • Renders patient poikilothermic — body temperature follows environment
  • QT prolongation — ECG monitoring with high doses
  • Conditioned avoidance response selectively inhibited in animals — clinical correlate for antipsychotic activity

Master Quick Revision — 50 Must-Know Points

  1. Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) — also called visceral, vegetative or involuntary nervous system; controls smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and secretory glands
  2. Sympathetic (SNS) = Thoracolumbar; "Fight or Flight"; 1:20 ratio; NE at neuroeffector junction; ACh at ganglia
  3. Parasympathetic (PSNS) = Craniosacral; "Rest and Digest"; 1:1 ratio; ACh at both ganglia and neuroeffector junction
  4. Acetylcholine synthesis — Choline + Acetyl-CoA via choline acetyltransferase; rate-limiting step = choline uptake (blocked by Hemicholinium)
  5. Botulinum toxin = inhibits ACh release (paralysis); Black widow spider venom = massive ACh release
  6. True ChE (AChE) — at cholinergic nerve sites, RBC; more sensitive to physostigmine | Pseudo ChE — in plasma, liver; more sensitive to organophosphates
  7. Muscarinic receptors — M1 (ganglia), M2 (heart — ↓ HR), M3 (smooth muscle/glands); all G-protein coupled; blocked by atropine
  8. Nicotinic receptors — NM (skeletal muscle — blocked by d-tubocurarine), NN (ganglia — blocked by hexamethonium); ligand-gated ion channels
  9. SLUD — Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation = classic muscarinic effects; also miosis, bradycardia, bronchospasm
  10. Organophosphate poisoning treatment: Atropine (blocks muscarinic) + Pralidoxime/2-PAM (reactivates AChE — must give before "aging")
  11. Atropine toxidrome: Hot as a hare, Dry as a bone, Red as a beet, Blind as a bat, Mad as a hatter
  12. Atropine vs Scopolamine: Scopolamine = sedating CNS, better anti-motion sickness; Atropine = stimulating CNS at high dose
  13. Glycopyrrolate — quaternary antimuscarinic; does not cross BBB; longer action than atropine; preferred pre-anaesthetic (no CNS effects)
  14. Catecholamines = EPI, NE, Dopamine (endogenous), Isoproterenol (synthetic); precursor = Tyrosine; rate-limiting enzyme = Tyrosine hydroxylase
  15. Adrenoceptors: α1 = vasoconstriction | α2 = presynaptic autoreceptor (↓ NE release) | β1 = heart (↑ HR, force) | β2 = bronchodi/vasodilation, tocolysis | D1 = renal vasodilation
  16. NE fate: Uptake 1 (reuptake into nerve, 65%) > Uptake 2 (diffusion, 15%) > MAO/COMT metabolism (20%)
  17. Xylazine — α2 agonist; sedation, analgesia, muscle relaxation; cattle 10× more sensitive than horses; reversed by Atipamezole/Yohimbine
  18. β-blocker contraindications: Asthma/COPD, bradycardia/heart block, acute HF, diabetes (masks hypoglycaemia)
  19. Histamine receptors: H1 = allergy/bronchoconstriction/itch | H2 = gastric acid | H3 = presynaptic autoreceptor | H4 = immune cells
  20. Triple response of Lewis — intradermal histamine: red spot (vasodilation) → flare (axon reflex) → wheal (oedema); all H1-mediated
  21. Serotonin (5-HT) from tryptophan; >90% in GIT enterochromaffin cells; 5-HT3 = emesis (blocked by ondansetron); 5-HT1B/D = migraine (triptans)
  22. Arachidonic acid pathway: COX → PGs, TXA2, PGI2 | LOX → Leukotrienes (SRS-A = bronchoconstriction)
  23. PGI2 vs TXA2: PGI2 (endothelium) = vasodilate + antiplatelet | TXA2 (platelets) = vasoconstrict + proplatelet — opposing balance
  24. NSAIDs mechanism: COX inhibition → ↓ PGs → anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antipyretic; COX-1 inhibition → GI ulcers, platelet dysfunction
  25. Paracetamol in cats: NEVER — cats lack glucuronidase → NAPQI accumulation → methaemoglobinaemia, Heinz body anaemia, hepatic necrosis; treat with N-acetylcysteine
  26. RAAS pathway: Renin → Angiotensinogen → Ang I → (ACE in lung) → Ang II → AT1 = vasoconstriction + aldosterone release
  27. ACE inhibitors (Enalapril, Benazepril): ↑ bradykinin → cough side effect; angioedema; renoprotective; used in heart failure/hypertension in dogs/cats
  28. Guedel's stages of anaesthesia: Stage I = Analgesia | II = Excitement (dangerous — pass quickly!) | III = Surgical (Planes 1–3) | IV = Medullary depression/death
  29. MAC = minimum alveolar concentration for 50% no movement; lower MAC = more potent; ↓ by opioids, α2 agonists, hypothermia; ↑ by hyperthermia
  30. Inhalant anaesthetic comparison: Halothane = hepatotoxic, sensitises heart to catecholamines | Isoflurane = most widely used | Sevoflurane = smoothest induction | Desflurane = fastest recovery
  31. Local anaesthetics: Esters (Procaine, Cocaine) = plasma ChE hydrolysis, PABA allergenic | Amides (Lidocaine, Bupivacaine) = liver metabolism, not allergenic; block Na⁺ channels
  32. Ketamine: NMDA antagonist; dissociative; ↑ CVS (sympathomimetic); ↑ ICP and IOP; maintains airway reflexes; emergence delirium — premedicate with BZD; excellent in cats
  33. Propofol: Rapid clean anaesthesia; antiemetic; cats — Heinz body anaemia with repeated doses; PRIS with prolonged infusion
  34. Succinylcholine: Only depolarising NMBD; fastest onset (30–60 sec); ultra-short; hydrolysed by pseudocholinesterase; hyperkalaemia (burns, crush, spinal injury) — dangerous
  35. NMBD reversal: Neostigmine + Atropine (non-depolarising) | Sugammadex encapsulates Rocuronium/Vecuronium — complete reversal without muscarinic SE
  36. Opioid receptors: μ = major analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, constipation | κ = spinal analgesia, dysphoria | δ = modulation | Reversal = Naloxone
  37. Species opioid differences: Dogs = sedation | Cats = excitement/dysphoria at high dose (buprenorphine preferred) | Horses = excitement (always use with sedative)
  38. Buprenorphine: Partial μ agonist; ceiling on respiratory depression; long duration; cats — sublingual effective; difficult to reverse with naloxone
  39. Butorphanol: κ agonist, μ partial antagonist; antitussive; ceiling effect; widely used veterinary pre-anaesthetic; can reverse full μ agonists
  40. Phenobarbital — first-line anticonvulsant in dogs/cats; GABA-A potentiation + Na⁺ channel; enzyme inducer (many drug interactions)
  41. Status epilepticus treatment: IV Diazepam → IV Phenobarbital → Propofol CRI → Pentobarbital (barbiturate coma)
  42. Doxapram — analeptic of choice in veterinary medicine; stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors; used for neonatal resuscitation; short duration (5–12 min)
  43. Strychnine — glycine antagonist (spinal cord); tetanic convulsions; rodenticide; treat with diazepam + quiet environment
  44. Acepromazine (ACP): D2 + α1 block → sedation + hypotension; NOT analgesic; not reversible; lowers seizure threshold; penile prolapse in horses
  45. Antipsychotic mechanism: D2 receptor blockade; EPS = D2 block in basal ganglia; Tardive dyskinesia = long-term irreversible; NMS (fever, rigidity) = treat with dantrolene + bromocriptine
  46. Azaperone — butyrophenone; used in pigs for sedation and aggression control; also used in horses as pre-anaesthetic
  47. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS): Hyperthermia + rigidity + autonomic instability + altered consciousness; treat: stop drug + dantrolene + bromocriptine + supportive
  48. Benzodiazepines vs Barbiturates: BZDs = ↑ Cl⁻ frequency, no ceiling, reversible (flumazenil) | Barbiturates = ↑ Cl⁻ duration + direct activation, lethal overdose, no antidote
  49. Guaifenesin (GGE) — centrally acting muscle relaxant; used in horses in "triple drip" (GGE + ketamine + xylazine) for field anaesthesia
  50. Methadone — μ agonist + NMDA antagonist; useful for neuropathic pain; no euphoria rush; used in horses and dogs perioperatively
  51. Diprenorphine — opioid antagonist used to reverse etorphine in wildlife capture; Etorphine (M99) = very potent μ agonist for wildlife immobilisation

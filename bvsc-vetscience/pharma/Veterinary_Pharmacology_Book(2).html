Chapter 01

Historical Aspects of Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy refers to drug treatment of diseases caused by invading micro-organisms or aberrant tissue growth. Drugs in this class are designed to inhibit or kill the infecting organism with no or minimal effect on the host. The term was coined by Paul Ehrlich, who used drugs of known chemical structure and demonstrated selective attenuation of infecting parasites as a practical proposition.

Definition of Key Terms

Antibiotics are substances produced by microorganisms that suppress the growth of or kill other microorganisms at very low concentrations. This definition excludes substances produced by higher forms (antibodies) or microbe-produced compounds needed in high concentrations (ethanol, lactic acid, H₂O₂). The preferred umbrella term is Antimicrobial Agent (AMA), covering both synthetic and naturally obtained drugs.

Nobel Prize Winners
All three pioneering groups received the Nobel Prize for their discoveries: Domagk (sulfonamides), Fleming, Chain & Florey (penicillin), and Waksman (streptomycin). Some antibacterials developed specifically for animals include tylosin, tiamulin, and tilmicosin.

Selective Toxicity — The Core Principle

The ideal antimicrobial agent must demonstrate selective toxicity — ability to injure or kill microorganisms without causing significant harm to the host. This selectivity exploits differences in the biochemistry of microorganisms versus mammalian cells (e.g., bacterial cell walls, 70S ribosomes vs. mammalian 80S ribosomes).

Chapter 02

Antimicrobial Resistance and Its Impact

Antimicrobial resistance is the unresponsiveness of a microorganism to an AMA; analogous to the phenomenon of tolerance seen in higher organisms. It poses major clinical and economic problems in veterinary practice.

Types of Resistance

TypeDefinitionClinical Significance
Natural (Intrinsic)Microbe has always been resistant — lacks the target site or metabolic processGroup specific; generally no clinical problem (e.g., Gram-negative organisms not affected by penicillin G)
AcquiredDevelopment of resistance by an organism previously sensitive, following exposure to an AMAMajor clinical problem; affects Staphylococci, Coliforms, tubercle bacilli

Mechanisms of Resistance Acquisition

1. Mutation

Stable, heritable genetic change occurring spontaneously and randomly. Not induced by the AMA — the antibiotic merely selects resistant mutants.

Mutation TypePatternExample
Single-stepSingle gene mutation → high-level resistance, emerges rapidlyEnterococci to Streptomycin
Multi-stepMultiple gene modifications, gradual developmentResistance to Erythromycin and Tetracyclines

2. Gene Transfer (Infectious Resistance)

MechanismDescriptionSignificance
ConjugationSexual contact via bridge/sex pilus; chromosomal or plasmid DNA transferMost important — occurs in colon between diverse Gram-negative bacilli; chloramphenicol resistance to typhoid bacilli via conjugation
TransductionTransfer of resistance gene through a bacteriophagePenicillin, erythromycin, chloramphenicol resistance
TransformationResistant bacterium releases resistance DNA into medium; imbibed by sensitive organismProbably not clinically significant
Bacterial Resistance Mechanisms (Summary)
  • Production of drug-inactivating enzymes (e.g., β-lactamases)
  • Altered or absent target site (e.g., modified PBPs in MRSA)
  • Decreased drug penetration / efflux pumps
  • Alternate metabolic pathway (e.g., Sulfonamide resistance)
  • Overproduction of competitive substrate (e.g., PABA overproduction)

Prudent Use of Antibiotics

Resistance can be reduced by: avoiding indiscriminate use of sulfonamides/AMAs, initiating therapy as early as possible, maintaining adequate bacteriostatic/bactericidal concentration, using narrow-spectrum agents when possible, and completing the prescribed treatment course.

Chapter 04

Sulphonamides

Sulfonamides (US) / Sulphonamides (UK) are synthetic non-antibiotic antibacterial drugs derived from sulfanilamide. Gerhard Domagk (1935) developed sulfa from the prodrug azo-dye Prontosil — he received the Nobel Prize for Medicine in 1939.

Chemistry and Physical Properties

White crystalline powder; insoluble in water but soluble in alkaline pH. Sodium salts are water-soluble. Weak organic acids (pKa 4.99–8.56). Parenteral preparations are alkaline — care needed during IV administration to prevent perivascular damage. Sulfacetamide is a neutral preparation suitable for ophthalmic use.

Spectrum of Activity

Broad spectrum, generally bacteriostatic, but bactericidal at high urinary concentrations.

SusceptibilityOrganisms
Good susceptibilityActinomyces, Bacillus, Brucella, Listeria, Streptococci, Chlamydia, Cryptosporidium
Moderate susceptibilityStaphylococcus, Enterococcus, some anaerobes
ResistantMycobacteria, Mycoplasma, Rickettsia, Spirochetes, Pseudomonas

Mechanism of Action

Sulfonamides are structural analogues of para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA). They competitively inhibit dihydropteroate synthetase (DHPS), blocking the conversion of PABA into dihydrofolic acid. This prevents synthesis of tetrahydrofolate, essential for purine and pyrimidine synthesis → bacteriostatic effect.

Sequential Blockade with Trimethoprim
Sulfonamides block step 1 (DHPS — PABA → Dihydrofolic acid). Trimethoprim blocks step 2 (DHFR — Dihydrofolic acid → Tetrahydrofolic acid). Together they produce sequential blockade, achieving a bactericidal synergistic effect. Ratio is 5:1 (sulfa:trimethoprim).

Pharmacokinetics

Rapidly absorbed from GIT (except gut-acting sulfas). Widely distributed including CSF and synovial fluid. Metabolism: Liver — acetylation (less soluble → crystalluria risk), aromatic hydroxylation, glucuronide conjugation. Excretion: Kidney (glomerular filtration + tubular secretion). Alkalinisation of urine increases solubility and elimination.

Adverse Effects

Crystalluria: Acetylated derivatives deposit in renal tubules → renal colic, haematuria. Prevented by adequate water intake and sodium bicarbonate. Poultry: Decreased egg production and thin-shelled eggs. Acute toxic effect (rapid IV overdose): muscle weakness, ataxia, blindness, collapse. Hypersensitivity: Skin rashes (rare).

Clinical Uses

Colibacillosis, pasteurellosis, footrot, coccidiosis. Poultry: Prevention and treatment of coccidiosis, infectious coryza, pullorum disease, fowl typhoid. Dapsone (diaminophenyl sulphone) — dermatitis herpetiformis in dogs, leprosy in humans.

Chapter 05

Trimethoprim

Trimethoprim belongs to the diaminopyrimidine class (along with Ormetoprim and Pyrimethamine). It is a weak organic base (pKa 7.6) that accumulates in acidic environments — urine, milk, and ruminal fluid.

Key Properties

Poorly soluble in water. Fixed-dose combination with sulfamethoxazole = Co-trimoxazole. Readily absorbed after oral administration in monogastrics; in ruminants it is trapped and undergoes microbial degradation. Protein binding: 30–60%. Widely distributed in tissues including prostate. Partly metabolised in liver; excreted by glomerular filtration and tubular secretion.

Mechanism of Action

Inhibits Dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) — the enzyme that converts dihydrofolic acid to tetrahydrofolic acid. Acts at step 2 of the sequential blockade, complementing sulfonamides at step 1. Alone it is bacteriostatic; combined with sulpha it becomes bactericidal.

Selective Toxicity
Trimethoprim is 50,000 times more active against bacterial DHFR than against mammalian DHFR. This selectivity makes it safe for use while being lethal to bacteria.

Resistance

Mutational or plasmid-mediated resistance develops readily when used alone. Resistance develops more slowly with the combination (co-trimoxazole) — especially with Enterobacteriaceae.

CattleSalmonellosis, diarrhoea, pneumonia30 mg/kg

Side Effects

Swelling at injection site. Folate deficiency → megaloblastic anaemia (treat with folinic acid). Transient arthritis in Dobermanns. Keratoconjunctivitis (reduced lacrimal secretion). Idiosyncratic thrombocytopenia. Salivation in cats (uncoated oral tablets). Withdrawal period: generally 3 days.

Chapter 06

Nitrofurans and Nitrofurantoin

Nitrofurans are synthetic antibacterials with a nitrofuran ring structure. Key members include Nitrofurantoin, Furazolidone, and Nitrofurazone. They are broad-spectrum agents active against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as some protozoa.

Mechanism of Action

Nitrofurans are prodrugs. Within bacterial cells they are reduced by microbial flavoproteins (nitroreductases) to reactive intermediates that attack ribosomal proteins and DNA, causing multiple simultaneous injuries to the organism. This multitarget action explains the extremely low rate of resistance development.

Nitrofurantoin — Key Features

Rapidly and completely absorbed from GIT; achieves therapeutic concentrations only in urine — hence its utility is limited to urinary tract infections (UTI). Active against E. coli, Staphylococci, Klebsiella, Enterococci. Inactive against Pseudomonas. Excreted unchanged in urine. Bactericidal at urinary concentrations.

Furazolidone

Poorly absorbed — suitable for enteric infections. Active against Salmonella, E. coli, Giardia, Campylobacter, and some protozoa (Histomonas meleagridis — blackhead in turkeys). Used in poultry for treatment of Histomoniasis and coccidiosis. Also active against Vibrio cholerae.

Adverse Effects and Restrictions

Pulmonary toxicity (rare). Haemolytic anaemia in G6PD-deficient individuals. GI disturbances. Note: Many nitrofurans are banned in food-producing animals in several countries due to concerns about carcinogenicity and residues in edible tissues. Nitrofurantoin remains approved for small animal UTI.

Drug Interactions

Antagonistic with fluoroquinolones — do not combine. Synergistic with trimethoprim-sulfa for some enteric pathogens.

Chapter 07

Nitroimidazoles

Nitroimidazoles include Metronidazole, Tinidazole, Ronidazole, and Dimetridazole. They are the drugs of choice for anaerobic bacterial infections and protozoan infections (Giardia, Trichomonas, Histomonas).

Mechanism of Action

Selectively toxic to anaerobic organisms. The nitro group is reduced by low-redox-potential electron transport proteins present in anaerobes (e.g., ferredoxin). The resulting reactive nitro radical intermediates cause strand breakage and disruption of DNA, leading to bactericidal action. Aerobic organisms lack the necessary reductases — explaining selective toxicity.

Metronidazole — Pharmacokinetics

Excellent oral bioavailability. Widely distributed including CNS (crosses blood-brain barrier). Metabolised in liver (CYP450). Excreted in urine (metabolites impart brown discolouration). Short half-life — requires multiple daily dosing.

GI disturbances (most common). Neurotoxicity with prolonged high-dose use — ataxia, seizures (especially cats). Metallic taste. Potential teratogen — avoid in pregnancy. Chapter 08

Quinolones and Fluoroquinolones

Quinolones are the first entirely man-made synthetic antibacterial agents. Developed from structural modification of chloroquine (1939). Nalidixic acid was the first (1967). In the 1980s, fluorination at position 6 and addition of piperazine moiety created the fluoroquinolones.

Mechanism of Action

Inhibit DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) — the enzyme that controls supercoiling of bacterial DNA by energy-dependent strand breakage and resealing. DNA gyrase contains two subunits (GyrA and GyrB) controlling nicking and resealing. Fluoroquinolones bind to the DNA gyrase–DNA complex → exonucleases digest the broken DNA → bactericidal, concentration-dependent killing.

Pharmacokinetics

Better absorbed after oral administration in monogastrics and preruminants; poor in ruminants (parenteral preferred). Lipophilic — well distributed in tissues including bone, cartilage, prostate, CSF. High concentration in liver, bile, urinary tract. Partially metabolised in liver; excreted in urine and bile. Urinary drug concentration exceeds serum — low dose sufficient for UTI.

Toxicity and Adverse Effects

Cartilage toxicity: Not recommended for pups <8 months (small breeds) or <18 months (large breeds) — causes cartilaginous erosion leading to permanent lameness. Not recommended in horses due to arthralgic effects. CNS convulsions (rare). Photosensitivity. Embryonic loss in pregnant animals.

Veterinary Clinical Applications
Enrofloxacin is the flagship fluoroquinolone for veterinary use:
  • Cattle/Sheep/Goat: Pneumonia (Mycoplasma bovis, Pasteurella), E. coli enteritis, septicaemia
  • Swine: Pneumonia, E. coli diarrhoea, salmonellosis, MMA syndrome
  • Dogs/Cats: UTI, respiratory tract infection, osteomyelitis, skin & wound infection
  • Poultry: 50 ppm in water — Mycoplasma, E. coli, Fowl cholera, Salmonellosis, Bordetella
Dose of Enrofloxacin: 2.5–5 mg/kg q 24 hrs.
Chapter 09

Beta-Lactam Antibiotics — Penicillin

Beta-lactam antibiotics share a common β-lactam ring in their structure and exert bactericidal effects by preventing bacterial cell wall synthesis. They include Penicillins, Cephalosporins, Monobactams, and Carbapenems.

Historical Milestones

Fleming (1928) observed Penicillium mould forming a clear zone free of Staphylococci — named the substance penicillin. Chain and Florey (1940) isolated it as impure powder; clinical use began 1941. Cephalosporium acremonium isolated from raw sewage in 1945 → gave rise to cephalosporins (clinical use 1964).

Mechanism of Action

Beta-lactams bind to Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs) on the bacterial cell membrane — enzymes involved in the final stages of peptidoglycan cross-linking. The β-lactam ring is a structural analogue of the D-alanine–D-alanine terminus of the peptide bridge. Binding inhibits transpeptidation → defective cell wall → osmotic instability → cell lysis.

Why Beta-Lactams are Bactericidal
Cell death results from lysis, mediated by bacterial autolysins (murein hydrolases). Beta-lactams show a post-antibiotic effect against Gram-positive cocci but NOT against Gram-negative bacilli.

Pharmacokinetics

Rapidly absorbed via IM or SC (max blood concentration 15–30 min). Oral Penicillin G requires 5× IM dose due to gastric acid inactivation. Procaine penicillin G prolongs absorption (~24 hrs); Benzathine penicillin G up to 7+ days. Does not penetrate CNS significantly (except inflamed meninges). Diffuses across placenta. Excreted rapidly in urine via tubular secretion (most within 1 hr of IM injection). Probenecid inhibits tubular secretion, prolonging action.

Penicillins — Groups and Clinical Use

This chapter expands on the clinical application, specific preparations, and adverse effects of the penicillin group.

Penicillin G (Benzylpenicillin)

Prototype natural penicillin. Available as: Sodium/Potassium salt (aqueous, short-acting); Procaine penicillin G (prolonged 18–24 hrs); Benzathine penicillin G (very prolonged, up to 7+ days). 1 IU = 0.6 μg sodium penicillin G. Administered IM — DO NOT give IV (procaine form).

Chapter 11

Cephalosporins

Cephalosporins are semisynthetic β-lactam antibiotics derived from Cephalosporin C (originally isolated from Cephalosporium acremonium from sewage in 1945; clinical use 1964). They share the β-lactam mechanism (PBP inhibition) with penicillins. More stable to many β-lactamases than penicillins.

Generation-wise Classification

GenerationExamplesSpectrum
1st generationCephalexin, Cefazolin, CephalothinGram +ve coverage; limited Gram −ve (E. coli, Proteus, Klebsiella)
2nd generationCefuroxime, Cefoxitin, CefaclorExpanded Gram −ve; Gram +ve slightly reduced; Cefoxitin → anaerobes
3rd generationCeftiofur, Cefotaxime, Ceftriaxone, CefpodoximeExcellent Gram −ve (including Pseudomonas — some); widely used in vet

Ceftiofur — Important Veterinary Cephalosporin
3rd generation cephalosporin approved for cattle, swine, horses, and poultry. Active against respiratory pathogens (Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida, Histophilus somni) and E. coli. Minimal withdrawal period formulations available. Metabolite desfuroylceftiofur retains antibacterial activity.

Pharmacokinetics

Some cephalosporins (cephalexin, cefpodoxime) are well absorbed orally. Most are hydrophilic — limited CNS penetration (3rd gen better penetration in meningitis). Excreted primarily by renal tubular secretion. Ceftriaxone is excreted via bile (enterohepatic circulation).

Adverse Effects

Generally safe. Hypersensitivity — cross-reaction with penicillin in ~1–10% of sensitive individuals. Nephrotoxicity with 1st generation (especially combined with aminoglycosides). GI disturbances (oral formulations). Pain at injection sites.

Clinical Use in Veterinary Practice

Respiratory tract infections (BRD complex), urinary tract infections, skin and wound infections, post-surgical prophylaxis, meningitis (3rd gen), bovine mastitis.

Chapter 12

Beta-Lactamase Inhibitors, Carbapenems & Monobactams

Beta-lactamases are enzymes produced by penicillin-resistant bacteria that hydrolyse the β-lactam ring, inactivating the antibiotic. Beta-lactamase inhibitors irreversibly bind to β-lactamase, protecting the active β-lactam antibiotic.

Clavulanic Acid

Produced by Streptomyces clavuligerus. A β-lactam compound with negligible antibacterial activity itself, but undergoes suicidal (irreversible) inhibition of β-lactamases. Good affinity for plasmid-mediated β-lactamases and all chromosomally mediated penicillinases. Combined with Amoxycillin (4:1) and Ticarcillin (15:1) — combinations are bactericidal. Well absorbed orally; half-life ~75 min; excreted largely unchanged in urine.

CombinationClinical Application
Amoxycillin + Clavulanic acidCattle: respiratory, anaerobic soft tissue, mastitis; Dogs & cats: UTI, pyoderma, mixed infections, peritonitis
Ticarcillin + Clavulanic acidGreater activity against Pseudomonas; IV only, 8-hourly dosing
Ampicillin + SulbactamBovine respiratory disease; extra-label salmonellosis; avoid in hindgut digesters
Piperacillin + Tazobactam (8:1)Polymicrobial infections, neutropenic fever (with aminoglycosides)

Sulbactam and Tazobactam

Sulbactam — synthetic derivative of 6-aminopenicillanic acid; poorly absorbed orally (prodrug: Sultamicillin). Lower affinity than clavulanic acid. Combined with ampicillin (similar PK) or cefoperazone. Tazobactam — broadest activity of the three inhibitors; resists hydrolysis by some β-lactamases.

Carbapenems

Derivatives of Streptomyces spp. Highly active against wide variety of Gram +ve and Gram −ve bacteria; resistant to many β-lactamases. Imipenem is combined with cilastatin (dihydropeptidase inhibitor) to prevent nephrotoxic metabolite formation. Meropenem and Biapenem resist hydrolysis. Synergistic with aminoglycosides (to prevent rapid Pseudomonas resistance). Veterinary use is limited — reserved for hospital-acquired infections.

Monobactams

Aztreonam — monocyclic β-lactam ring only (no thiazolidine ring). Synthetic analogue; stable to most β-lactamases. Active against almost all Gram −ve bacteria; Gram +ve and anaerobes are resistant. Synergistic with aminoglycosides. Low immunogenic potential — usable in penicillin-sensitive individuals. Not absorbed orally; veterinary use limited.

Chapter 14

Aminoglycosides

Aminoglycosides are a group of natural and semisynthetic antibiotics discovered through systematic screening of soil microbes. Waksman (1944) discovered streptomycin from Streptomyces griseus. Structurally composed of amino sugars joined by glycosidic linkages. Active against bacteria, mycoplasma, and mycobacteria.

Common Properties

Polar organic bases — highly active in alkaline medium. Poor oral bioavailability (hydrophilic — cannot cross barriers). Water-soluble sulphate salts. Predominantly Gram-negative activity; not active against anaerobes (require O₂-dependent transport). Excreted unchanged in urine. Narrow margin of safety — ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity are common.

Mechanism of Action

Enter bacteria by active transport (O₂-dependent) through porins. Bind irreversibly to 30S ribosomal subunit → misreading of mRNA codon → faulty amino acids incorporated → lethal proteins inserted into cell membrane → altered permeability → cell death. Exhibit concentration-dependent killing and post-antibiotic effect (PAE).

Biphasic Mode of Killing
Phase 1 (Initial rapid killing): Drug rapidly penetrates and kills bacteria. Phase 2 (Late slow killing): Survivors become resistant due to decreased permeability. Administering antibiotic during this refractory period enhances toxicity rather than efficacy — hence once-daily dosing is preferred (maximises PAE, reduces toxicity).

Neomycin, Kanamycin and Gentamicin

Neomycin

Mixture of Neomycin B and C. Source: Streptomyces fradiae. White/yellow crystalline powder, water-soluble. Most stable aminoglycoside. Restricted to local (topical) use because of severe nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity when administered systemically.

Spectrum: Good against Staphylococcus aureus; most Gram-negative organisms including Pseudomonas susceptible. Resistance: Plasmid-mediated — common in enteric commensals. Clinical use: Coliform enteritis; skin, eye and ear infections; dry cow treatment (with Penicillin G). Topical Neomycin sulfate + Bacitracin for wound dressing. Caution: Routine addition to milk powder of neonatal calves → increased diarrhoea (disrupts intestinal flora). Intrauterine boluses in postparturient cows increased services per conception.

Kanamycin

Source: Streptomyces kanamyceticus. Similar to neomycin but inactive against Pseudomonas. Larger therapeutic index than neomycin. Used with penicillin for Pasteurella pneumonia; treatment of Staphylococcus aureus infections. Replaced by Gentamicin in most applications due to wider spectrum.

Tetracyclines — Introduction and Classification

Tetracyclines are broad-spectrum antibiotics characterised by a tetracyclic naphthacene ring system. They are produced by Streptomyces species. First member — Chlortetracycline (Aureomycin) — discovered in 1947.

Classification of Tetracyclines

GenerationExamplesKey Features
Short-actingTetracycline, Oxytetracycline, Chlortetracyclineq 8–12 hr dosing; standard spectrum

Long-actingDoxycycline, MinocyclineLonger half-life; lipophilic; q 12–24 hr; better CNS penetration

Mechanism of Action

Reversible binding to the 30S ribosomal subunit → prevents binding of aminoacyl-tRNA to the acceptor (A) site of the ribosome → inhibits protein synthesis elongation. Active transport required to enter bacteria (involves OmpF porins and proton motive force in Gram-negatives). Bacteriostatic at therapeutic concentrations.

Antimicrobial Spectrum

Truly broad-spectrum — active against: Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, Rickettsia, Spirochaetes, some protozoa (Theileria, Anaplasma). Inactive against most anaerobes and Pseudomonas.

Mechanisms of Resistance

Most common: Plasmid-mediated efflux pump (TetA-TetL proteins) that actively pumps tetracycline out of the cell. Also: ribosomal protection proteins (TetM, TetO), enzymatic inactivation (rare). Cross-resistance is common among early tetracyclines but doxycycline and minocycline may retain activity against some resistant strains.

Chapter 17

Tetracyclines — Pharmacokinetics and Clinical Use

Absorption

Absorbed primarily in upper small intestine. GI absorption impaired by: sodium bicarbonate, aluminium hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide, iron, calcium salts, milk and milk products (forms insoluble chelates). Ruminants: Tetracyclines at therapeutic levels should NOT be administered PO — poorly absorbed and depress ruminal microfloral activity. Long-acting oxytetracycline (IM) produces effects for ~72 hrs.

Distribution

Rapid, extensive distribution after parenteral administration. High concentrations: kidneys, liver, bile, lungs, spleen, bone. Plasma protein binding: Oxytetracycline 30%, Tetracycline 60%, Doxycycline 90%.

Via kidneys (glomerular filtration, 50–80%) and GI tract (biliary elimination). Doxycycline — major excretion route is biliary (enterohepatic circulation). Also eliminated in milk (peaks 6 hr post-parenteral dose; traces persist 48 hrs).

Chapter 18

Chloramphenicol

Chloramphenicol is a nitrobenzene derivative originally isolated from Streptomyces venezuelae in 1947 — now produced synthetically. It was the first truly broad-spectrum antibiotic.

Mechanism of Action

Binds irreversibly to the 50S ribosomal subunit and blocks peptidyl transferase, preventing the transfer of the elongating peptide chain to the newly attached aminoacyl tRNA at the A site → inhibits protein synthesis elongation → bacteriostatic.

Spectrum

Truly broad-spectrum: Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, Rickettsia, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma, anaerobes. Active against many β-lactam-resistant organisms. Inactive against Pseudomonas.

Pharmacokinetics

Excellent oral bioavailability (both base and palmitate pro-drug). Widely distributed including CSF (30–50% of plasma) — important for meningitis treatment. Metabolised in liver by glucuronidation. Excreted in urine (mainly as glucuronide). Neonates lack glucuronosyltransferase → accumulation → Grey Baby Syndrome (cardiovascular collapse).

Adverse Effects — Critical Safety Concerns

Bone Marrow Toxicity (Two Types)
1. Dose-related reversible: Suppression of erythropoiesis (↓ reticulocytes, anaemia) — common, reversible on cessation. 2. Idiosyncratic irreversible aplastic anaemia: Rare (1:25,000–40,000) but fatal — panmyelopathy. This is the reason Chloramphenicol is BANNED in food-producing animals in most countries.

Veterinary Restrictions and Use

Banned in food-producing animals (cattle, poultry, pigs, sheep) in most countries including India (FSSAI regulations). Permitted in horses and companion animals (dogs, cats) in many countries. Used for: Ocular infections (topical), CNS infections (penetrates CSF), ehrlichiosis in dogs, salmonellosis. Florfenicol — a fluorinated analogue without the aplastic anaemia risk — is approved for use in food animals (cattle, fish).

Macrolides

Macrolides are characterised by a macrocyclic lactone ring attached to two or more sugar moieties. Examples: Erythromycin, Tylosin, Spiramycin, Tilmicosin, Roxithromycin, Azithromycin.

Mechanism of Action

Reversible binding to the 50S ribosomal subunit → prevents translocation of peptidyl tRNA from A site to P site → inhibits protein synthesis. Bacteriostatic at therapeutic concentrations; bactericidal at higher concentrations. Spectrum similar to penicillin but wider — includes Mycoplasma, Mycobacteria, Chlamydia, and Rickettsia.

Erythromycin — Prototype

Source: Streptomyces erythreus. Macrocyclic lactone nucleus. Base is poorly soluble in water and unstable in gastric acid → requires enteric coating or ester/salt formulations. Absorption: Oral (enteric coated) or parenteral (gluconate/lactobionate). Distribution: High intracellular concentrations; penetrates pleura, peritoneum, placenta; but NOT blood-brain barrier. Metabolism: Liver (CYP450 inhibitor). Excretion: Urine and bile (enterohepatic circulation).

Chapter 20

Pleuromutilins and Tiamulin

Pleuromutilins are diterpene antibiotics discovered in the early 1950s from Pleurotus mutilis. Tiamulin is the primary semisynthetic derivative. Exclusively used in animals — largely in swine.

Mechanism of Action

Bacteriostatic. Binds to the 50S ribosomal subunit at the peptidyl transferase centre, causing break in the peptide chain and inhibiting peptidyl transferase enzyme. Structurally unique — no cross-resistance with other 50S inhibitors except limited one-way cross-resistance with tylosin.

Antimicrobial Activity

Excellent activity against: Gram-positive anaerobes, Mycoplasma (outstanding), some Gram-negative aerobes. Resistant: Enterobacteriaceae. Tiamulin has outstanding activity against Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae (swine enzootic pneumonia).

Resistance

Develops by chromosomal mutation; stepwise pattern; slower than tylosin. One-way cross-resistance with tylosin (Mycoplasma resistant to tylosin are resistant to tiamulin and vice versa).

Pharmacokinetics

Completely absorbed after oral administration in ruminants and monogastrics. Lipophilic, weak organic base. Better tissue penetration; concentrates in milk. Metabolised extensively; excreted in bile and urine.

Critical Drug Interaction
Tiamulin + Monensin (ionophore) → severe ionophore toxicity in chickens and pigs — DO NOT use concurrently. Death may result.

Clinical Applications

Swine: Enzootic pneumonia (Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae), Swine dysentery (Brachyspira hyodysenteriae), Proliferative enteropathy (Lawsonia intracellularis). Poultry: Mycoplasma infections (feed or drinking water). Available as water-soluble powder, premix, and injectable. Valnemulin: A newer pleuromutilin with superior activity.

Chapter 21

Lincosamides

Lincosamides include Lincomycin and Clindamycin. They bind to the 50S ribosomal subunit and inhibit the peptidyl transferase enzyme — similar mechanism to chloramphenicol and macrolides.

Spectrum

Active primarily against: Gram-positive aerobes (Staphylococci, Streptococci), Gram-positive and Gram-negative anaerobes (excellent activity — drug of choice for anaerobic infections in small animals), Mycoplasma, Toxoplasma. Inactive: Most Gram-negative aerobes, Enterobacteriaceae.

MLSB Cross-Resistance

Macrolide-Lincosamide-Streptogramin B (MLSB) resistance — organisms resistant to any one of these classes via ribosomal methylation are resistant to all three. This limits clinical utility in resistant Staphylococci.

Peptide Antibiotics

Polymyxins

Polymyxins (Polymyxin B and Polymyxin E/Colistin) are polypeptide antibiotics obtained from Bacillus polymyxa. Colistimethate is the form for parenteral administration.

Mechanism of action: Cationic surface-active agents (detergent-like). Interact strongly with phospholipids in Gram-negative bacterial cell membranes, competitively displacing calcium and magnesium → radically disrupt membrane permeability → bactericidal. Active against: Gram-negative bacteria (Enterobacter, Klebsiella, Salmonella, Pasteurella, Bordetella, E. coli); relatively inactive against Gram-positive bacteria.

Bacitracin

Branched cyclic decapeptide. Inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis (blocks dephosphorylation of lipid carrier that transports peptidoglycan precursors). Active against Gram-positive bacteria. Too toxic for systemic use — restricted to topical application. Used in wound dressings (combined with Neomycin), ophthalmic ointments, and as a growth promoter (historical use in poultry/swine).

Vancomycin

Glycopeptide antibiotic from Streptomyces orientalis. Inhibits cell wall synthesis by binding to D-alanine–D-alanine terminal on peptidoglycan precursors → prevents transglycosylation and transpeptidation. Active against Gram-positive bacteria, including MRSA. Poorly absorbed orally. Drug of last resort for MRSA and Clostridium difficile. Adverse effects: nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, "Red Man Syndrome" with rapid IV infusion.

Cycloserine, Ionophores, Rifampin & Miscellaneous

Cycloserine

Source: Streptomyces orchidaceus. Analogue of D-alanine. Mechanism: Competitive inhibition of (1) L-alanine racemase (converts L-alanine to D-alanine) and (2) D-alanyl-D-alanine synthetase (incorporates D-alanine into pentapeptide of peptidoglycan). Oral tuberculostat. Slow resistance development. No cross-resistance. Well distributed including CSF. Adverse effects: Accumulates in serum → renal insufficiency, CNS disturbance, epileptic seizures, peripheral neuropathy.

Ionophore Antibiotics

Carboxylic polyether antibiotics from Streptomyces spp. Include: Lasalocid, Maduramicin, Monensin, Narasin, Salinomycin, Semduramicin. Behave as alkali metal ionophores — form complex with sodium ions → passive transport of K⁺ and replacement by H⁺ → decrease intracellular pH → cidal to Gram-positive organisms. Shift rumen microflora towards Gram-negative population → increase propionic acid production → better feed efficiency.

Rifampin (Rifampicin)

Synthetically modified member of Rifamycin. Mechanism: Inhibits bacterial RNA polymerase (β subunit) → prevents transcription → bactericidal. Amphotericin B — The Gold Standard

Polyene macrolide from Streptomyces nodosus. Binds to ergosterol in fungal cell membrane → forms pores → loss of intracellular potassium, magnesium, sugars → cell death. Active against: Aspergillus, Candida, Cryptococcus, Blastomyces, Histoplasma, Coccidioides. Administered IV (not absorbed orally). Dose-limiting nephrotoxicity (tubular damage — reversible). Azoles — Mechanism

Inhibit lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51) — a cytochrome P450 enzyme in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway. Depletion of ergosterol → disrupts membrane integrity and function. Ketoconazole: Broad antifungal; also inhibits mammalian steroidogenesis (useful in Cushing's disease in dogs). Griseofulvin

From Penicillium griseofulvum. Oral antidermatophyte. Concentrates in newly synthesised keratin — active against ringworm (Trichophyton, Microsporum). Mechanism: inhibits mitosis by interfering with microtubule polymerisation and disrupting the spindle. Teratogenic — avoid in pregnancy. Long treatment course (weeks to months).

Nystatin

Polyene antifungal; too toxic for systemic use. Used topically for Candida infections — skin, mucous membranes, GIT (NOT absorbed orally). Combined with antibiotics to prevent Candida superinfection.

Antiviral Chemotherapy

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites using host genetic machinery for replication — making specific antiviral targeting difficult. About 69% of illness is caused by viruses; only ~16 antiviral drugs are currently available for animals and humans.

Reasons for Limited Antiviral Therapy

Drugs must penetrate host cells. Lack of broad-spectrum antivirals. Narrow spectrum (target specific viral enzymes). Resistance develops easily (point mutation). Drugs only inhibit actively replicating virus — cannot eliminate latent infections. Mostly virustatic — success depends on host immune response.

Acyclovir — Key Antiviral

Synthetic acyclic purine nucleoside analogue of 2'-deoxyguanosine. Selective for viral cells — phosphorylated only in virus-infected cells (by viral thymidine kinase) → non-toxic to uninfected cells (no viral TK = no phosphorylation = no activation). Active against Herpes simplex 1 & 2, Varicella Zoster. Veterinary use: Equine herpes in foals; bovine herpes mammillitis; feline rhinotracheitis (topical); equine coital exanthema.

Cytokines released by virus-infected cells. Possess antiviral, immunomodulatory, and antiproliferative effects. Highly species specific. Inhibit all steps in viral multiplication (binding, penetration, uncoating, mRNA synthesis, translation, assembly, release). Bovine IFN-α and γ now produced by genetic engineering.

Benzimidazoles — Mechanism

Bind to β-tubulin of parasites, preventing microtubule polymerisation → disruption of cell division and glucose uptake → larvicidal and ovicidal as well as adulticidal. Ivermectin — Mechanism

Binds to glutamate-gated chloride channels (invertebrate-specific) → increases chloride permeability → hyperpolarisation → flaccid paralysis of pharyngeal muscles and somatic muscles of nematodes and ectoparasites. Also enhances GABA release. Safe in mammals (GABA channels behind blood-brain barrier). Exception: Collies and related breeds with MDR1/ABCB1 gene mutation — ivermectin penetrates CNS → toxicity.

Praziquantel — Mechanism

Increases permeability of tegument of cestodes and trematodes to calcium → spastic paralysis → dislodgement and death. Also causes tegumental damage → exposes parasite antigens to host immune system. Drug of choice for tapeworms and schistosomes.

Imidocarb Dipropionate

Chemically: 3,3-bis(2-imidazolin-2-yl) carbanilide dipropionate. Mechanism: Vacuolisation of the cytoplasm and alteration in size of the nucleus. Well absorbed and distributed throughout the body. Quinapyramine (Antrycide)

Two compounds: Quinapyramine chloride (slowly absorbed, strong prophylactic effect) and sulphate (rapidly absorbed, mainly trypanocidal, low prophylactic value). Suramin

Complex aromatic organic compound. Marked curative activity against T. evansi in horses, camels, cattle, dogs. Potentially toxic — narrow therapeutic index. Horses and donkeys very susceptible; camels relatively resistant. Symptoms: liver, kidney, spleen, and adrenal gland damage.

Diminazene Aceturate (Berenil)

Aromatic diamidine. Used for babesiosis and trypanosomiasis. Administered IM. Can cause pain and local reaction at injection site. Neurological signs in overdose — dogs are sensitive. Cerebellar signs in cattle at high doses.

Anticoccidial Chemotherapy

Coccidiosis was a dreadful disease in the broiler industry in the 1940s — causing bloody diarrhoea and heavy mortality. It affects cattle, sheep, goat, pigs, dogs and cats. External Antiparasitics

Insecticides/ectoparasiticides are used on animals to control mites, fleas, ticks, and flies. Young animals are most susceptible; cats are very sensitive to cholinergic stimulants and pyrethroids.

Organophosphates (OPC)

Include Coumaphos, Fenthion, Diazinon, Dichlorvos, Tetrachlorvinylfos. Mechanism: Irreversibly inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE) → accumulation of ACh at cholinergic synapses → SLUD signs (Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation) + fasciculation, ataxia, convulsion. Lipophilic — well absorbed through skin. Treatment: Decontamination + Atropine sulphate (anticholinergic) + Pralidoxime (2-PAM) (reactivates AChE if given early).

Carbamates

Examples: Carbaryl, Propoxur. Used in small animals (powder, shampoo, collar). Mechanism: Inhibit AChE via reversible carbamylation (unlike irreversible OPC binding). Adverse effects similar to OPC. Atropine sulphate is antidote. 2-PAM should NOT be used — carbamate binding is reversible and 2-PAM itself inhibits cholinesterase.

Pyrethroids

Allethrin, Cypermethrin, Fenvalerate, Permethrin. Alkaloids of pyrethrum. Mechanism: Prolong the opening of sodium channels in arthropod neurons → membrane depolarisation → arthropod paralysis. Generally safe for mammals. Do NOT use in cats — feline inability to glucuronidate → neurotoxicity.

Macrocyclic Lactones as Ectoparasiticides

Ivermectin, Doramectin, Selamectin — effective against mites, lice, ticks and fly larvae as well as nematodes. Selamectin (topical) — broad-spectrum for dogs and cats (fleas, heartworm prevention, mites, ticks).

Chapter 32

Antiseptics and Disinfectants

Understanding the difference between antiseptics and disinfectants is fundamental to infection control in veterinary practice.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
SterilizationDestruction or elimination of ALL forms of life including spores — physical or chemical
DisinfectionKilling of pathogenic organisms; lacks sporicidal activity
DisinfectantChemical agent applied to inanimate objects to destroy microorganisms
AntisepticSubstance applied to living tissue to inhibit or destroy microorganisms
GermicideAgent that destroys pathogenic microorganisms
BacteriostaticInhibits growth without killing (concentration dependent)

Levels of Disinfection

High-level: Active against bacterial endospores. Intermediate-level: Inactivate tubercle bacilli but not spores. Low-level: Rapidly kills vegetative bacteria and fungi but NOT tubercle bacilli, endospores, or small non-lipid viruses.

Ideal Properties of an Antiseptic

Broad spectrum (bacteria, fungi, virus). Rapidly effective and germicidal. Not allow emergence of resistant pathogens. Not inactivated by protein/organic matter. Minimally toxic. Non-staining, non-irritant, non-corrosive. Odourless and deodorising. Detergent property. Residual action after rinsing. Simple, stable, economical.

Factors Affecting Disinfectant Action

Type and concentration of disinfectant. Type of organism, number, presence of spores/capsules. Temperature and pH of solution. Presence of organic matter (most important — reduces efficacy). Length of exposure. Temperature and pressure (gaseous disinfectants).

Master Quick Revision — 55 Must-Know Points

  1. Paul Ehrlich — Father of Chemotherapy; coined the term; discovered arsenicals (arsphenamine 1906, neoarsphenamine 1909) for syphilis; developed the concept of selective toxicity ('Magic Bullets')
  2. Domagk (1935) — Demonstrated therapeutic effect of Prontosil (sulfonamide dye) → initiated modern chemotherapy era; Nobel Prize 1939
  3. Fleming (1929) — Discovered penicillin from Penicillium mould; Chain & Florey (1940) isolated it; clinical use from 1941
  4. Waksman (1944) — Discovered streptomycin from Streptomyces griseus; also discovered that Actinomycetes are a treasure-house of antibiotics
  5. AMA = Antimicrobial Agent — preferred term covering both synthetic (antibacterial) and naturally produced (antibiotic) drugs that attenuate microorganisms
  6. Selective toxicity — Ability to injure/kill microorganisms without causing significant harm to the host; exploits differences in cell wall composition, ribosomal subunits (70S vs 80S), ergosterol vs cholesterol membranes
  7. Natural resistance — Microbe always was resistant (lacks target/metabolic process); group specific; not clinically problematic | Acquired resistance — Develops after AMA exposure; major clinical problem
  8. Conjugation — Most important mechanism of resistance transfer; sexual bridge/sex pilus between Gram-negative bacilli in colon; transfers R-factor (plasmid DNA)
  9. Sulfonamide mechanism — Competitive inhibition of DHPS (step 1) → blocks PABA → dihydrofolic acid conversion → prevents folate synthesis → bacteriostatic
  10. Trimethoprim mechanism — Inhibits DHFR (step 2) → blocks dihydrofolic acid → tetrahydrofolic acid; 50,000× more active against bacterial DHFR; bacteriostatic alone, bactericidal with sulpha
  11. Sequential blockade = Sulfonamide (step 1) + Trimethoprim (step 2) → bactericidal synergy; ratio 5:1 (sulfa:TMP)
  12. Crystalluria prevention — Adequate water intake + sodium bicarbonate (alkalinise urine); triple sulfa reduces individual concentration
  13. Fluoroquinolone mechanism — Inhibit DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) → strand breakage → bactericidal, concentration-dependent killing
  14. Fluoroquinolone restrictions — NOT in pups <8 months (small) or <18 months (large) → cartilaginous erosion; NOT recommended in horses (arthralgic effect); Enrofloxacin specific for veterinary use
  15. Beta-lactam mechanism — Bind to PBPs (penicillin-binding proteins) → inhibit transpeptidation of peptidoglycan → defective cell wall → lysis → bactericidal
  16. Beta-lactam resistance — (1) β-lactamase production (major); (2) Modified outer membrane permeability; (3) Altered PBPs (MRSA)
  17. Clavulanic acid — β-lactamase inhibitor from Streptomyces clavuligerus; negligible antibacterial activity; suicidal inhibitor of β-lactamase; combined with Amoxycillin (4:1)
  18. Aminoglycoside mechanism — Irreversible 30S binding → mRNA misreading → faulty proteins in cell membrane → altered permeability → death; concentration-dependent + PAE
  19. Biphasic aminoglycoside killing — Rapid initial killing → surviving cells develop permeability decrease (refractory phase) → slow killing; once-daily dosing preferred
  20. Aminoglycoside toxicity — Ototoxicity (cochlear + vestibular); Nephrotoxicity (acute tubular necrosis); Neuromuscular blockade (treated with IV calcium or neostigmine)
  21. Tetracycline mechanism — Reversible 30S binding → prevents aminoacyl-tRNA binding to A site → bacteriostatic
  22. Tetracycline calcium chelation — Deposited in growing bones and unerupted teeth → yellow discolouration → avoid in young animals and pregnancy
  23. Tetracycline absorption inhibitors — Dairy products, antacids (Al, Mg, Ca), iron, sodium bicarbonate → chelation; NOT to be given PO to ruminants
  24. Doxycycline — Most lipophilic tetracycline; penetrates blood-brain barrier (~30% plasma); 90% protein-bound; major excretion via bile (not kidney); less calcium chelation
  25. Chloramphenicol mechanism — Irreversible 50S binding → blocks peptidyl transferase → bacteriostatic; BANNED in food animals due to risk of fatal idiosyncratic aplastic anaemia
  26. Florfenicol — Fluorinated analogue of chloramphenicol; approved for food animals; no aplastic anaemia risk
  27. Macrolide mechanism — Reversible 50S binding → prevents translocation of peptidyl tRNA from A to P site → bacteriostatic
  28. MLSB resistance — Macrolide-Lincosamide-Streptogramin B cross-resistance via ribosomal methylation (most common macrolide resistance mechanism)
  29. Tilmicosin warning — Long-acting macrolide for bovine respiratory disease; fatal to humans and horses at standard cattle doses
  30. Tiamulin + Monensin — DANGEROUS combination → severe ionophore toxicity and death in chickens and pigs; contraindicated
  31. Lincosamides — Drug of choice for anaerobic infections in small animals; DO NOT USE in hindgut fermenters (horses, rabbits, guinea pigs) — fatal colitis
  32. Polymyxin mechanism — Cationic detergent → disrupts Gram-negative cell membrane phospholipids; bactericidal; nephrotoxic and neurotoxic
  33. Colistin = Polymyxin E — Last resort for multidrug-resistant Gram-negative infections; plasmid-mediated colistin resistance (MCR) is emerging global threat
  34. Amphotericin B mechanism — Polyene; binds ergosterol in fungal membrane → pores → cell death; gold standard antifungal; dose-limiting nephrotoxicity
  35. Azole mechanism — Inhibit lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51) → deplete ergosterol → disrupt membrane integrity
  36. Griseofulvin — Oral antidermatophyte; inhibits microtubule polymerisation → inhibits mitosis; long treatment course; teratogenic
  37. Acyclovir selectivity — Phosphorylated ONLY by viral thymidine kinase (in infected cells) → non-toxic to uninfected cells; active against Herpes simplex and Varicella
  38. Antiviral limitations — Drugs inhibit only actively replicating virus; cannot eliminate latent infections; mostly virustatic; narrow spectrum
  39. Interferons — Cytokines from virus-infected cells; highly species-specific; three types (IFN-α, IFN-β, IFN-γ); inhibit all steps of viral multiplication
  40. Antineoplastic dosing — Based on Body Surface Area (m²), not body weight; correlates better with BMR, blood volume, cardiac output, renal function; SA = Km × W²/³ / 10⁴
  41. Cyclophosphamide toxicity — Haemorrhagic cystitis; treat with MESNA (sodium methyl ethyl sulfonate) or acetyl cysteine as free radical scavengers
  42. Cell cycle specific vs non-specific — Cycle specific (S or M phase): Methotrexate, Vinca alkaloids; Cycle non-specific (all stages except G₀): Alkylating agents, antineoplastic antibiotics
  43. Imidocarb — Drug of choice for babesiosis and anaplasmosis; mechanism: vacuolisation of cytoplasm; NOT to be given IV; detectable in tissues up to 4 weeks after IM
  44. Anticoccidial programmes — Shuttle programme (two drugs sequentially in same flock) vs Rotation programme (different drugs in successive flocks) — both combat resistance development
  45. Ionophore anticoccidials — Most effective; interfere with ion transport; active against sporozoites and 1st schizonts; must be fed continuously; growth promoters; horses most susceptible to toxicity
  46. Ivermectin mechanism — Binds glutamate-gated chloride channels (invertebrate-specific) → hyperpolarisation → flaccid paralysis; safe in mammals (channels behind BBB); AVOID in MDR1-mutant Collies
  47. Benzimidazole mechanism — Bind β-tubulin of parasites → prevent microtubule polymerisation → slow acting (3–5 days); also ovicidal and larvicidal
  48. Praziquantel mechanism — Increases Ca²⁺ permeability of tegument → spastic paralysis of cestodes/trematodes; drug of choice for tapeworms and schistosomes
  49. OPC antidote — Atropine sulphate (block muscarinic effects) + Pralidoxime/2-PAM (reactivate AChE) | Carbamate antidote: Atropine ONLY (NOT 2-PAM)
  50. Pyrethroids + cats — Dangerous combination; cats lack glucuronyl transferase → accumulation → neurotoxicity; permethrin spot-on formulations for dogs are toxic to cats
  51. Ideal disinfectant properties — Broad spectrum; rapid germicidal; not inactivated by organic matter; non-toxic, non-corrosive, non-staining; deodorising; detergent property; economical
  52. Organic matter — Most important factor reducing disinfectant efficacy; must clean before disinfecting; aldehydes (glutaraldehyde) penetrate organic matter better than most others
  53. Povidone iodine — Broad-spectrum antiseptic including spores and fungi; iodophores release free iodine slowly (prolonged action, reduced irritation)
  54. QAC (Quaternary Ammonium Compounds) — Cationic surfactants; inactivated by anionic soap and hard water; better against Gram-positive than Gram-negative organisms
  55. Indigenous plants — Vinca rosea (Vincristine/Vinblastine → anticancer, TVT in dogs); Rauwolfia (Reserpine → antihypertensive); Azadirachta indica (Neem → antibacterial, antifungal, anthelmintic, insecticidal)

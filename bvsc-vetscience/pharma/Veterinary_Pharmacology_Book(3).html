Chapter 01

Definitions, Scope & History of Toxicology

Toxicology is defined as 'the science of poisons' — the study of adverse effects of chemicals or physical agents on living organisms and ecosystems, including prevention and amelioration of such adverse effects. It is both a science (observational, data-gathering) and an art (utilizing data to predict outcomes in populations).

Key Terminology

TermDefinition
PoisonNaturally occurring or man-made chemicals that, following entry in small quantities, produce biochemical abnormalities or physical lesions. Also called toxicants or toxic agents.
ToxinPoisons produced by a biological source — phytotoxins (plants), mycotoxins (fungi), zootoxins (lower animals), venoms (transmitted by bite/sting).
ToxicityInherent capacity of a substance to produce toxic or detrimental changes; the adverse end product of exposure to chemical, physical or biological agents.
ToxicosisThe condition resulting from exposure to poisons (synonymous with poisoning or intoxication).

Three Phases of Toxicology

Toxicology is studied under three phases: exposure phase (contact with the toxicant), toxicokinetic phase (ADME — what the body does to the toxicant), and toxicodynamic phase (what the toxicant does to the body at cellular/molecular level).

Chapter 03

Types of Poisoning & Toxicity Testing

Types of Poisoning

TypeCharacteristics
Acute PoisoningRelatively large single dose; rapid manifestation of severe clinical signs; sudden violent syndrome
Sub-acute PoisoningLower exposure level; longer survival than acute; symptoms develop gradually; toxicity studies: 90 days; used to determine NOEL/NOAEL
Chronic PoisoningMultiple exposures; cumulative effect; long delay between first exposure and signs; study duration: 6 months–2 years (rodents), 1 year (non-rodents)

Untoward Effects of Toxic Substances

EffectDescription / Example
AllergySensitization to previous dose of the same material
TeratogenicityMalformations in offspring due to gestational exposure; examples: Veratrum, Lupinus plants; drugs — thalidomide, colchicine
CarcinogenicityAgent induces neoplasia after considerable delay; transforms normal cells into uncontrolled proliferating ones
MutagenicityAgent induces heritable changes in DNA by covalent modification of bases; mutations passed on during cell division

Toxicity Testing Methods

Different testing methods include: acute toxicity, subchronic toxicity, chronic toxicity, developmental toxicity, reproductive toxicity, phototoxicity, behavioural toxicity, hypersensitivity, ocular and skin irritation tests, mutagenicity, teratogenicity and carcinogenicity. Toxicokinetic studies complement these. Rodents (rats and mice) are the most common test species.

Chronicity Factor
Ratio of acute LD₅₀ to chronic LD₅₀. A factor > 2.0 indicates a cumulative toxicant. Example: Potassium cyanide — acute LD₅₀ = 10 mg/kg, but pre-exposed animals tolerate 250 mg/kg → chronicity factor = 0.04 (not cumulative due to tolerance development).

Common Sources of Animal Poisoning

Naturally occurring: inorganic minerals (fluoride, nitrates, copper, molybdenum, selenium, lead); plants; mould products; venomous snakes, toads and insects.

Man-made hazards: Industrial products (arsenic, lead, fluoride, cadmium, mercury; organic substances ethanol, cyanide, fluoroacetamide); domestic materials (lead paint, phenolic compounds, pesticides, human medications); pharmaceutical preparations.

Chapter 04

Toxicokinetics — ADME of Toxicants

Toxicokinetics differs from pharmacokinetics in that clinical toxicology cases involve potential poisoning (not yet absorbed), actual toxicoses (altered physiology may alter kinetics), and residue contamination of food products.

Absorption

Absorption follows pulmonary or cutaneous exposure or oral ingestion. Also occurs via subcutaneous, intramuscular, intraperitoneal or intravenous routes, and via mammary gland, uterus and eye. The primary factor affecting absorption is solubility — insoluble and ionized compounds are poorly absorbed; lipid-soluble substances are readily absorbed even through intact skin.

RouteKey Points
PulmonaryImportant for gases, volatiles, fine powders, aerosols; all soluble substances rapidly absorbed via vascular pulmonary mucosa; birds are particularly vulnerable to fumes from overheated PTFE cookware
CutaneousSignificant for oily/emulsion vehicles; DMSO increases absorption; powders generally not absorbed (except chlorinated hydrocarbons); abrasions increase rate
Oral/GISmall intestine = primary site; HCl in stomach increases solubility; feed dilutes toxins; constipation increases toxicity; diarrhoea decreases absorption; ruminant rumen contents dilute and microflora detoxify

Tissue Distribution

Selective deposition in various tissues depends on receptor sites. Key examples of selective accumulation:

Biotransformation

Most substances undergo biotransformation. Lethal synthesis — non-toxic compounds converted to toxic metabolites (e.g., parathion → paraoxon; fluoroacetate → fluorocitrate, which blocks Kreb's cycle).

Phase I reactions: hydroxylation, deamination, dealkylation → polar metabolites; may be excreted in urine. Phase II reactions: glucuronic acid conjugation, sulfation, acetylation, glutathione conjugation, methylation. Cats have poorly developed glucuronyl conjugating capacity → susceptible to benzoic acid toxicity.

Excretion & Bioaccumulation

Xenobiotics excreted via faeces, urine, sweat, breath or milk. Organochlorine compounds, arsenic trioxide and lead are poorly absorbed and mainly excreted in faeces. Aflatoxins excreted in milk — residue significance.

Bioaccumulation: organism absorbs toxicant faster than it is excreted. Biomagnification (bioamplification): increase in concentration across trophic (food chain) levels. Bioconcentration: uptake from water > excretion.

Toxicokinetics vs. Pharmacokinetics — Key Differences

In toxicoses, kinetics are more likely altered by saturation of metabolic enzymes (resulting in nonlinear kinetics), organ damage (liver/kidney failure altering metabolism/excretion), and circulatory collapse causing hypotension and acidosis.

Chapter 05

Toxicodynamics — Mechanisms of Toxic Action

Toxicodynamics = what the toxicant does to the body. Cellular damage is the basis of most toxicological injury, involving quantitative changes in function of cells, tissues and organs.

Cellular Mechanisms of Toxic Injury

Mixed function oxidases (primarily in endoplasmic reticulum) biotransform xenobiotics to electrophilic intermediates that covalently bind to cellular macromolecules (denaturation). Free radicals damage cellular macromolecules — lipid peroxidation is a key consequence. Glutathione depletion enhances oxidative damage. Antioxidant defences: superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase, vitamin E.

Types of Toxicant–Target Molecule Interactions

Interaction TypeExample

Specific Mechanisms of Intoxication

Key mechanisms include: chemical injury (corrosives, caustics); enzyme inhibition/competition (cholinesterase inhibition by OPs); uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation; inhibition of nucleic acid and protein synthesis; functional effects on nervous system (altered membrane permeability to ions); blood/vascular injury (methaemoglobin formation, coagulopathy); developmental effects; carcinogenesis; immunosuppression.

Age, Sex & Nutritional Status

Age: Very young (poorly developed detoxification) and very old (age-related lesions, lowered metabolic/excretory capacity) animals are more vulnerable. Sex: Little difference; rat is an exception. Pregnancy increases susceptibility (e.g., pregnant ewes more susceptible to lead). Nutrition: Protein-deficient diets, gut parasites and malnutrition markedly influence response to toxicants.

Environmental Factors

Temperature and humidity affect rates of consumption and occurrence of toxicants. Many mycotoxins correlate with seasonal/climatic changes. Ergot ischemic effects worsen in winter cold. Plant nitrate levels affected by rainfall. Air circulation affects silo gas accumulation. Some toxicants degrade in bright sunlight.

Chapter 07

General Treatment of Poisoning — Part I

Cardinal Principle
Treat the animal — not the poison. Stabilize vital physiological functions first. Antidotal measures are useless if the animal has lost vital functions.

Principles of Treatment

The five principles are: (1) Remove source and prevent further exposure; (2) Delay further absorption; (3) Hasten elimination of absorbed poison; (4) Provide supportive therapy; (5) Use specific antidotes.

1. Decontamination — Skin & Eye

Wash with plenty of lukewarm water. Clip hair/wool if necessary. Use soapy water (low pH soap) and rinse copiously. Never use: organic solvents (alcohol, white spirit) or oily substances — they increase percutaneous absorption. Do not rub vigorously. Flush eyes many times with water or normal saline.

2. Delaying Absorption — Emetics

Emetics are of value in dogs, cats and pigs if done within a few hours. Contraindicated in: rodents, rabbits, horses and ruminants (cannot vomit safely). Also contraindicated when: swallowing reflex is absent, animal is convulsing/sedated, corrosive agents/volatile hydrocarbons involved, aspiration pneumonia risk.

Emetic TypeAgent / Dose

Gastric Lavage & Adsorbents

Gastric lavage: flush 10–15 times with water/normal saline until aspirate is clear; head lowered to 30°; 10 ml/kg body weight. Not practical in large animals.

Activated charcoal: 2–5 g/kg body weight as slurry in water (1g/5ml). After charcoal-toxin complex forms, toxin begins to desorb if stays too long — use cathartic to hasten expulsion. Universal antidote: 10 g activated charcoal + 5 g kaolin + 5 g tannic acid + 5 g magnesium oxide in 200 ml water — used when specific antidote not available.

Important: Milk is NOT a general antidote
Milk promotes absorption of liposoluble poisons. Do not administer milk as an antidote — this is a cardinal rule in toxicology.

3. Hastening Elimination

Cathartics: Osmotic cathartics — sodium sulphate, magnesium sulphate or sorbitol orally. Contraindicated in dehydration. Forced diuresis: Indicated for serious clinical toxicosis, potentially lethal dose, or impaired elimination. Risks: pulmonary/cerebral oedema, metabolic alkalosis, electrolyte imbalance.

Urinary pH manipulation: Alkalinizing urine (sodium bicarbonate) increases excretion of acidic compounds (aspirin, barbiturates, 2,4-D). Acidifying urine (ammonium chloride, ascorbic acid) increases excretion of basic compounds (amphetamine, alkaloids).

4. Supportive Therapy

CNS hyperactivity/seizuresDiazepam first; phenobarbital/pentobarbital if unsuccessful; methocarbamol (muscle relaxation). Avoid phenothiazines — lower seizure threshold

Metal Poisoning — Arsenic

Among heavy metals, arsenic plays a major role in toxicological hazards. The most common compound is arsenic trioxide. It forms arsenites with alkalis.

Sources of Arsenic Poisoning

Sodium/potassium arsenite (weed killers, insecticides, sheep dip, wood preservative); copper arsenite (Paris green — insecticide); arsenical dips (combined with sulphur for sheep and cattle); organic arsenicals used in treatment of blackhead in turkeys (histomoniasis) and as skin alteratives; contaminated water near metal smelting works; inadvertent access to arsenical containers; poisoning often due to human carelessness.

Factors Affecting Toxicity

Trivalent compounds are more toxic than pentavalent (pentavalent compounds must be converted to trivalent form). Finely divided soluble forms are more toxic than coarse powders or solids. Bovines and felines are more commonly poisoned; rare in swine and poultry. Herbivores commonly poisoned via contaminated forage.

Absorption and Fate

Soluble salts are more toxic and absorbed through skin. After absorption, arsenic accumulates in liver initially, then stored in bones, skin and keratinized tissues (hair, hoof) — irremovable once in keratinized hair cells. Excreted in urine, faeces, sweat and milk. Arsenic attaches to sulphydryl groups of sulphur-containing amino acids in body.

Mechanism of Toxicity

Arsenic reacts with sulphydryl groups of lipoic acid (essential co-factor for enzymatic decarboxylation of keto acids — pyruvate, ketoglutarate, ketobutyrate). This inhibits formation of acetyl-, succinyl- and propionyl-CoA → inhibition of glycolysis and Krebs cycle. Arsenic also inactivates oxidative enzymes and glutathione. Pentavalent arsenate uncouples mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.

Clinical Symptoms

TypeSigns
Per-acuteDeath is rapid; intense abdominal pain, staggering gait, collapse, paralysis, death
AcuteSalivation, thirst, vomition (possible species), violent colic, watery diarrhoea (sometimes haemorrhagic), exhaustion, collapse, death
Sub-acuteDepression, loss of appetite, staggering gait, apparent paralysis of hindquarters, trembling, stupor, convulsions, coldness of extremities, subnormal temperature, proteinuria, haematuria
ChronicIndigestion, thirst, wasting, unthriftiness, dry staggering coat, brick-red mucous membranes, weak irregular pulse

PM Lesions

Intense rose-red inflammation of alimentary tract; soft yellow liver; oedematous and congested lungs; haemorrhages in heart, peritoneum, kidneys and liver; in birds: inflammation of proventriculus and gizzard with sloughing of horny layer.

Diagnosis & Treatment

Diagnosis: Sudden colic, thirst, straining, purgation; history of access to arsenical sources. Chronic poisoning is difficult to diagnose.

Treatment: Induction of emesis; gastric lavage; enema (carnivores); purgatives (ruminants); demulcents. Sodium thiosulphate: horses/cattle 8–10 g IV as 10–20% solution + 20–30 g orally in 300 ml water. BAL (Dimercaprol): 2–3 mg/kg IM; binds arsenic-lipoic acid complex forming arsenic-mercaptide (non-toxic, easily excreted); relatively ineffective after onset of clinical symptoms. Water-soluble alternatives: DMSA (Succimer), DMPS (Unithiol). Thioctic acid (lipoic acid) and d-penicillamine also useful.

Chapter 10

Metal Poisoning — Lead

Lead poisoning is a cumulative toxicosis. Forms of lead: red lead, white lead, lead arsenate, lead sulphate, lead chromate, lead acetate (sugar of lead — important soluble salt), tetraethyl lead (in petrol).

Sources

White/red lead (plumbing, linoleum, golf balls, roofing felt); petrol/auto exhaust (grass near busy highways); licking discarded storage batteries; lead arsenate insecticide; acidic food in lead-coated vessels; drinking water contaminated by battery effluent; sheep may preferentially graze high contamination areas.

Factors Influencing Toxicity

Very young animals are more susceptible. Goats, swine and chickens are more resistant. Pregnant ewes are more susceptible. Soluble lead acetate is more toxic than insoluble lead oxide. Large amounts ingested in 1–2 days more toxic than gradual intake. Presence of food/ingesta delays absorption.

Absorption and Fate

Lead enters primarily through the mouth. Only a small portion is absorbed; over 98% excreted in faeces. Lead binds to haemoglobin in RBCs and serum albumin (only 1% in free form). Stored mainly in bones — 'bone sink for lead' (~95% of body burden in bone; detoxification mechanism for chronic low-level exposure). Lead crosses both blood-brain barrier and placental barrier. Excreted in faeces, urine and milk.

Mechanism of Toxicity

Lead inhibits enzymes with free sulphydryl groups → decreased haeme synthesis (anaemia). Specifically inhibits δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) dehydratase and haeme synthetase → accumulation of protoporphyrin IX and non-haeme iron in RBCs. Prevents iron entry from cytosol to mitochondria.

Clinical Symptoms

SpeciesClinical Signs
Cattle (Acute)Signs appear 2–3 days after fatal dose; calves bellow and stagger with rolling eyes, frothing mouth; animal appears blind; excited; head pushing against wall; muscular spasm, tetany, death
Cattle (Chronic)Anorexia, constipation, recumbency, death
SheepSimilar to cattle but tetany not observed; pregnant ewes may abort
HorsesLaryngeal muscle paralysis → roaring; paralysis of limbs, nasal discharge, jaundice
DogsGI symptoms (anorexia, vomiting, colic, diarrhoea) + nervous symptoms (hysterical barking, epileptiform convulsions)
BirdsAnorexia, ataxia, excitement, loss of condition; decreased egg production, fertility and hatchability
HumansBurton's blue line in gums, wrist drop, growth arrest, lead lines in bones on x-ray, basophilic stippling of RBCs

Diagnosis

Blood lead >0.2 ppm (whole blood) or liver lead >4 ppm indicates lead poisoning (twice these levels are fatal). Increased urine ALA. Decreased blood ALA dehydratase activity. Nucleated RBCs and basophilic stippling in dogs (also in autoimmune haemolytic anaemia — differential).

Treatment

Sodium calcium edetate (CaNa₂EDTA): chelates lead; cattle/horses 110 mg/kg IV/SC in two doses at 6-hour intervals every other day for 3 treatments; dogs 110 mg/kg SC as 1% solution in saline/dextrose divided into 4 doses every other day for 3 treatments. Thiamine 2–4 mg/kg SC in cattle (given with EDTA). BAL increases lead excretion in urine; can reach brain tissue. Magnesium sulphate orally reduces lead solubility. Dexamethasone + mannitol for cerebral oedema. Broad-spectrum antibiotics for secondary bacterial infection (lead is immunosuppressant).

Chapter 11

Metal Poisoning — Mercury & Iron

Mercury Poisoning

Mercury available as: elemental mercury, inorganic mercury and organic mercury. Sources: feed treated with mercurial fungicides; contaminated water; thermometers; dental amalgams; fish/marine organisms (bioaccumulation of organic mercury); burning coal releases mercury.

Mercury — Absorption and Fate

Aryl mercurials are rapidly metabolized to inorganic salts. Alkyl mercurials are slowly metabolized to divalent mercury. Inorganic mercury excreted mainly in urine; organic mercury excreted mainly in faeces.

Mercury — Mechanism of Toxicity

Inorganic mercury salts cause direct tissue necrosis and renal tubular necrosis. Mercuric ions bind covalently with sulphur → inhibit sulphydryl enzymes in microsomes and mitochondria. Organic alkyl mercurials interfere with metabolic activity and prevent synthesis of essential proteins → cellular degeneration and necrosis; most important target organ = brain.

Mercury — Clinical Symptoms & PM Lesions

Inorganic mercury: stomatitis, pharyngitis, vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration, shock, oliguria, azoturia. Organic mercury: erythema, conjunctivitis, lacrimation, stomatitis, neurological seizures. Mercurial ptyalism (humans): profuse salivation, swollen/bleeding gums, loosening teeth, jaw necrosis. PM: GI ulcers, necrotic enteritis/colitis, pale swollen kidney, renal tubular necrosis.

Mercury — Treatment

Egg white, activated charcoal, sodium thiosulphate (binds mercury), saline cathartic, oral d-penicillamine. BAL is not very effective after chronic organic mercurial exposure.

Iron Toxicosis

Iron is essential but lethal at high doses. Toxic dose: 20–40 mg elemental Fe/kg may cause toxicosis; >60 mg/kg is potentially serious; ~200 mg/kg is lethal. Acute poisoning occurs primarily in dogs (indiscriminate eating habits). Injectable iron (dextran/dextrin complex) toxicity seen in pigs, especially piglets from selenium/vitamin E deficient sows.

Iron — Mechanism of Toxicity

True iron toxicity = direct damage to GI epithelium (corrosive, strong irritant) + free radical production (lipid peroxidation, glutathione depletion causing liver damage) + hypotension/increased capillary permeability due to ferritin + interference with blood coagulation. Death due to inadequate perfusion of vital organs.

Iron — Clinical Symptoms

Anaphylactic reactions: stagger, collapse, cyanosis, dyspnoea, shock. Oral iron toxicity: emesis, bloody diarrhoea, paleness, weakness, prostration, cyanosis, circulatory collapse. Baby pigs: pale skin, corrosion of mucosa, dark faeces, diarrhoea, tachycardia. PM: cyanosis, hepatic necrosis, icterus, pulmonary oedema, yellowish-brown tissue discolouration near injection site.

Iron — Treatment

Anaphylaxis: epinephrine, antihistaminics, oxygen. Oral toxicity: milk of magnesia to precipitate iron; egg, water, milk, emetics. Desferrioxamine 40 mg/kg IV every 4–8 hours (reddish-brown vin rose coloured urine = success). Ascorbic acid + desferrioxamine orally enhances iron excretion.

Metal Poisoning — Selenium & Copper

Selenium Poisoning

Selenium is similar to sulphur and may replace sulphur in amino acids. In Ireland, selenium toxicity is called dog murrain. Sources include obligate indicator plants (require 100–10,000 ppm selenium; e.g., Astragalus/locoweed, Oonopsis/goldenweed), facultative indicator plants (tolerate 25–100 ppm; e.g., Sideranthus, Atriplex/saltbrush), non-accumulator plants on seleniferous soils (1–25 ppm), feed supplements, injectable drugs, seafood.

Selenium — Mechanism of Toxicity

Glutathione depletion and lipid peroxidation are major mechanisms. Selenium replaces sulphur in amino acids (cysteine and methionine), affecting essential proteins. Chronic selenosis depresses ATP formation (inhibition of sulphydryl enzymes). Tissue ascorbic acid levels fall → vascular damage.

Copper Poisoning

Copper sulphate is widely used as an antifungal and in destruction of snails. Copper — Mechanism of Toxicity

Copper salts act as direct tissue irritants and oxidants → coagulative necrosis of GI mucosa. Copper — Clinical Symptoms & PM Lesions

Acute: Nausea, vomiting, salivation, purgation, violent abdominal pain, dehydration, tachycardia, shock, collapse. Faeces are deep green. Chronic: Decreased ruminal fermentation → impaired liver function (anorexia, depression, diarrhoea) → generalised icterus, haemoglobinaemia, haemoglobinuria, recumbency. PM: In chronic cases — icterus, enlarged yellow fragile liver, enlarged spleen, port-wine coloured urine, gun-metal kidney.

Copper — Treatment

Treatment is often unsuccessful. Gastrointestinal sedatives; d-penicillamine + calcium versenate; ammonium tetrathiomolybdate; daily ammonium molybdate + sodium thiosulphate.

Metal Poisoning — Molybdenum & Non-metal Toxicoses

Molybdenum Poisoning (Teart Disease)

Chronic molybdenum poisoning is known as teart. Severity related to water-soluble molybdenum content of herbage. Hay from teart pastures is harmless. Non-ruminants are less susceptible. Cattle are less tolerant than sheep.

Molybdenum — Mechanism of Toxicity

Reduces copper storage in liver of sheep and cattle. Competition between molybdenum and phosphorus (Mo displaces P → skeletal lesions). Molybdenum forms complexes with catechols → bacteriostatic activity in GI bacteria → liquid faeces full of gas bubbles. Anaemia due to copper deficiency.

Molybdenum — Clinical Symptoms

Persistent severe scouring with liquid faeces full of gas bubbles (peat scours / teart disease). Weight loss, harsh staring coat. Pacing disease (abnormal pacing gait). Spectacled appearance — depigmentation around eyes. In sheep/young animals: enzootic ataxia (Australia) or swayback disease (UK) — stiffness of back and legs.

Molybdenum — Treatment

Daily administration of copper sulphate.

Nitrate-Nitrite, Fluoride & Phosphorus Poisoning

Nitrate-Nitrite Poisoning

Nitrates are non-toxic; they are converted to nitrites in feedstuffs or in the alimentary tract (particularly in the rumen). Nitrite is 6–10 times more toxic than nitrates. Ruminants are more susceptible than monogastric animals.

Nitrate Sources

Whey, preserved feed, fertilizers, well water; plants that accumulate nitrate on nitrogen-rich soils: Amaranthus retroflexus, Brassica species, Chenopodium album, Datura, Beta vulgaris, Ipomoea species. Nitrate content higher on dull days and at night (due to lowered nitrate reductase activity). During drought, nitrate increases in soil — danger when drought breaks and hungry animals access lush new growth.

Nitrate — Mechanism of Toxicity

Mechanism 1: One mole of absorbed nitrite reacts with two moles of haemoglobin → methaemoglobin formation (ferrous Fe²⁺ → ferric Fe³⁺). Symptoms at 20% methaemoglobin; death when 80% is converted. Mechanism 2: Relaxation of vascular smooth muscle → vasodilatation → systemic hypotension and decreased cardiac output.

Nitrate — Clinical Symptoms & Treatment

Symptoms: Abdominal pain, diarrhoea, muscular weakness, incoordination, accelerated heart rate, dyspnoea, progressive cyanosis (bluish mucous membranes), coma, death. Dark brown/coffee-coloured blood. Abortion in pregnant animals.

Treatment: Methylene blue IV at 4–8 mg/kg in cattle/sheep as 1% solution. Methylene blue is reduced to leucomethylene blue by NADPH₂-reductase → converts methaemoglobin back to haemoglobin. Second dose after 6–8 hours. Ascorbic acid also useful. Large doses of oral antibiotics can reduce nitrate-to-nitrite conversion by rumen microflora.

Fluoride Toxicosis

Sources: industrial fluoride (smelters, phosphate fertilizer plants), fluoride-contaminated water, feed supplements with excessive fluoride. Fluoride accumulates proportionally to duration and rate of exposure in skeleton. Depletion from bones is slow. Accumulation in skeleton not toxicologically significant in foetal bone.

Fluoride — Mechanism of Toxicity

Excessive fluoride results in delayed and impaired mineralisation of bone (fluorosis). Inhibits enzymes, especially those requiring Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ as cofactors. Fluoride binds to calcium → hypocalcaemia.

Fluoride — Clinical Symptoms

Anorexia, excessive thirst, salivation, initially diarrhoea then constipation, polyuria then anuria, nasal discharge, weak pulse, normal body temperature but cold ears and skin, muscle rigidity, hyperexcitability, blindness, stumbling, walking backwards. Dental fluorosis (mottled, chalky teeth); skeletal fluorosis (thickened bones, exostoses).

Phosphorus Poisoning

Sources: fertilizer; rat/ant baits containing white phosphorus. Red phosphorus = inert and non-toxic; White/yellow phosphorus = toxic. Local caustic action responsible for gastroenteritis and diarrhoea. No specific treatment available.

Clinical symptoms: Garlic odour of GI contents (diagnostic). Per-acute: violent convulsions, severe CNS depression, coma. Acute: salivation, nausea, vomiting, severe diarrhoea, fever, polydipsia, polyuria. Extravasation of blood into subcutaneous tissues and muscles.

Treatment: Activated charcoal, emetics, saline purgatives, demulcents and astringents.

Chapter 15

Urea/Ammonia & Common Salt Poisoning

Urea/Non-Protein Nitrogen (NPN) Poisoning

Urea is used as NPN supplement in ruminant rations and as fertilizer. Toxicosis results from accumulation of ammonia. Urease (present in plants, microorganisms, and rumen) hydrolyses urea — speeded up by alkaline pH. Ammonium salts used as expectorants and in urolithiasis.

Urea — Factors Enhancing Toxicosis

Rapid ingestion; low energy/protein + high fibre diet; palatable urea concentrate (e.g., urea molasses); high rumen pH; high body temperature; water deficiency; feeds rich in urease (soybeans); stress; hepatic insufficiency.

Urea — Mechanism of Toxicity

In rumen, ammonia is initially as ammonium ion (NH₄⁺) — not absorbed. At pH 6.2 → mostly NH₄⁺. At pH 9 → NH₃/NH₄⁺ = 1 (pKa of ammonium ion = 9.3). At pH ≥10 → soluble uncharged NH₃ is readily absorbed. In blood at pH 7.4, almost all ammonia is in ionic form (NH₄⁺). High blood ammonia is CNS toxic.

Urea — Clinical Symptoms & Treatment

Symptoms: Initial hyperaesthesia, tremors, twitching and muscular spasms (starting from eyelids, proceeding toward tail); autonomic signs: salivation, bradycardia, hypertension, severe colic; GI signs: rumen atony, bloat, diarrhoea; dyspnoea, tetany, convulsions. Treatment: Cold water drenching to lower rumen pH; 5% acetic acid (vinegar) orally to acidify rumen; rumenotomy; supportive therapy.

Common Salt (NaCl) Poisoning — Water Deprivation Toxicosis

Common salt toxicosis (also called salt poisoning or water deprivation-sodium ion toxicosis) occurs when animals are deprived of water and then allowed to drink freely, or are fed diets high in sodium without adequate water. Pigs and poultry are most commonly affected.

Common Salt — Mechanism & Clinical Signs

Hypernatremia → cellular dehydration → CNS oedema. Cerebrospinal fluid sodium concentrations remain elevated. When water is suddenly available after deprivation, rapid influx into brain cells → cerebral oedema. In pigs: typical signs are restlessness, thirst, pruritus, constipation/diarrhoea, then CNS signs including circling, head pressing, blindness, opisthotonus, convulsions, death. In poultry: wet droppings, wing drooping, paralysis, high mortality.

PM lesions: Eosinophilic meningoencephalitis (perivasular cuffing with eosinophils in brain — pathognomonic in pigs). Treatment: Gradual water restriction and slowly rehydrate; diuretics to reduce cerebral oedema; supportive care. Rapid rehydration worsens outcome.

Cyanogenic Plants & Photosensitization

Cyanogenic Plants (Prussic Acid / HCN Poisoning)

Hydrocyanic acid (prussic acid) is the most toxic and rapidly acting form of cyanide. Sodium and potassium salts are slightly toxic; ferrocyanides and thiocyanates are complex cyanides and practically harmless. Cyanide poisoning in animals is generally associated with ingestion of cyanogenic plants.

Cyanogenic Plant Sources

Plants containing cyanogenic glycosides include: sorghum (Sorghum bicolor, S. sudanense), Johnson grass, arrowgrass (Triglochin maritimum), white clover (Trifolium repens), flax (Linum usitatissimum), cassava (Manihot esculenta), elderberry (Sambucus species), bitter almond and cherry laurel. Young plants and regrowth after drought or frost have highest HCN content.

Cyanide — Mechanism of Toxicity

Cyanide ion binds to ferric iron (Fe³⁺) of cytochrome oxidase → prevents conversion of Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ → electron transport chain blocked → cellular anoxia despite adequate oxygen in blood. Blood is bright cherry red (oxyhaemoglobin not utilized). Venous blood has same colour as arterial blood.

Cyanide — Treatment

Sodium nitrite IV: induces methaemoglobin formation; ferric iron of methaemoglobin competes with cytochrome oxidase for cyanide → forms cyanmethaemoglobin → reactivates cytochrome oxidase. Then Sodium thiosulphate IV: converts cyanide in cyanmethaemoglobin to thiocyanate (non-toxic) via rhodanese enzyme. Sodium nitrite should not be repeated (excessive methaemoglobin formation is fatal).

Bracken Fern (Pteridium aquilinum)

Causes thiamine deficiency (thiaminase enzyme) in horses, pigs, and cats. In cattle: enzootic haematuria (haemorrhagic cystitis) and aplastic anaemia (bone marrow suppression → agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia). Toxicity in cattle also partially due to a carcinogen. Horses: neurologic signs; cattle: haemorrhagic syndromes.

Aflatoxins

Produced by Aspergillus flavus and A. parasiticus. AFB₁ is the most toxic and carcinogenic. AFB₁ binds to nuclear DNA → decreases RNA synthesis → decreases enzyme and protein synthesis. Produced by Penicillium rubrum and P. purpurogenum (common soil fungi). Exposure through cereal grains — primarily corn. Cause hepatotoxicity and haemorrhagic disease. Treatment: supportive care, change of diet (avoid corn).

Fumonisins

Produced by Fusarium moniliforme. Cause leukoencephalomalacia in horses (liquefactive necrosis of white matter of brain) and pulmonary oedema in swine. Sphingolipid biosynthesis is disrupted — fumonisins are structurally similar to sphingoid bases and inhibit sphingolipid biosynthesis. Treatment: change of diet; quiet surroundings; maintain hydration.

Ochratoxins

Produced by Aspergillus ochraceus and Penicillium verrucosum. Primarily nephrotoxic; target organ is kidney → nephropathy, immunosuppression. Affect protein synthesis. Pigs most susceptible. Found in grain and grain products.

Zearalenone (F-2 Toxin)

Produced by Fusarium graminearum. Oestrogenic mycotoxin. Causes reproductive disorders: vulvovaginitis, nymphomanic behaviour, infertility, abortion, vaginal prolapse, mammary gland hypertrophy, and precocious puberty in prepubertal gilts. Pigs are most susceptible.

Ergot (Claviceps purpurea)

Sclerotia (ergots) of Claviceps purpurea replace grain kernels of rye, wheat and other grasses. Contains ergotamine and other ergot alkaloids → vasoconstrictive and oxytocic effects. Causes ergotism: gangrenous form (dry gangrene of extremities — St. Antony's Fire) and convulsive form (CNS effects). Abortion in cattle.

Agrochemical Toxicoses — Organochlorines & Organophosphates

Organochlorine (Chlorinated Hydrocarbon) Insecticides

Examples: DDT, aldrin, dieldrin, chlordane, BHC/lindane, heptachlor, endrin, toxaphene, endosulfan. These are persistent in the environment; cumulative in fat depots (bioaccumulate). Now largely banned in many countries.

Organochlorine — Mechanism of Toxicity

Act on the nervous system: sodium influx is enhanced and potassium outflow is inhibited → enhanced action potential → increased tendency for seizures. A metabolite of DDT inhibits adrenal output by selective necrosis of zona fasciculata and zona reticularis.

Organochlorine — Clinical Signs & Treatment

CNS stimulation: hyperexcitability, tremors, incoordination, convulsions. Tachycardia. Chronic exposure: hepatotoxicity, immunosuppression. Treatment: no specific antidote. Control seizures with barbiturates or diazepam. Activated charcoal. High-fat diet can mobilize stored toxicant — avoid during acute toxicosis. Avoid epinephrine (sensitizes myocardium to arrhythmias).

Organophosphate (OP) Insecticides

First OP introduced: TEPP (tetraethyl pyrophosphate) during WWII. Extremely toxic nerve gases tabun and sarin also developed. Current OPs include: parathion, malathion, diazinon, dimethoate, fenthion, dichlorvos (DDVP), phorate, phosmet, chlorpyrifos, methyl parathion.

OP — Mechanism of Toxicity

OPs irreversibly inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE) at both anionic and esteratic sites → accumulation of acetylcholine (ACh) at cholinergic synapses → overstimulation of muscarinic and nicotinic receptors.

Carbamates, Pyrethroids & Other Insecticides

Carbamate Insecticides

Derivatives of carbamic acid. Examples: aldicarb, carbaryl, methomyl, carbofuran, propoxur. Mechanism: reversible inhibition of acetylcholinesterase (compared to irreversible with OPs). Clinical signs similar to OPs (SLUDGE) but generally shorter duration and less severe.

Key Comparison — OPs vs. Carbamates
OPs: Irreversible AChE inhibition; 2-PAM (oximes) ARE useful (if given early). Carbamates: Reversible AChE inhibition; 2-PAM (oximes) are NOT useful (may worsen signs); atropine alone is the treatment.

Pyrethrins & Pyrethroids

Pyrethrins = natural insecticides from Chrysanthemum cinerariaefolium. Pyrethroids = synthetic analogues with greater stability and potency. Examples of pyrethroids: permethrin, cypermethrin, deltamethrin, allethrin, fenvalerate, resmethrin.

Pyrethroids — Mechanism of Toxicity

Prolong opening of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in axonal membranes → repetitive neuronal firing. Also inhibit Ca²⁺-ATPase and Ca²⁺-Mg²⁺-ATPase. Allethrin allosterically affects acetylcholine nicotinic receptor binding. Two syndromes: Type I (T syndrome — Tremor): coordinated tremors, whole body writhing; Type II (CS syndrome — Choreoathetosis/Salivation): choreoathetosis, salivation, convulsions.

Pyrethroids — Treatment

No specific antidote. Decontamination; diazepam for tremors/seizures; atropine for hypersalivation; supportive care. Pyrethroid is potentiated by piperonyl butoxide (synergist in formulations).

Cats and Pyrethroids

Cats are highly susceptible to permethrin and other synthetic pyrethroids — lack sufficient glucuronyl transferase to metabolize them. Permethrin spot-on products for dogs should never be used on cats.

Ivermectin

Macrolide endectocide. Cumulative toxicity varies with species, breed and age. Collies and related breeds are extremely susceptible (MDR1 gene mutation — P-glycoprotein deficiency → ivermectin crosses blood-brain barrier). Younger animals more susceptible. Mechanism: enhances GABA-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission → CNS depression. Treatment: supportive; physostigmine may help (analeptic for GABA antagonism).

Venomous Bites & Stings

General Principles of Zoo Toxins (Venoms)

Venomous animals include snakes, scorpions, spiders, wasps, bees, toads, and marine animals (cone snails, jellyfish). Venom components selectively target: nervous system (neurotoxins) or cardiovascular system (cardiotoxins). Key variables: species, venom composition, dose, site of envenomation, and species of animal bitten.

Snake Bite

Identification: poisonous snake bite = two fang marks without tooth rows; non-poisonous snake bite = two rows of teeth marks without fang marks. Venom types: Neurotoxic (cobras, kraits, sea snakes) → block neuromuscular junction; Haemotoxic/cytotoxic (vipers, pit vipers) → coagulopathy, haemolysis, local necrosis; Cardiotoxic (some cobras).

Snake Bite — Clinical Signs & Treatment

Salvation, hyperexcitability, mydriasis, asphyxia, local swelling, pain and necrosis at bite site, neuromuscular paralysis, haemorrhagic syndrome. Scorpion Sting

Scorpion venom contains neurotoxins that modulate ion channels → massive release of catecholamines and ACh → autonomic storm. Clinical signs: local pain, autonomic instability (tachycardia, hypertension, arrhythmias), hypersalivation, lacrimation, respiratory distress. Treatment: specific antivenin (most effective); prazosin (alpha-blocker) for hypertensive crisis; atropine for muscarinic signs; supportive care.

Spider Envenomation

Spiders have direct lytic effect on RBCs. Most venomous: Brown recluse spider, Hobo spider, Black widow spider. Black widow venom (alpha-latrotoxin) causes massive ACh and catecholamine release at neuromuscular junction → muscle spasms, pain, autonomic instability. Treatment: calcium gluconate IV for muscle spasms; specific antivenin; supportive care.

Toad Poisoning (Bufo)

Bufo toads secrete toxins from parotid glands. Components: bufadienolides (cardiac glycoside-like — inhibit Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), bufotoxins, bufotenins (serotonin-like), epinephrine, cholesterol, ergosterol, 5-HT. Clinical signs vary with amount absorbed; profuse salivation, head shaking, pawing at mouth, vomiting, cardiac arrhythmias, convulsions, death. Treatment: thorough mouth washing with water; atropine for bradycardia; diazepam for seizures; supportive care.

Radiation Hazards & Industrial Toxicants

Historical Development of Radiation Toxicology

1895 — Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen discovered X-rays (Nobel Prize for Physics, 1901). 1898 — Radium discovered by Marie Curie. Early 20th century — radium therapy misused for benign conditions. 1903 — Marie and Pierre Curie were among first to suffer radiation-related health effects. Nuclear power accidents at Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima highlight ongoing radiation hazards.

Radiation Concepts

TermDefinition
Gray (Gy)Dose per mass of body tissue — 1 Gy = 1 J/kg
Sievert (Sv)Dose equivalent — accounts for biological effectiveness of different radiation types
Half-lifeTime for 50% of radioactive substance to decay; ranges from very short to billions of years; carbon-14 half-life = ~5,730 years

Reducing Radiation Exposure

Time: Reduce time near source. Distance: Increase distance from source (follows inverse square law). Shielding: Place shielding material between source and individual (lead aprons, concrete walls).

Types of Radiation Effects

Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS): haemopoietic syndrome (600–1,000 rad — bone marrow failure, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia), GI syndrome (1,000–5,000 rad — GI epithelium loss, diarrhoea, dehydration), CNS syndrome (>5,000 rad — cerebral oedema, death within hours to days). Chronic effects: carcinogenesis (leukaemia, thyroid, lung cancers), cataracts, reduced fertility, premature ageing, teratogenesis.

Food Additives & Preservatives

Food additives are substances intentionally added to food to achieve a desired technological function (preservation, colouring, flavouring, texturing). Feed additives in veterinary context include substances used to improve feed efficiency and growth rate.

Toxicity Concerns with Food Additives

Although tested for toxicity, humans and animals are exposed chronically to these additives. Key toxicological concerns include: allergic reactions (particularly azo dyes like tartrazine), carcinogenicity of nitrosamines (formed from nitrites in acidic conditions), excitotoxicity of MSG (Chinese restaurant syndrome — headache, flushing, chest tightness), saccharin's weak carcinogenicity in rodents at very high doses, BHA/BHT at high doses → forestomach tumours in rodents.

Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) for Food Additives

ADI = daily intake determined to be 'safe'; usually derived from the LOAEL (Lowest Observed Effect Level) or NOAEL with a 100-fold safety factor. ADI is the amount of exposure considered safe, expressed as mg/kg body weight/day. When the sum of a person's intake from all sources approaches the ADI, the substance must be controlled more strictly.

Regulatory Framework

CODEX Alimentarius Commission (FAO/WHO) sets international food standards. Each food additive must demonstrate: technological need (no safer alternative), safety at proposed use levels, and non-misleading use. Pre-market approval is required in most countries. In India: Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) governs food additives. Regular monitoring of food additives in market products is essential to ensure compliance with MRL and ADI values.

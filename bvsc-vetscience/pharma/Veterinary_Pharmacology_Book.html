History & Evolution of Pharmacology

Pharmacology is the science that studies all aspects of drug action on living systems — their sources, properties, mechanisms, metabolism, and therapeutic uses. The history of pharmacology traces from ancient healing arts to modern molecular science.

Ancient Medicine

CivilisationPeriodKey Contributions
Chinese Medicine~2500 B.C.Earliest records; use of Ephedra (Ma huang) as a tonic
Indian (Ayurveda)AncientCharaka, Sushruta & Vagbhata compiled old and new drugs; science of life
Egyptian~1500 B.C.Ebers Papyrus — drug collection including castor oil and pomegranate bark

Pre-Christian Era

Greek medicine is considered the origin of modern therapeutics. Hippocrates (5th century B.C.) separated medicine from religion — known as the Father of Medicine. He held that four elements (water, fire, air, earth) produce four humors (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) whose imbalance causes disease.

Galen — famous Greek physician who practiced in Rome. His name survives in the term galenical drugs (pharmaceuticals compounded mechanically, mostly from vegetable material). Known as the Father of Polypharmacy.

Mediaeval Medicine

Paracelsus introduced inorganic chemicals (mercury, sulphur, iron, copper sulphate) into medicine, calling this Iatro Chemistry or medicinal chemistry. He popularised laudanum (opium preparation) and tinctures.

Revolts in Medicine

By the early 19th century, shotgun prescriptions (multiple substances with no therapeutic basis) flourished. Gregory advocated venesection, leeching and large doses of purgatives — termed allopathy ("other suffering").

Homeopathy
Samuel Hahnemann introduced homeopathy ("similar suffering") at the start of the 19th century. Two principles: like cures like and dilution potentiates drug action. Father of Homeopathy.

Routes of Drug Administration

Routes of drug administration are classified into three main classes: Enteral (via gut), Parenteral (beyond intestinal — IV, IM, SC, IP etc.), and Topical (skin and mucous membranes).

Factors Governing Choice of Route EXAM HOT

  • Physical and chemical properties of the drug (solid/liquid/gas; solubility, stability, pH, irritancy)
  • Site of desired action — localised or generalised
  • Rate and extent of absorption from different routes
  • Effect of digestive juices and first-pass metabolism
  • Rapidity with which response is desired (routine vs emergency)
  • Accuracy of dosage required
  • Condition of the patient (unconscious, vomiting)

Oral Administration EXAM HOT

Dosage forms: Liquids (solutions, suspensions, emulsions); Solids (powders, tablets, enteric-coated tablets, capsules, granules).

Chapter 05

Structure of Biological Membranes

Biological membranes are mosaics of functional units composed of lipoprotein complexes. The fundamental structure is the unit membrane (plasma membrane) — 80–100 Å thick.

Fluid Mosaic Model EXAM HOT

Fluid Mosaic Model
Singer & Nicolson (1972) proposed this model. Key features:
  • Bilayer of thick lipids with hydrocarbon chains oriented inward (continuous hydrophobic phase)
  • Hydrophilic heads oriented outward
  • Individual lipid molecules can move laterally — making the membrane fluid, flexible, electrically resistant and relatively impermeable to highly polar molecules
  • Membrane proteins embedded in bilayer serve as receptors, carriers, channels
  • Intrinsic proteins extend through full thickness; surround fine aqueous pores
  • Biological membranes behave as lipoids punctured by aqueous pores

Functions of Membranes

  • Selective barrier — controls what enters/exits the cell
  • Site of receptors — elicit electrical or chemical signaling pathways
  • Carrier proteins — facilitate transport of specific substances
  • Enzyme activity — enzymatic proteins on membrane surface
  • Cell recognition and communication
Chapter 06

Passage of Drugs Across Membranes

Drug molecules must cross biological membranes to reach their sites of action. The main mechanisms are: passive diffusion, carrier-mediated transport, pinocytosis/phagocytosis, and filtration.

Passive Diffusion EXAM HOT

The most important mechanism for the majority of drugs. Lipid-soluble drugs dissolve in the lipoidal matrix. Rate of transport is proportional to the lipid:water partition coefficient.

Passive diffusion depends on:

  • Concentration gradient — greater difference = faster diffusion
  • Molecular size — smaller molecules diffuse faster
  • Lipophilicity — higher lipid solubility = higher diffusion rate
  • Temperature — lower temperature = slower diffusion
  • Membrane thickness — thicker membrane = slower diffusion

Carrier-Mediated Transport

Drug combines with a carrier in the membrane; the complex translocates from one side to the other. Carriers for polar molecules form a hydrophobic coating. Substances permitting ion transit = ionophores. Transport is specific, saturable and competitively inhibited.

TypeEnergy Required?DirectionSaturable?Examples
Facilitated diffusionNoAlong concentration gradientYesVitamin B12 from gut; sugars, amino acids
Active transportYesAgainst concentration gradientYesTubular secretion of penicillin; iodide uptake by thyroid

Pinocytosis & Phagocytosis

Pinocytosis = membrane invaginates around fluid droplets. Phagocytosis = membrane engulfs solid particles. These processes are important for uptake of large macromolecules like proteins, polysaccharides and lipids.

Filtration

Passage of small water-soluble molecules through aqueous membrane pores. Important for drugs with molecular weight <100 Da through renal glomeruli and capillary walls.

Ionisation & Drug Absorption EXAM HOT
Most drugs are either weak acids or weak bases. The ionisation state depends on pH and the drug's pKa:
  • Henderson-Hasselbalch equation determines the degree of ionisation
  • Weak acids (pKa 3–8): Better absorbed in acidic stomach; un-ionised in acid
  • Weak bases (pKa 6–12): Better absorbed in alkaline intestine; un-ionised in alkali
  • Un-ionised form = lipid soluble = crosses membranes readily
  • Ionised form = water soluble = trapped on one side (ion trapping)
Memory Block — Membrane Transport
Passive = No energy; along gradient | Active = Energy (ATP); against gradient | Facilitated = No energy + carrier; along gradient | Ionised = trapped; Un-ionised = crosses freely
Chapter 07

Absorption of Drugs

Absorption is the transfer of a drug from its site of administration into the systemic circulation. It determines the onset and duration of drug action.

Absorption from Gastrointestinal Tract

GI absorption is influenced by:

  • Drug lipid solubility and degree of ionisation (Henderson-Hasselbalch)
  • Surface area of GI mucosa (small intestine has greatest surface area due to villi)
  • Splanchnic blood flow
  • Gastric emptying rate
  • Presence of food and other drugs

Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation EXAM HOT

Henderson-Hasselbalch
For weak acids: pH = pKa + log [ionised] / [un-ionised]
For weak bases: pH = pKa + log [un-ionised] / [ionised]

Key principle: A weak acid is better absorbed in an acidic environment (un-ionised → lipid soluble). A weak base is better absorbed in an alkaline environment.

Ion Trapping

When a drug moves across a membrane and the pH on the other side causes ionisation, the drug becomes trapped and cannot diffuse back. This is exploited therapeutically:

  • Alkalinisation of urine traps acidic drugs (e.g. salicylates) → increased renal excretion
  • Acidification of urine traps basic drugs (e.g. amphetamine) → increased renal excretion
  • Alkalinisation of plasma after poisoning with acidic drugs concentrates drug in blood for dialysis

First-Pass Effect EXAM HOT
Drugs absorbed from the GI tract enter the portal circulation → pass through the liver before reaching systemic circulation. The liver may extensively metabolise drugs, reducing their bioavailability. Examples: morphine, lidocaine, propranolol — high first-pass effect.
Chapter 08

Distribution of Drugs

Distribution is the process by which the drug, after absorption, passes from the blood into the tissues. It is influenced by blood flow, membrane permeability, plasma protein binding, and tissue binding.

Volume of Distribution (Vd) EXAM HOT

Volume of Distribution
Vd = Amount of drug in the body / Plasma drug concentration
A drug with a high Vd is extensively distributed to tissues. A drug with a low Vd is largely retained in plasma.

Plasma Protein Binding EXAM HOT

Most drugs bind to plasma proteins, especially albumin (for acidic drugs) and α1-acid glycoprotein (for basic drugs). Only the free (unbound) fraction is pharmacologically active.

ConceptExplanation
Protein bindingReversible; acts as drug reservoir in plasma
Free drugPharmacologically active; available for receptor interaction, metabolism and excretion
Drug displacementDrug A displaced by Drug B → sudden rise in free A → toxicity risk
SaturationAt high doses, binding sites saturate → disproportionate rise in free drug

Tissue Storage

  • Fat/adipose tissue: Lipid-soluble drugs (e.g. thiopental) accumulate; redistribution prolongs action
  • Bone: Tetracyclines and fluoride bind to bone matrix
  • Liver & kidney: Concentrate certain drugs due to active transport

Chapter 09

Biotransformation of Drugs

Biotransformation (drug metabolism) is the enzymatic conversion of drugs into other chemical forms. The liver is the primary site. The main enzyme system is the microsomal cytochrome P-450 (CYP450) mixed-function oxidase system.

Objectives of Biotransformation EXAM HOT

  • Convert lipid-soluble drugs → water-soluble metabolites → easier excretion
  • Drugs are usually converted from active → inactive forms (inactivation)
  • Some drugs are activated (pro-drugs → active metabolites)
  • Some active metabolites are more active than parent drug (e.g. diazepam → oxazepam)
  • Some reactions produce toxic metabolites (e.g. acetaminophen → NAPQI)

Phases of Biotransformation EXAM HOT

PhaseReactionsResultExamples
Phase IOxidation, Reduction, Hydrolysis — Non-syntheticAdds/exposes functional groups (-OH, -NH2, -COOH); product may be active, inactive or toxicCYP450 oxidation; ester hydrolysis
Phase IIConjugation — SyntheticLinks drug/Phase I metabolite to endogenous molecule; usually inactivates and increases water solubilityGlucuronidation, Sulfation, Acetylation, Methylation, Glutathione conjugation

Microsomal CYP450 System

Located in the endoplasmic reticulum of hepatocytes. Catalyses oxidative reactions. Multiple isoforms (CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP3A4) with overlapping specificities. Requires NADPH and molecular oxygen.

Enzyme Induction & Inhibition EXAM HOT

ConceptMechanismClinical ConsequenceExamples
Enzyme InductionIncreased synthesis of CYP450 enzymes; takes days-weeks to developFaster metabolism → reduced drug effect (tolerance) or activation of pro-drugsPhenobarbital, Rifampicin, Griseofulvin induce CYP enzymes
Enzyme InhibitionCompetitive or non-competitive inhibition of CYP450Slower metabolism → drug accumulation → toxicityChloramphenicol, Cimetidine, Ketoconazole inhibit CYP enzymes

Species Differences in Biotransformation

Significant variations exist across veterinary species:

  • Cats: Deficient in glucuronyl transferase → poor glucuronidation → toxicity with drugs like paracetamol, aspirin (do not metabolise phenolic compounds well)
  • Dogs: Poor acetylators
  • Pigs: Similar to humans in many metabolic pathways
  • Ruminants: Extensive presystemic metabolism in rumen; microbial biotransformation

Excretion of Drugs

Excretion is the irreversible removal of drug from the body. The kidney is the main excretory organ. Other routes include bile (faeces), lungs, saliva, sweat, milk and tears.

Renal Elimination EXAM HOT

ProcessLocationCharacteristics
Glomerular filtrationGlomerulusPassive; filters all except protein-bound drug; GFR ~125 ml/min in humans; depends on plasma protein binding
Tubular secretionProximal tubuleActive transport; secretes organic acids and organic bases via separate carrier systems; can be saturated; competitive inhibition possible (probenecid blocks penicillin secretion)
Tubular reabsorptionDistal tubule/Collecting ductPassive; reabsorbs lipid-soluble un-ionised drugs; pH of tubular fluid affects ionisation → ion trapping

Manipulation of Urinary pH for Drug Elimination

To enhance excretion of a drug in overdose:

  • Acidic drugs (salicylates, phenobarbitone): Alkalinise urine (NaHCO3) → ionises acid → traps it in tubule → excreted
  • Basic drugs (amphetamine, morphine): Acidify urine (ammonium chloride) → ionises base → traps in tubule → excreted

Biliary Excretion & Enterohepatic Circulation

Some drugs and metabolites (often glucuronide conjugates) are excreted in bile into the intestine. Intestinal bacteria can hydrolyse conjugates, releasing free drug which is reabsorbed — enterohepatic circulation. This extends the drug's duration of action (e.g. morphine, digoxin, some oestrogens).

When drug is given repeatedly, it accumulates until a steady state is reached — where rate of administration = rate of elimination.

  • Loading dose is used to achieve therapeutic concentrations rapidly
  • Maintenance dose sustains steady state
  • Accumulation risk increases with prolonged half-life or impaired elimination

Dose-Response Curves

The relationship between drug dose and pharmacological response is described by a dose-response curve. These curves reveal important information about drug potency, efficacy, and selectivity.

Graded Dose-Response Curve EXAM HOT

When the response of a single individual or tissue is measured at increasing doses, a sigmoidal (S-shaped) curve results when plotted on a log-dose scale.

FeatureMeaning
Threshold doseMinimum dose producing a measurable response
EC50 / ED50Dose producing 50% of maximal response; measure of potency (lower EC50 = more potent)
EmaxMaximum response achievable regardless of dose; measure of efficacy

Master Quick Revision — 50 Must-Know Points

  1. Hippocrates (5th century B.C.) — Father of Medicine; separated medicine from religion; four humors theory (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile)
  2. Galen — Father of Polypharmacy; practiced in Rome; galenical drugs named after him
  3. Paracelsus — introduced Iatro Chemistry (medicinal chemistry); used mercury, sulphur; popularised laudanum
  4. Samuel Hahnemann — Father of Homeopathy; principles: 'like cures like' and 'dilution potentiates'
  5. Pharmacodynamics = What the drug does to the body | Pharmacokinetics = What the body does to the drug (ADME)
  6. Drug — from French 'Drogue' (dry herb); any chemical agent except food used to promote/safeguard health
  7. Placebo — pharmacologically inert; cure by suggestion; Latin = 'I shall be pleasing'
  8. Pro-drugs — inactive as administered; converted to active form in body (e.g. enalapril → enalaprilat)
  9. Alkaloids — nitrogenous; names end in -ine; oxygen-containing = solids; oxygen-free = liquids/volatile; insoluble in water
  10. Glycosides — hydrolysis yields sugar + aglycone (active); cardiac glycosides (digitalis, strophanthus, squill) for CHF
  11. Routes of administration: Enteral (via gut) / Parenteral (beyond gut) / Topical (skin/mucosa)
  12. IV route — fastest onset; 100% bioavailability; most dangerous; no retrieval once injected
  13. Oral first-pass effect — portal circulation → liver metabolism before systemic circulation (high FP: morphine, lidocaine, propranolol)
  14. Fluid Mosaic Model — lipid bilayer with floating proteins; membrane is fluid, flexible, impermeable to polar molecules
  15. Passive diffusion — most important mechanism; lipid-soluble > polar molecules; follows Fick's law; concentration gradient driven
  16. Henderson-Hasselbalch equation — weak acids absorbed in acidic environment; weak bases absorbed in alkaline environment
  17. Ion trapping — alkalinise urine to excrete acids; acidify urine to excrete bases — exploit in drug overdose
  18. Biotransformation phases — Phase I (oxidation/reduction/hydrolysis, adds functional groups) + Phase II (conjugation, adds water solubility)
  19. CYP450 enzyme induction — Phenobarbital, Rifampicin, Griseofulvin | Inhibition — Chloramphenicol, Cimetidine, Ketoconazole
  20. Cats — deficient glucuronyl transferase (avoid paracetamol, aspirin) | Dogs — poor acetylators
  21. Furosemide (Loop diuretic) — most potent; inhibits NKCC2 at thick ascending LOH; causes hypokalaemia
  22. Spironolactone — K+-sparing; aldosterone antagonist; used with loop/thiazide to prevent hypokalaemia
  23. Therapeutic Index = LD50/ED50; narrow TI drugs (digoxin, aminoglycosides) require monitoring
  24. Cardiac glycosides — inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase → ↑intracellular Ca2+ → positive inotropy; negative chronotropy; narrow TI
  25. Cardiac glycoside toxicity — treat with K+ correction, atropine (bradycardia), Digibind (antibody fragments)
  26. Vaughan Williams Class I = Na+ blockers (quinidine, lidocaine) | Class II = β-blockers | Class III = K+ blockers (amiodarone) | Class IV = Ca2+ blockers (verapamil)
  27. Heparin — fast, parenteral, activates antithrombin III, monitored with aPTT, antidote = protamine sulphate
  28. Warfarin (coumarin) — oral, Vitamin K antagonist, blocks II/VII/IX/X, monitored with PT/INR, antidote = Vitamin K1
  29. Insulin receptor — tyrosine kinase type receptor; insulin → glucose uptake (GLUT4), glycogen synthesis
  30. Glucocorticoids — nuclear receptors; anti-inflammatory (↓PLA2, ↓COX-2, ↓cytokines); immunosuppressive; catabolic
  31. PGF2α (Dinoprost) — luteolytic + oxytocic; synchronise oestrus in cattle/sheep; treat pyometra; retained placenta
  32. Oxytocin — stimulates uterine contractions (if cervix dilated); promotes milk letdown; short duration of action
  33. Clenbuterol — β2 agonist; bronchodilator AND tocolytic (uterine relaxant); widely used in cattle and horses
  34. Cyclosporin A — calcineurin inhibitor; blocks IL-2; treats KCS in dogs (Optimmune drops), atopic dermatitis (Atopica)
  35. Piperine (black pepper) — bioenhancer; inhibits CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein; increases bioavailability of co-administered drugs
  36. Vitamin A deficiency = Night blindness, xerophthalmia | Vit D deficiency = Rickets/osteomalacia | Vit E deficiency = White muscle disease | Vit K deficiency = Bleeding
  37. Thiamine (B1) deficiency — Polioencephalomalacia (PEM) in ruminants; Chastek paralysis in mink/foxes (raw fish → thiaminase)
  38. Vitamin B12 — requires cobalt; deficiency in sheep on cobalt-poor pasture causes 'pine disease' (ill thrift, wasting)
  39. DMSO — potent penetration enhancer; anti-inflammatory; causes garlic breath; widely used in equine therapy
  40. Rumen antacids — treat grain overload acidosis (MgOH, NaHCO3) | Antibloat (antifoaming) — dimethicone, poloxalene for frothy bloat
  41. Apomorphine — emetic in dogs (dopamine agonist at CRTZ) | Xylazine — emetic in cats (α2 agonist effect on CRTZ)
  42. Loperamide — peripherally acting opioid antidiarrhoeal; does not cross BBB; increases GI transit time
  43. Misoprostol — synthetic PGE1; cytoprotective; reduces NSAID-induced gastric ulcers
  44. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) — omeprazole; irreversibly inhibit H+/K+-ATPase; strongest acid suppressants
  45. Sucralfate — forms ulcer-protective barrier; requires acidic environment; stimulates prostaglandins
  46. Levamisole — dual role: anthelminthic (nicotinic agonist) + immunostimulant (restores T-cell and phagocyte function)
  47. Expectorants — increase mucus secretion for clearance (guaifenesin) | Mucolytics — break disulphide bonds in mucus (bromhexine, acetylcysteine)
  48. Antiarrhythmic adenosine — terminates acute SVT; very short half-life (~10 seconds); given as rapid IV bolus
  49. Clinical trial phases — Phase I: safety/PK in healthy volunteers | Phase II: efficacy in patients | Phase III: large RCT vs standard | Phase IV: post-marketing surveillance
  50. Biopharmaceuticals — large-molecule drugs from biological sources (recombinant proteins, mAbs, gene therapy vectors, cytokines)
  51. Oesophageal groove reflex — in young ruminants; channels milk to abomasum; stimulated by suckling or copper sulphate; exploited for drug delivery
